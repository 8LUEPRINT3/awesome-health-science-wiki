Safe and effective care requires careful planning and coordination. It does not just happen. Facilities and agencies put systems and processes in place to protect clients from harm and to ensure high-quality care. This chapter discusses systems and processes you will need to know as a support worker, including care planning, reporting, and recording.

## The Care-Planning Process in Facilities

Nurses in facilities use a method called the care-planning process (also known as the nursing process) to plan and deliver care to clients. Its purpose is to meet the client’s needs for care and support. The care-planning process in facilities has the following steps:

* 1. Assessment
* 2. Nursing diagnosis
* 3. Planning
* 4. Implementation
* 5. Evaluation

### Assessment

Assessment occurs through the evaluation of information collected about the client through observation, reflection, and communication. In some facilities, the team leader or primary care nurse is responsible for ensuring that the client’s emotional, social, intellectual, and spiritual health has been assessed and documented. However, while it is the role of a regulated professional to perform a formal physical assessment on a client, support workers are responsible for observing and making basic assessments on clients. Support workers must also report and record their findings according to their agency’s policy. The team leader—a nurse, social worker, or caseworker, depending on the facility or agency—gathers as much information as possible from various sources, including other health care providers on the
team. Once the assessment has been completed, team members, including the client and the client’s family, gather to set goals to meet the needs of the client.

Some facilities and agencies have adopted a client-centred approach that involves the client to a greater degree and also other health care providers, including support workers. This collaborative approach occurs at all stages of the care-planning process.

### Nursing and Medical Diagnoses

The nurse uses information gathered from the assessment to make a nursing diagnosis. The support worker’s observations and basic assessments are very important and must be reported so that the nurse has as much information as possible to determine a care plan.

The client may have a specific health issue or may be at risk for developing a health challenge. Some clients may have more than one health challenge and nursing diagnosis. A nursing diagnosis and a medical diagnosis are different. A medical diagnosis is made by a physician or nurse practitioner and is the identification of a disease or condition—for example, cancer, pneumonia, bipolar disorder, stroke, heart attack, acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), or diabetes. Medications, therapies, and surgery are ordered only by physicians or nurse practitioners to treat diseases or other medical conditions.

A nursing diagnosis is a statement describing a health problem that is treated by nursing measures. Nursing diagnoses take into account the whole client and are made only by nurses. Nurses regard all aspects of the client’s health to be important. An example of a nursing diagnosis may be low self-esteem, social isolation, or spiritual distress. Most Canadian nurses use nursing diagnoses from the list established by the North American Nursing Diagnosis Association (NANDA) or from similar lists of diagnoses.

### Planning

Planning involves establishing priorities and goals and developing measures or actions to help the client meet these goals.

#### Establishing Priorities

The client, the nurse, the family (or loved ones, if involved), and the health care team, including support workers, will discuss the client’s needs and then decide on the client’s priorities. They may use Maslow’s hierarchy of needs to set priorities (see [Chapter 23](../B9780323709392000237/B9780323709392000237.xhtml)).

#### Setting Goals

After the client, the family, the case manager, and the health care team all agree on the priorities, they will discuss the goals for the client’s care. In some situations, this discussion may involve only the client and the health care team. Goals for client care should be practical, achievable, and measurable ([BOX 26.1](#b0010)). Every goal should also identify time-specific parameters for evaluating the results ([FIGURE 26.1](#f0010)). If a client does not achieve a goal by the date set by the client and team, the client must be the first person consulted in establishing a more realistic goal. The team should then meet to develop a plan to help the client meet this goal.

#### Determining Interventions

After setting the goals, the team will discuss interventions. An intervention is an action or measure taken by the health care team to help the client meet a goal. For example, if a physician or nurse practitioner orders that Mrs. Jacob walk 100 metres twice a day, the nurse will include this order in the care plan to ensure it is followed by the health care team.

#### Establishing the Care Plan

Across Canada, various recording methods are used to provide you, as a support worker, with the information you will need to care for your client. Some facilities are still using a Kardex or paper system, although most now provide a computer-generated care plan. The care plan is a document that details the care and services the client must receive during each shift. The plan contains the client’s diagnosis, goals, and the interventions required to achieve each goal. The care plan has several important functions:

1. • It lists the care and services the client must receive.
2. • It ensures that the client’s care is consistent, no matter who provides the care. For example, Mr. Sayeed’s care plan details methods for helping him overcome swallowing difficulties. Each care provider uses the same methods.

### Implementation

Implementation means carrying out or performing an action, and the actions listed in the care plan take place at this stage of the process. The nurse in charge of the client’s care assigns or delegates tasks to the members of the health care team. As the support worker, you are only assigned or delegated tasks that are within the legal limits of your role and job description. The nurse will communicate the tasks assigned or delegated to you according to the facility or agency policy. This communication tells you what tasks you need to perform for each client.

The implementation process has four main functions:

1. • Providing the care
2. • Observing the client during the care
3. • Reporting and recording that the care has been completed
4. • Reporting and recording the observations made during the care

Observing the client is an important part of the implementation process. After care has been completed, support workers must report and record their actions and observations, according to employer policy.

### Evaluation

Evaluation means assessing and measuring the progress a client has made toward meeting the goals that a team has agreed on. Goals may be met totally, partly, or not at all. The nurse assesses the reasons a client may have made no progress or only partial progress toward reaching a goal. Your input of your observations will help the nurse change the interventions, complete further assessments, and alter the care plan, as required. Changes to the care plan are made in consultation with the client, family members, and other members of the health care team. Team meetings occur routinely, according to facility or agency policy. These meetings are held to share information, to set and modify goals, and to suggest ideas for modifying a client’s care to meet the client’s needs. As a support worker, you are an important part of the team, as you are the one spending the most time with your client (see [Chapters 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml), [10](../B9780323709392000109/B9780323709392000109.xhtml), and [11](../B9780323709392000110/B9780323709392000110.xhtml)).

## The Care-Planning Process in Community Settings

Case managers coordinate and manage client care. The care-planning process used by case managers in community settings usually involves four steps: assessment, planning, implementation, and evaluation.

### Assessment

The case manager meets with the client and family members to identify the client’s issues and needs. Usually, the meeting takes place in the client’s home. If the client is coming home from the hospital, the case manager uses information from the hospital record or information obtained through a referral sent to the case manager’s agency. The referral information may, in some cases, have been completed by a hospital case manager or the discharge planner.

The family is very important to the assessment process in community care settings. Serious illnesses and disabilities can greatly affect family life (see [Chapter 6](../B9780323709392000067/B9780323709392000067.xhtml)). Family members take on new roles, including the role of caregiver to the client with an illness or disability. The case manager considers the needs, health, and well-being of the entire family. The case manager also considers whether family members need help adjusting to the situation or training to help them
care for the client. Together, the case manager, the client, and family members decide what care and services are needed. For example, they consider nursing and personal care needs and services, such as Meals on Wheels, housekeeping, and transportation, as well as the need for special equipment, such as for oxygen therapy.

The case manager also considers whether the client’s home is a safe environment. For example, the home must be reasonably clean, be free of infestations, and have handwashing facilities and adequate heating and cooling systems. The case manager assesses whether the home needs modifications to accommodate the client’s safety needs. For example, the home may need grab bars installed in the bathroom or a mechanical lifting device in the bedroom. Often, further safety assessments by other specialists are needed. For example, an occupational therapist may assess a home for wheelchair accessibility (see [Chapter 16](../B978032370939200016X/B978032370939200016X.xhtml)). Assessment tools such as the Resident Assessment Instrument (RAI) are available to team members.

### Planning

In community care settings, the planning stage can be lengthy and complicated. First, the case manager, the client, and family members establish priorities, set goals, and determine available resources. Then, the case manager develops a master care plan based on the goals and puts together a health care team.

The case manager and family members consider the resources available for care. The case manager determines how much publicly funded home care the client and family are eligible for. The family may choose to pay for additional care and services from a private agency.

The care plan includes the care and services provided by both family members and outside professionals and agencies. These professionals and agencies often develop their own care plans, but the case manager is in charge of the master plan. For example, the nursing care plan may be one part of the master care plan.

In many jurisdictions, the case manager schedules all outside services and arranges financing for them. If the client needs help from a support worker, the case manager contacts an agency. A supervisor assigns a support worker to provide care or support to the client. The assignment may be communicated to the support worker by phone or on an assignment sheet. In other jurisdictions, the case manager is also part of the home care team.

When clients have multiple needs, several agencies may be involved. For example, Mr. Tremblay is recovering from a stroke. His wife is his primary caregiver. He needs 4 hours of nursing care a week as well as visits from respiratory, occupational, and speech therapists and a physiotherapist. A support worker is also needed to help Mr. Tremblay prepare for bed when his wife is at work. Arrangements are made for Meals on Wheels so that Mrs. Tremblay does not have to prepare every meal.

Some clients in the community choose to coordinate and manage their own care. They may develop their own written care plan, or they may not write anything down. If there are no written directions for you, as the support worker, you will have to ask for detailed instructions from the client. You must ensure that you know exactly what you are allowed to do according to your employer’s policies and what you must do if the client’s expectations are beyond your scope of practice. If you are hired privately, you must decide whether you can or cannot provide the care requested based on your education, since you are directly liable for legal actions.

### Implementation

Agency staff provides care and services on the dates and times arranged by the case manager. If any unforeseen needs arise, the client or a family member calls the case manager. After assessing the situation, the case manager may ask the agency for an unscheduled visit. For example, Mrs. Tremblay feels too ill to care for her husband one morning. She calls her case manager, who contacts the agency for a support worker to care for Mr. Tremblay.

### Evaluation

Evaluation in a home care setting is an ongoing process, so the case manager periodically meets with the client and the family to assess progress. The case manager also meets with and receives reports from
the care providers and service providers, who continually monitor and evaluate their own care plans.

## Your Role In The Care-Planning Process

In any health care setting, as a support worker, you have an important role in the care-planning process. You make observations and relate these observations to the team members. You also provide feedback in response to the rest of the team’s questions about the client and document the care you provide so that others on the team can use this information when reassessing the client’s progress, revising client goals, and modifying the care plan.

### Developing Observation Skills

Support workers generally spend more time with clients than other health care providers do. The support worker, therefore, may be the first to notice a change in a client’s condition and observe the client’s preferences and reactions to interventions. As a support worker, you are expected to make careful and accurate reports of these observations, which will be used in the care-planning process.

[BOX 26.2](#b0015) is a guide for making observations and basic assessments (also known as objective data). You may observe other conditions and situations. Be alert to changes in your client’s condition or behaviour. Focus your observations on the client’s physical, mental, emotional, and social condition. Look for the following:

1. • Changes in physical condition—for example, the client’s skin on both arms is red and blistering
2. • Changes in mental condition—for example, the client is forgetting how to use a toothbrush today in spite of frequent cueing
3. • Changes in emotional states—for example, the client started crying during breakfast
4. • Changes in social condition—for example, the client’s friend does not visit at the usual time
5. • New conditions that you observe—for example, the client was incontinent ×3 for loose, watery stool

#### Objective Data

The information you gather using your senses is called objective data or signs. You need to use your senses of sight, hearing, touch, and smell. You may see the way the client shows discomfort when lying down, sitting, or walking, or you may observe flushed or pale skin and reddened or swollen areas on the client’s body. You may hear abnormal sounds in the client’s chest during respiration. You can feel by touch any changes in your client’s skin temperature. With your sense of smell, you can detect any body, wound, and breath odours and unusual odours from urine and feces.

#### Subjective Data

Subjective data (or symptoms) consist of information reported by a client but not directly observed by others. The following statements are examples of subjective data:

1. • “I hardly slept last night. I lay awake from 1:00 a.m. until the sun came up.”
2. • “With George gone, I just don’t feel like living anymore. I feel so hopeless.”
3. • “The pain is worse when I move. It is a sharp pain that goes up from my ankle to my hip. Thankfully, it comes and goes. I couldn’t stand it if I felt it all the time.”

When you report or record subjective data, do not interpret the client’s comments. Use the client’s exact words.

### Describing Your Observations and Basic Assessments

Your observations are critical to the care-planning process, since nurses and case managers use them for the assessment and evaluation steps. Remember these points when describing your observations:

1. •
   Be precise and accurate.
    Provide details of what you actually see, hear, touch, and smell. Measurements, calculations, and times must be accurate. When describing subjective data, report or record the client’s exact words.
2. •
   Do not interpret or make assumptions.
    In most cases, your observations are sufficient, and you do not need to interpret them. Do not make assumptions. An assumption is a guess, usually based on insufficient evidence. When you make assumptions, you may be making a wrong or hasty conclusion.

## Verbal Reporting

As a support worker, you need to report and record your actions and observations. A verbal report is the spoken account of care provided and observations made. Employers use different methods for verbal reporting, and all have policies about how often to report and what to report, so you need to know those policies. In certain circumstances, listed in [BOX 26.4](#b0025), you must always contact your supervisor. Verbal reports allow for quick, up-to-date information sharing. However, they lack a permanent record of what is reported.

Remember that information about a client is confidential. Be careful when communicating client information to other members of the health care team. Choose a quiet area where you will not be overheard by others. Do not discuss a client in their room or in a common area. Keep all your conversations about clients professional. When making reports by phone to your supervisor from your own home, make sure no one in your home can hear you.

### Verbal Reporting in a Facility

In a facility, support workers report actions and observations to the charge nurse or to the supervisor. Reports must be prompt, thorough, and accurate.

1. • Always include the client’s name, the room and bed number, and the time you made the observation or gave the care.
2. • Report only what you observed or performed.
3. • Prioritize items—start your report with the most important points.
4. • Give reports as often as the client’s condition requires or as often as requested by the nurse.
5. • Immediately report any changes in the client’s previously reported condition.

At the end of a shift, the charge nurse gives a report (called the end-of-shift report) to the incoming charge nurse. This report includes information about each
client’s condition, the care given, and the care that must be given on the next shift. Some facilities expect all team members to hear the end-of-shift report as they come on duty.

## Client Records or Charts

A chart (also known as a record) is a permanent and legal document required to record a client’s condition, signs and symptoms of any illness, the care and treatment given to the client, and the client’s responses to care. This record documents the care provided from admission to discharge or death and is filed and kept (in Ontario, for a minimum of 5 years). It is used as a reference for future health care treatment, and it could be subpoenaed (ordered) for a court case involving malpractice.

In this age of technology, most facilities and agencies have implemented computerized reporting systems, but some employers are still using paper-based reports for some forms. This chapter will show you some of the forms that are used in the authors’ area, but your facilities and agencies will have their own. Your employer will, as part of your orientation, ensure that you know what documentation it requires.

1. •
   Communication.
    Health care teams rely on reports to communicate information about their clients ([FIGURE 26.2](#f0015)). All team members must be informed about the client’s condition and care during each shift. The report is a method to ensure that all members of the team are aware of the care the client has received. Recording is an accurate way to communicate information about the client. The care plan is one part of the report.
2. •
   Planning client care.
    The client record documents how the client tolerated a procedure or type
   of care. This information helps the health care team plan future interventions that would be best tolerated by the client.

BOX 26.4 When to Contact Your Supervisor

Contact your supervisor in any of the following cases:

1. • There is an emergency, such as finding your client lying unconscious on the floor.
2. • You observe a change in the client’s condition or normal functioning.
3. • The client becomes ill; for example, the client vomits, has diarrhea, or develops a fever.
4. • The client is in distress, either physical or emotional.
5. • You believe the client’s safety is at risk.
6. • You believe your safety is at risk or may become at risk.
7. • A problem arises involving medications.
8. • The client complains about their condition or care.
9. • The client asks you a question about their diagnosis, condition, or treatment plans.
10. • The client or family member asks you to do something that contradicts the care plan.
11. • You have a conflict with a client or a family member.
12. • A question or problem arises, and you need help to sort it out.

1. •
   Currency.
    Care plans change as the client’s needs, preferences, and condition change. Reports enable team members to keep the client’s information up to date. Other areas of the report are also kept up to date—for example, with the latest reports of laboratory tests and doctors’ orders.
2. •
   Accountability.
    Printed charts are signed and dated by members of the health care team. Computerized charts offer automatic tracking. Ensure that you keep track of all printed charts and emails since all team members are accountable for their own words and actions.
   In a legal case, you may need to testify about what you wrote on the chart. Computer reports are traceable, as you would have used your log-in to update your reports, and are legally binding.

BOX 26.5 Guidelines for Making Telephone Reports to Your Supervisor

1. • Keep agency phone numbers and extension numbers handy.
2. • Make sure you have a pen and paper handy.
3. • Identify yourself, the date, and the time of your call.
4. • Give a concise, accurate, and descriptive report. For example, say what you observed (“Client has an abrasion 5 cm by 3 cm on his buttocks”), when you made the observation (“I saw it when bathing the client”), and what the client said (“I fell down the stairs last night”).
5. • Use the client’s exact words.
6. • Speak clearly and slowly. Pause between sentences.
7. • Write down the instructions from your supervisor.
8. • Repeat the instructions back to your supervisor.
9. • Write the instructions on the appropriate document in the client’s chart.
10. • Remember that the client’s information is confidential. It is shared only with the health care team members involved in the client’s care. Follow the rules outlined in the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA), available online. Legislation may vary between provinces and territories, so you must develop a working knowledge of the legislation that applies in your area.

#### Electronic Documentation

Both written reports and electronic reports have advantages as well as disadvantages. Written reports provide a permanent record, but they are time-consuming and can be difficult to read if the handwriting is not clear. Electronic reports are fast and efficient, but confidentiality can be more difficult to maintain because of the risk for data being accessed by unauthorized people. Use the guidelines in [BOX 26.6](#b0035) and follow your employer’s policies.

Hospitals, long-term care facilities, and community agencies are increasingly using electronic charting. Some facilities have computers outside each client’s room, and support workers are required to enter the care given, the amount of time it took to provide the care, and how much the client was able to help with the care. In some provinces, these data help determine how much funding facilities will receive. Other hospitals and long-term care facilities have computers at the nursing stations, where support workers enter data. Some community agencies are now using handheld digital devices to ensure that the recorded information regarding their clients is current.

#### Recording Time

The 24-hour clock is used to document care. It uses a four-digit number for time ([FIGURE 26.8](#f0045)). The first two digits are for the hour: 0100 = 1:00 a.m.; 1300 = 1:00 p.m. The last two digits are for minutes: 0110 = 1:10 a.m. The abbreviations a.m. and p.m. are not used, but sometimes the abbreviation h or hr is used. Follow your employer’s policies. As [BOX 26.8](#b0045) (on page 586) shows, morning hours are the same in the 24-hour clock as they are in the conventional clock. For p.m. times, add 12 to the first two digits (the hours) of clock time. For example, if it is 2:00 p.m., add 12 to 2 to make 1400. For 8:35 p.m., add 12 to 8 to make 2035. The 24-hour clock helps make communication more accurate. Since health care team members do not have to write “a.m.” or “p.m.,” the risk of confusion is reduced.

#### Terminology and Abbreviations

In health care, medical terminology and abbreviations are used to communicate effectively and clearly (see [Chapter 25](../B9780323709392000250/B9780323709392000250.xhtml)). If a member of your health care team uses a word that you do not understand, ask the person what the word means. It is a good idea to keep a medical dictionary at hand so you can look up the meanings of unfamiliar words. All members of the health care team must use only terms and abbreviations approved by employer policy to ensure correct interpretations by everyone on the health care team.

#### Methods of Charting

Agencies and facilities use a variety of methods for charting. Some examples include progress (narrative) notes, SOAP charting, PIE charting, and focus (DAR) charting ([BOX 26.9](#b0050)).

Narrative charting records information about the client and client care in chronological order. The content resembles a log of the client’s day.

SOAP charting uses four essential components in recording. The components are:

* S—subjective data
* O—objective data
* A—analysis, or assessment, of the data
* P—plan of care

PIE charting uses three essential components in recording. The components are:

* P—problem
* I—intervention
* E—evaluation

### Recording in the Community

Agencies and case managers keep the bulk of client charts in their organizations; however, some parts of a client’s chart are usually, but not always, kept in the client’s home, often in a binder. The forms in the binder will vary according to agency policy and the client’s condition and needs. Among other documents, the binder usually contains the care plan, progress notes, ADL checklists, flow sheets, and task sheets. Some agencies have smartphones or tablets that the support worker would use to document client care and information.

Agency policies differ—some do not allow support workers to enter anything on the documents in the client record, whereas others expect support workers to accurately record tasks and observations in the appropriate manner. Most agencies have forms (or entry sections in electronic documentation systems) called client care task sheets that the support worker carries with them to every assignment (see [FIGURE 26.7](#f0040), on page 583). You would start a new task sheet for each client. As you complete tasks, you check off the relevant areas of the form.

Most task sheets contain space to record special circumstances or observations. You may be expected to note whether the client performed activities independently, dependently, or with some assistance. As mentioned, any changes you observe in a client’s normal functioning or condition should be reported by phone to your supervisor. Record on the task sheet any verbal reports that you make as well as phone instructions received from your supervisor.

When finished with completing task sheets, you hand them in, place them in the correct location, or download the gathered information monthly or weekly, depending on agency policy, along with forms that track work expenses such as mileage for your vehicle if you use one for your work. Your supervisor may use your task sheets to prepare a report on each client. For this report, you may be asked for additional information about some of your clients.

### Confidentiality

As a support worker, you are ethically and legally bound to keep client information confidential, including any information about the client that you record in the client chart, whether by hand or on an electronic device. All employers have strict guidelines about the confidentiality of records and client information. You must be particularly careful to observe guidelines on accessing, reporting, and transporting information, depending on the regulations of your province or territory. The federal government has very strict guidelines to protect an individual’s rights to privacy.

Only health care team members who are directly involved in the client’s care have access to confidential information. Those not directly involved usually are not allowed access to the client’s records. Housekeeping staff, kitchen staff, and office clerks, for example, do not need to see the records or hear any confidential details about a client. In a home care setting, only
certain family members have access to these details. Your supervisor will tell you who is allowed to look at the record.

### Impact of Electronic Communication on Health Care and Recording

Electronic communication has opened the door to many changes in the way health care is researched, provided, and documented. For example, many clients research their health needs and get health questions answered by simply searching on the Internet. However, Internet search engines do not screen the available information for accuracy and reliability. To better inform the public, there are government websites and telephone services that can reliably answer a client (or family member’s) questions regarding health care.

In many remote areas of the country, health care may be difficult to access, a situation that is especially problematic if the client’s condition requires the services of a medical or nursing specialist. To assist clients in such areas, health care workers may send health data about the client electronically to a nurse practitioner, doctor, or health team many kilometres away. Electronic health records are easy to read and respond to and are helping to bridge this distance.

Food and water are necessary for life and health. The amount and quality of foods and fluids in the diet are important, as they affect a person’s current as well as future health and well-being. For all age groups, a poor diet will affect physical and mental growth and function, increase the risk for disease, and slow healing. Poor physical and mental functioning increases the risk for accidents and injuries.

Food and drink contribute to social and emotional health also. Eating and drinking are part of social activity with family and friends ([FIGURE 27.1](#f0010)).

As a support worker, you will serve food and fluids to clients and assist them with eating. In home care, you may also prepare meals for clients. This chapter introduces you to the basics of nutrition.

## Basic Nutrition

Nutrition refers to the processes involved in the ingestion, digestion, absorption, and use of foods and fluids by the body. Ingestion is the process of taking food and fluids into the body. Digestion is the process of physically and chemically breaking down nutrients so that they can be absorbed for use by the cells. Absorption is the process by which substances pass through the intestinal wall into the blood. Review the description of the digestive system in [Chapter 32](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml).

Good nutrition is needed for growth, healing, and the maintenance of body functions. Selected foods must provide a well-balanced diet and the correct amount of calories to sustain a healthy weight range. A diet high in fat and empty calories (lacking in nutrients) can cause unwanted health problems. Those who follow Canada’s Food Guide (2019) will obtain all of the nutrients they need for a balanced diet. Health care providers can refer to Health Canada’s document entitled Canada’s Dietary Guidelines (<https://food-guide.canada.ca/en/guidelines/>), which accompanies the 2019 version of Canada’s Food Guide. These guidelines explain how health care workers and policymakers can promote healthy eating and overall nutritional well-being for all Canadians.

### Nutrients

Foods and fluids contain nutrients—substances that are ingested, digested, absorbed, and used by the body. Nutrients are grouped into proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, minerals, and water.

Proteins, fats, and carbohydrates give the body fuel for energy. The amount of energy provided by a nutrient is measured in calories. A calorie is a unit of measure of the energy produced as the body burns food. For example:

1. • 1 g of carbohydrate supplies the body with 4 calories
2. • 1 g of protein supplies the body with 4 calories
3. • 1 g of fat supplies the body with 9 calories

#### Protein

Protein is needed for tissue growth and repair. Protein sources include meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk and milk products, cereals, beans, peas, and nuts. Animal products are the best sources of protein, so people who do not eat animal products must consume sufficient protein from plant sources, which are best eaten in combinations—for example, beans and rice. Protein deficiency can result in severe malnutrition. Children and older people who do not eat properly are at the greatest risk.

#### Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates provide energy for the body and fibre for bowel elimination and to maintain bowel regularity. Most carbohydrates come from plants. There are three main kinds of carbohydrates:

1. •
   Simple sugars
    are found in table sugar, fruits, and fruit juices.
2. •
   Starches
    are found in bread, pasta, rice, and potatoes.
3. •
   Fibre
    is found in bran, nuts, seeds, and raw fruits with skins. Fibre cannot be digested and passes through the intestines undigested.

During digestion, most carbohydrates (except fibre) are broken down into sugars, which are then absorbed into the bloodstream.

#### Fats

Fats provide energy, help the body to use certain vitamins, and add flavour to food. Some fat is necessary in the diet. Dietary fat that is not needed by the body (as with excess calories from all sources) is stored as body fat. There are three main types of dietary fat:

1. •
   Saturated fat
    is found in animal and dairy products (e.g., meat, butter, milk, and cheeses).
2. •
   Trans fat
    is found in margarine, shortening, store-bought cookies, cakes, pies, doughnuts, and fried foods, and is the least healthy fat. Trans fat is created when liquid oil is chemically altered to form a more solid substance. It is used to increase the flavour and shelf life of foods.
3. •
   Unsaturated fat
    is found in fish and many vegetable oils (e.g., canola oil and olive oil). Unsaturated fat is healthier than saturated fat and trans fat.

#### Vitamins

Vitamins are needed daily for normal function and growth, but they do not provide calories. Each vitamin is needed for specific body functions ([TABLE 27.1](#t0010))—for example, vitamin A is necessary for vision. Vitamins are an essential part of a healthy diet, and the lack of a specific vitamin may result in illness. All necessary vitamins can be obtained by following Canada’s Dietary Guidelines. Vitamins A, D, E, and K are fat-soluble vitamins; excess doses of these vitamins are stored in the body and can cause complications. Vitamins C and B complex are called water-soluble vitamins; excess doses of these vitamins are excreted in urine. Older persons are at risk for developing vitamin deficiencies because the aging process affects the body’s ability to absorb certain vitamins.

#### Minerals

Minerals are chemical substances found in both plant and animal foods. Each mineral is needed for specific body functions—for example, calcium and phosphorus are used to form strong bones and teeth. [TABLE 27.2](#t0015) lists the major functions and sources of minerals. All necessary minerals can be obtained in Canada’s Dietary Guidelines.

#### Water

Water is the
most important nutrient
 necessary for life. The body needs water for maintaining cell function, regulating body temperature, delivering nutrients, removing waste, and performing other body processes. Water enters the body through fluids and foods and is lost through urine and feces, through the skin as perspiration, and through the lungs with expiration. There must be a balance between the amount of fluid taken in and the amount lost. Death can
result from inadequate water intake or from excessive fluid loss.

Diseases such as heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, diabetes, osteoporosis, and certain cancers are related to diet and the kinds of foods eaten. Therefore, the goal and benefits of healthy eating are better overall health, lower risk of disease, and a healthy body weight. A person who eats well will have more energy, feel and look better, and have stronger muscles and bones.

### Vegetables and Fruits

Vegetables and fruits (including juices) provide carbohydrates, vitamins C and A, iron, and magnesium
and are naturally low in fat. It is good to eat at least one dark green vegetable (such as broccoli, romaine lettuce, or spinach) and one orange vegetable (such as carrots, sweet potatoes, or winter squash) each day. Try to prepare vegetables and fruits with little or no added fat, sugar, or salt. Many prepackaged or canned foods are high in sodium (salt). Fresh vegetables and fruits are more beneficial than juices. Frozen or canned fruit should be unsweetened, as sweetened and syrupy products are high in sugar and calories. Juice should be diluted with water when being served to children or if the client is on a calorie-restricted diet.

Vegetables provide the best value when steamed, baked, or stir fried, and not deep fried, which can make vegetable preparations high in fat. For example, French fries are very high in fat compared with baked or boiled potatoes. Butter, oil, mayonnaise, salad dressing, sour cream, and sauces—toppings that are high in fat—are often added to vegetables.
Small amounts of low-fat toppings help keep vegetable preparations low in fat.

BOX 27.1 Fortified and Enriched Foods

Food processing removes valuable nutrients from food. To replace losses or to enhance nutrient content, foods are enriched or fortified. In enriched foods—for example, wheat flour, cereal, and pasta—nutrients are replaced to their original level or higher. In fortified foods—for example, milk fortified with vitamin D—nutrients are added. Labels on products usually indicate whether a food has been fortified or enriched.

### Whole Grain Foods

Whole grain foods contain important nutrients such as fibre, vitamins, and minerals. Whole grain foods are a healthier choice than refined grains because whole grain foods include all parts of the grain and thus contain more fibre than refined or processed grains. Eating foods high in fibre can reduce the risk of stroke, colon cancer, heart disease, and type 2 diabetes.[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur274)

Read the ingredient list and choose foods that list the words “whole grain” followed by the name of the specific grain as one of the first ingredients, such as “whole grain oats” or “whole grain wheat.” Whole wheat foods are not whole grain but can still be a healthy choice because they do contain fibre.[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur274)

When choosing whole wheat foods, ensure that they are enriched or fortified with the nutrients that were lost during the processing process. Enriched foods are recommended because of their higher content of iron and B vitamins ([BOX 27.1](#b0010)).

### Nutritional Challenges That Canadians Face

There are many challenges that Canadians face (especially in northern Canada or in large, heavily populated urban areas) when it comes to being able to obtain, store, or eat foods that are nutritionally sound. These challenges must be addressed if all Canadians are to have the choice of eating healthy foods. A few of these challenges are listed below:

1. •
   Limited income.
    Fresh foods are usually more expensive than processed or fast foods. Many Canadians who struggle to pay rent may not be able to afford to store, refrigerate, or cook food. Some Canadians live in overcrowded or substandard housing where rodents and insects contaminate their foods, and many people are unable to afford food for their families. While many communities have food banks that can assist low-income people, there are many communities that do not. Fortunately, many breakfast school programs now provide a nutritious breakfast to children who would not be able to eat one otherwise. Unfortunately, not all schools have these programs.
2. •
   Geographic isolation.
    Many communities throughout Canada are geographically remote. This means that fresh foods (especially fruits and vegetables) must be shipped over long distances to stores and may quickly spoil or are already past their expiration date for peak nutrition. Some fresh foods are simply not available. The cost of shipping increases the cost of food, often meaning people cannot afford to purchase it.
3. •
   Inclement weather
   . Foods that must be flown in or shipped by truck to remote areas of Canada may not reach their destination during inclement weather conditions. In areas with a very short growing season, foods cannot be grown locally and must be imported.
4. •
   Lack of availability of local foods. For people who rely on hunting or fishing to feed their families (especially many Indigenous people), poor conservation efforts to protect the air, water, and soil from contamination in the past have led to food shortages in some parts of the country. Chemical dumping in rivers and lakes, often illegal, affects fish and wildlife, which now often contain toxic amounts of pollutants. These pollutants can cause birth defects and health challenges among the people who eat the foods containing them. In addition, unrestricted logging, mining, and poor conservation efforts have decreased the availability of open land in some parts of Canada, decreasing the number of animals to hunt.
5. •
   Lack of education on nutrition.
    In spite of the efforts of federal, provincial, and territorial governments and local health units to educate children and adults about the benefits of healthy eating, many people still do not know how to make healthy food choices for themselves or their families. Some people choose foods that lack nutritional benefits because they were raised to eat those foods or because they do not know how to prepare healthy foods that are easy to make.
6. •
   Mental illness or addictions.
    Some people who live with mental illness or addiction may be unable to make proper choices about how to eat or feed their families. This can lead to health problems that last for generations.

## Nutrition Throughout the Life Cycle

Nutritional requirements differ throughout the life cycle of a person.

### Infancy and Childhood

Infancy is a period of rapid growth and development. Babies can be either breastfed or bottle-fed. Breast milk provides the best source of nutrients and antibodies during the baby’s first 6 months. Formula milk can provide adequate nourishment but lacks antibodies. The pediatrician or public health nurse usually provides guidance to parents on feeding times for infants.

At 4 to 6 months, iron-fortified cereals are introduced, followed by puréed foods. Most babies are ready for “finger foods” and chopped foods at 10 months to 1 year. After the first year, the growth rate slows down.

Children usually have strong likes and dislikes for foods, which can make meal planning a challenge. In North America, there has been a dangerous rise in childhood obesity, resulting in part from inactivity, eating nutrient-poor processed foods, and choosing foods that are high in saturated fats and sugars, rather than choosing fruits and vegetables. Children need to be taught how to make proper food choices. Canada’s Dietary Guidelines emphasize the benefits of teaching children how to prepare healthy foods to help foster lifelong healthy eating choices. Regular meals and physical activity are important for their growth and well-being.
Children under 4 years of age should not be given a low-fat diet, as fat is needed for brain development and energy.

As a support worker, when you care for infants and children, your supervisor will provide instructions about their dietary requirements. Follow the care plan for these clients.

### Adolescence

In both boys and girls, the biggest growth spurt after infancy occurs during puberty, and increased nutrients are needed for this rapid growth. Many adolescents, however, have unhealthy eating habits—for example, they may skip meals, consume only fast foods and soft drinks, go on diets, and drink alcohol. Eating disorders (see [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml)) are becoming increasingly common in this age group, although they can occur at any age. Poor eating habits can lead to iron deficiency and poor physical or mental health, or both.

### Young and Middle Adulthood

Nutritional requirements in young and middle adulthood depend on age, gender, body size, and activity levels. Unless extremely active, most adults have lower energy needs than adolescents, and energy needs continue to decline into the 40s and 50s. If calorie intake exceeds energy needs, adults gain weight. A healthy diet containing essential nutrients is therefore extremely important for maintaining a healthy weight and to enhance personal well-being.

#### Pregnancy

Pregnant individuals and their developing fetuses require nutrient-rich food, about 500 additional calories per day.

Pregnant individuals are advised not to smoke, drink alcohol, or take drugs, except for those prescribed by a health care provider. Pregnant individuals who do not usually consume meat and dairy products need to discuss their diet with their physician to ensure that they consume adequate levels and types of protein.

Health Canada suggests that pregnant and breastfeeding individuals increase folic acid, iron, and calcium intake. Low folic acid intake before and during pregnancy increases the risk for spinal cord and brain abnormalities in infants.

### Late Adulthood

Older persons have wide variations in their health and nutritional status, as it is affected by emotional, social, and physical factors.

Many older people are used to cooking for and eating with a family group. If they have lost a spouse or are not used to living alone, preparing a meal for just themselves may hold no interest for them. As well, some older people do not drive and may not be able to carry heavy grocery bags on public transit. They may not have family or friends nearby who can help them with shopping and meal preparation. Those with low income may avoid buying high-protein foods such as meat and cheese, which are usually expensive. Older persons living in long-term care facilities may not like the food that is served.

Physical changes related to aging, such as the following, can affect the nutrition of older persons both in the community and in a facility:

1. • Loss of teeth or poor-fitting dentures can affect the ability to chew and the desire to eat.
2. • Reduction of the number of taste buds may decrease the enjoyment of food.
3. • Decreased strength in and effectiveness of chewing and swallowing muscles may increase the risk of choking while eating.
4. • Vision, hearing, and perceptual challenges may decrease meal enjoyment.
5. • Increased fatigue upon exertion may cause a reduced desire to continue eating.
6. • Impaired ability to feed oneself may decrease meal enjoyment.
7. • Decreased muscle strength and range of motion (ROM) may affect a person’s ability to eat without assistance.
8. • Decreased saliva secretion may cause dysphagia—difficulty (dys) swallowing (phagia) (see [Chapters 32](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml) and [43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml)).
9. • Decreased peristalsis (common among older adults) can result in slower emptying of the stomach and colon, flatulence (gas), and constipation.
10. • Decreased secretion of digestive juices makes fried and fatty foods hard to digest and may cause indigestion.
11. • Nutrients are not absorbed as easily.
12. • Medications may have adverse effects such as nausea, constipation, and loss of appetite.
13. • People who live with dementia are at increased risk for malnutrition and dehydration (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)).

Older persons require certain foods to maintain their physical health. Because energy levels are usually lower in older people, they require fewer calories to sustain body weight, but their nutritional requirements remain high. High-protein foods are needed for tissue growth and repair. Foods high in calcium help keep bones strong; high-fibre foods such as raw vegetables help avoid constipation, but they can be hard to chew and can cause indigestion. Foods providing soft bulk, such as cooked fruits and vegetables, are often preferred for older clients with constipation or chewing problems. Drinking more fluids may also aid digestion, kidney function, chewing, and swallowing.

Good oral hygiene and well-fitted dentures can help prevent irritation of gums and mouth sores and improve the ability to eat and taste.

## Factors That Affect Eating and Nutrition

Nutrition and eating habits are affected by numerous factors; some of them begin during infancy and continue throughout life, whereas some others develop later.

1. •
   Personal choice.
    Likes and dislikes of certain foods are a matter of personal preferences that begin in childhood. They are influenced by the way foods taste, smell, and look and the way they are prepared.
2. •
   Allergies.
    An allergy is a sensitivity to a substance that causes the body to react with signs and symptoms. Common allergic reactions are swelling of the lips, throat, tongue, or face; skin rash; coughing or difficulty breathing; abdominal cramps; nausea; or diarrhea. In severe cases, anaphylactic shock—a life-threatening sensitivity to a substance that can be fatal—can occur. For some people, food allergies are just an annoyance, but for some others, avoiding certain foods is a matter of life and death. Nuts and shellfish cause the most severe reactions.
3. •
   Food intolerance.
    A food intolerance is a noticeable reaction to food, such as indigestion and diarrhea, but it does not involve the immune system and is not as serious as a food allergy. For example, lactose intolerance occurs in people who lack the enzyme lactase, which is needed to break down the sugar (lactose) in milk. Therefore, people who are lactose intolerant cannot digest milk.
4. •
   Culture.
    Culture influences dietary practices, food choices, and food preparation (see the Respecting Diversity: Overcoming Cultural Barriers box). The way food is cooked—frying, baking, smoking, or roasting—or not cooked depends on cultural practices. The use of sauces and spices is also related to culture. Your employer will instruct you in food preparation for your client who may have special cultural dietary practices.

1. •

## Caffeine Intake

Health Canada has been conducting research into caffeine consumption levels that can be recommended for various age groups. Its research has shown that consumption of 400 mg/day is not associated with any adverse effects in adults. For the majority of the adult population, small amounts of caffeine can help increase alertness and concentration. For some people, small or larger amounts of caffeine can cause insomnia, headaches, irritability, and nervousness.

For individuals who are pregnant, planning to become pregnant, or breastfeeding, the recommended
intake is no more than 300 mg/day. If you asked your friends and family how much caffeine they consume in a day, they would probably count only the number of cups of coffee or tea they have.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur275) For example, they might say they generally have three cups of coffee, but they would probably not specify if each cup is 240 mL (135 mg of caffeine), 480 mL, or 600 mL (595 mg of caffeine). They also might not add the caffeine from colas, chocolates, energy drinks, and over-the-counter medications.

Just one 600-mL cup of coffee contains more caffeine than the daily limit recommended for adults. How coffee is made also determines how much caffeine it contains. An average-sized 240-mL cup of instant coffee can contain as much as 106 mg of caffeine, whereas the same volume of roasted, ground, filter-dripped coffee contains 179 mg of caffeine. Even “decaffeinated” coffee contains caffeine. For example, a 240-mL cup of roasted, ground, filter-dripped decaffeinated coffee has 3 mg of caffeine, and the same volume of instant decaffeinated coffee contains 5 mg of caffeine.[6](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur276)

We know that caffeine has been identified in tea, coffee, chocolate, and some energy drinks. Certain herbs such as guarana and yerba mate that are added to energy drinks and bars also contain caffeine. Currently, these products are not required to identify the amount of caffeine added.

Recommended caffeine amounts for children are even less:

1. • 4–6 years: 45 mg/day
2. • 7–9 years: 62.5 mg/day
3. • 10–12 years: 85 mg/day

As a support worker, be aware of the amount of caffeine you are consuming and the amount your client is consuming, as too much caffeine can cause significant adverse effects.

## Meal Planning and Preparation

Your role in meal planning and preparation depends on the care plan and on your client’s needs. Most home care agencies expect clients’ families to provide groceries and main meals. The case manager arranges for Meals on Wheels, if this service is available in your province or territory and if the family and client agree to use it. Meals on Wheels provides clients with their main meal of the day. You may need to prepare a light meal for a client—for example, toast for breakfast or a sandwich for lunch. Sometimes, you may have to prepare several meals and freeze them for future use. Occasionally, you may be required to plan menus and shop for groceries.

When preparing meals for clients, consider their dietary requirements, food preferences, eating limitations, and eating habits.

1. •
   Dietary requirements.
    The care plan includes special diets, mealtime instructions, dietary practices, and food allergies and food intolerances. If there is no information in the care plan, ask your supervisor for guidance. A good cookbook is a helpful guide for planning and preparing meals.
2. •
   Food preferences.
    Many clients have strong food preferences and definite ideas about preparing meals. Follow the client’s wishes. Never give clients food that they are not allowed to eat. If you have concerns about a client’s diet, speak with your supervisor.
3. •
   Eating and swallowing challenges.
    Follow the client’s care plan to ensure that you consider the client’s eating and swallowing limitations. Some clients may have difficulty holding regular cutlery and must have adaptive placements, plates, or cutlery. Some clients have swallowing difficulties that must be taken into consideration when their food is prepared (see page 610). Clients who have difficulty seeing food on the plate may need your assistance to guide them (see pages 610–612).
4. •
   Eating habits.
    Clients may choose to have the main meal in the evening or at noon. Some clients eat several meals of the same size but never snack. Others snack between meals. Some clients eat the same things for breakfast and lunch every day.

### Shopping for Groceries

You may be expected to shop for a client’s food. Use shopping lists to remember needed items. If your client is on a special diet, read food labels carefully. The labels will tell you if the product is “diet,” “low fat,” and so on. Keep the list in one place, and encourage the client to add to it throughout the week. Add
personal care items as needed. On shopping day, check and finalize the list.

#### Checking Expiry Dates

By law, expiry or “best before” dates must appear on products that have a limited shelf life. If stored properly, the product can be safely used before the date listed. There are three commonly used dates:

1. •
   Sell by date
    is the last recommended date of sale. Most products will keep for at least 3 days beyond this date.
2. •
   Best before date
    is the last date that the manufacturer will guarantee freshness.
3. •
   Expiry date
    is the last date that the product can be safely consumed.

Some products—for example, packaged meats and fish—contain only the date on which they were packaged. Select the products that were packaged most recently.

#### Handling Clients’ Money

If you need to make food purchases for the client from a grocery store or convenience store, clients will usually provide cash. Always handle someone else’s money carefully. You must be honest, efficient, and organized. Keep a separate wallet or purse for the client’s money, change, and receipts. Use the receipts to total the money spent, and return the right amount of change to the client. Most employers have strict policies about handling clients’ money. Be sure to follow these policies.

### Following Recipes

Use a recipe to prepare meals. If necessary, consult a basic cookbook that has key terms. You may have to substitute ingredients if your client does not have an ingredient required for the recipe. For example, canola oil and margarine can usually be substituted for olive oil. Never substitute an ingredient without getting the client’s permission and checking the care plan. Do not use a substitute ingredient if the client is on a special diet or has a food allergy or intolerance. Consult your supervisor for guidance.

### Food Safety

Food safety is an important consideration for all people because foodborne illnesses can cause serious illness or death. A foodborne illness is an illness caused by improperly cooked or stored food. Diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting are common signs and symptoms of a foodborne illness. Some people are at particularly high risk for severe symptoms—for example, infants, children, older persons, people with chronic illnesses, and people with weakened immune systems.

Pathogens are disease-causing microbes (see [Chapter 18](../B9780323709392000183/B9780323709392000183.xhtml)), which are commonly called germs. When pathogens are present in or on food, the food is said to be contaminated. Many foods naturally have pathogens in them in their raw state—for example, meat, fish, poultry, and eggs. When food is cooked properly, however, most pathogens are killed. However, raw food may spread pathogens to other, ready-to-eat foods—referred to as cross-contamination. For example, when fluids from raw chicken meat drip onto vegetables in the refrigerator, the vegetables become contaminated. If they are eaten before they are properly washed, they could cause illness.

It is usually hard to tell by sight, smell, or taste if something is contaminated. Safe food-handling
practices and effective cooking can prevent cross-contamination and foodborne illnesses. Most pathogens thrive at room temperature, but they die at temperatures below 4°C (40°F) and above 60°C (140°F). Therefore, it is important to cook foods well and preserve them in the refrigerator. Never leave foods sitting out at room temperature for more than a few minutes.

If you prepare and serve meals to clients, you must know safe food-handling practices. Food safety training is a very educational course for all support workers to take and is available in most cities and towns. Observe safety practices when grocery shopping and when storing, cooking, reheating, and serving food ([BOX 27.2](#b0015)).

## Special Diets

Physicians may order special diets for clients because of a nutritional deficiency or a disease, for the purpose of eliminating or decreasing certain substances in the diet, or for weight control (see [TABLE 27.4](#t0025) on page 608). Special diets are common before and after surgery and for people with diabetes or an intolerance to gluten (page 610). People with diseases of the heart, kidneys, gallbladder, liver, stomach, or intestines may receive special diets. Allergies, food intolerances, obesity, and other disorders also require special diets.

When a special diet is ordered, a nurse and dietitian work together to create a plan for meeting the client’s nutritional needs. The plan takes into consideration the client’s preferences, culture, religion, and food allergies and intolerances, as well as any eating problems such as dysphagia (see page 610).

In facilities, the terms regular diet, general diet, and house diet mean that there are no dietary limits or restrictions. Three common special diets are the sodium-controlled diet, diabetes meal planning, and the gluten-free diet.

### The Sodium-Controlled Diet

The average amount of sodium found in the daily diet of a person is 3 000 to 5 000 mg, twice the daily amount required by the body. Healthy people excrete the excess sodium in the urine. Heart and kidney diseases, as well as some drugs and some complications of pregnancy, cause the body to retain the extra sodium.

Sodium causes the body to retain water. If there is too much sodium, tissues can swell with water, and excess fluid can build in the blood vessels, making the heart work harder. The extra workload for the heart can cause serious complications and even death. Restricting sodium in the diet helps the body retain less water, and less water in the tissues and blood vessels reduces the amount of work for the heart.

The physician may order sodium control (restriction) for the client. Many low-salt or salt-free foods are available on the market; use food labels to determine salt content.

1. •
   2000- to 3000-mg sodium diet
   —called the low-salt diet or no-added-salt diet. Sodium restriction is mild, but all high-sodium foods are omitted. A minimum amount of salt is used for cooking, and salt is not added to foods at the table.
2. •
   1000-mg sodium diet
   —sodium restriction is moderate. Food is cooked without salt, and foods high in sodium are omitted. Canned vegetables that are high in sodium due to canning are restricted in amount. Salt-free products such as salt-free bread are used. Diet planning is necessary.
3. •
   500-mg sodium diet
   —sodium restriction is severe. Restrictions for the mild and moderate sodium diets are followed. In addition, vegetables high in sodium are omitted. Other restrictions are as follows: milk—1 cup per day; egg—1 per day; and meat—120 g (4 oz) per day. Diet planning is essential.

### Diabetic Diet Meal Planning

Diabetic diet meal planning is done for clients with diabetes. Type 1 diabetes is caused by the lack of insulin. Type 2 diabetes is caused by insulin resistance (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)). In healthy people, the pancreas produces and secretes insulin, which enables the body to use the sugar consumed. In people who lack insulin, sugar builds up in the bloodstream rather than being used by cells for energy. Diabetes is usually treated with insulin or medication, diet, and exercise.

The dietitian and the client develop a meal plan together. Consistency in following it is key. The plan involves the following:

1. •
   The client’s food preferences. It may be necessary to limit amounts or change the way food is prepared.

BOX 27.2 Guidelines for Safe Food Practices

Shopping

1. • Packaging should be secure. Do not buy packages that are ripped or have broken seals, and do not buy dented cans.
2. • Check the “best before” date. Do not buy expired items.
3. • Select refrigerated and frozen foods last. Do not buy items with ice crystals on the package.
4. • Put meats, poultry, and seafood in bags to avoid cross-contamination.

Food Storage

1. • Do not leave groceries in a warm car. Freeze or refrigerate items promptly.
2. • Store raw meat, poultry, and seafood in plastic bags or covered bowls on the bottom shelf of the refrigerator. This prevents their fluids from dripping onto other foods.
3. • Store leftovers in small, shallow containers in the refrigerator. Such containers allow rapid cooling and prevent the growth of pathogens. Cover the containers with lids, foil, or plastic wrap. Write on the containers the date you stored the leftovers.
4. • Do not refreeze food.

Food Preparation

1. • Wash your hands before and after preparing food. Wash your hands immediately before and after handling raw meat, poultry, seafood, or eggs to avoid cross-contamination of other foods.
2. • Do not cough or sneeze over food.
3. • Wear a hairnet.
4. • Wear gloves if you have cuts on your hands or wrists.
5. • Defrost foods in the refrigerator, in the microwave, or under cold running water. Do not defrost food at room temperature.
6. • Prevent contact between raw and ready-to-eat food.
7. • Wash vegetables and fruits to remove pathogens and pesticides.
8. • Discard food with expired “best before” dates. Discard food if it has mould on it. If in doubt about freshness, discard the product.
9. • Do not keep leftovers longer than 2 to 3 days.
10. • Use clean utensils to take food items from refrigerated containers.
11. • Rinse raw meats, poultry, and seafood before use. Wash the sink thoroughly (see [Chapter 44](../B9780323709392000444/B9780323709392000444.xhtml)).
12. • Avoid recipes that call for eggs not having to be cooked.
13. • Use different cutting boards for raw meats and poultry; seafood; cooked food; cheese; and washed fruits and vegetables. For added safety, cut raw meats, poultry, and seafood on disposable waxed paper placed on top of the board. Wash the cutting board thoroughly (see [Chapter 44](../B9780323709392000444/B9780323709392000444.xhtml)).
14. • Wash and dry the tops of cans to remove pathogens. Wash can openers to prevent pathogens from entering cans.
15. • Wash knives or scissors used to cut open food packages.
16. • Follow the guidelines for cleaning kitchens (see [Chapter 44](../B9780323709392000444/B9780323709392000444.xhtml)).

Cooking and Reheating Food

1. • Cook foods to at least their minimum safe temperature ([TABLE 27.4](#t0025)). Cooking foods at the right temperature kills pathogens.
2. • Cook food thoroughly, especially meat, poultry, seafood, and eggs. Use a meat thermometer to determine if the meat is cooked. Eggs should be firm when eaten.
3. • Reheat sauces and gravies to a rolling boil.
4. • Stir and rotate food reheated in microwaves to prevent cold or hot spots.

Serving Food

1. • Serve food immediately after cooking it or removing it from the refrigerator. Remember, pathogens grow rapidly at room temperature.
2. • Serve food on a clean plate. Wash plates, platters, or containers used for raw meats, poultry, seafood, or eggs immediately after use.
3. • Use clean table linen, plastic mats, and eating surfaces.
4. • Do not use chipped or cracked dishes.

### Gluten-Free Diet

Gluten is a protein that comes from wheat or related grains, such as barley, oats, rye, triticale, Kamut, or spelt. Gluten can also be introduced into a food if that food came into contact with gluten-contaminated items during manufacturing or distribution. Approximately 340000 Canadians (or 1% of the population) cannot tolerate gluten.[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur277) Celiac disease is caused by a negative physical reaction to a gluten protein found in wheat or related grains such as barley, rye, and sometimes oats. The inflammation and destruction of the inner lining of the small intestine in celiac disease is caused by an allergic reaction to gluten in the diet.[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur277)

## Assisting Clients With Eating

A client’s appetite and ability to eat can be affected by weakness and illness, odours, unpleasant equipment, an uncomfortable position, the need for oral hygiene, the need to eliminate, and pain. As a support worker, you can help control some of these factors by helping your clients prepare for meals:

1. • Assist with oral hygiene, elimination, and handwashing.
2. • Change clothing of and provide clean linens for clients who are incontinent.
3. • Be sure dentures, eyeglasses, and hearing aids are in place. Ensure that eyeglasses are clean and hearing aids are turned on.
4. • Help clients get to the dining room.
5. • Ensure a comfortable position for eating. Help clients transfer from bed to chair or move to a sitting position in bed.

Some clients who cannot feed themselves because of weakness, paralysis, casts, or other physical limits need to be fed. Any client who needs to be fed may be at risk for swallowing difficulties or aspiration, so it is important that you pay close attention to the client as they eat. Needing to be fed can be difficult for the client to accept, so you need to be kind and supportive when feeding these clients (see the Providing Compassionate Care: Assisting a Client With Eating box on page 615).

### Making Meals Enjoyable

Some clients lose interest in eating because of illness or other factors. Small details can help a client enjoy a meal. Some of the following may not apply to clients on special diets. Check their individual care plans.

1. •
   Assist with menu choices. Help home care clients make choices from the Meals on Wheels menu. Help long-term care clients select a meal from the facility menu. If you are planning and preparing meals, involve the client in meal decisions.
2. •
   Make the setting attractive.
    In the client’s home, let the client choose table linens and utensils.
3. •
   Serve hot meals immediately. Lukewarm meals lack appeal.
4. •
   Serve moderate portions. Some clients lose their appetite at the sight of too much food. Ask clients how much food they want. Place the desired amount on the plate.
5. •
   Make mealtimes social occasions. To avoid loneliness at mealtimes, encourage clients in long-term care facilities to dine with others. In home care settings, provide company to clients by staying with them while you do quiet tasks, such as folding laundry.

### Challenges With Eating

Changes resulting from aging, illness, and disabilities—such as chewing and swallowing difficulties, weakness, and vision impairment—can cause eating challenges.

#### Chewing Difficulties

Clients with chewing difficulties are served foods that provide soft bulk (see “Mechanical soft diet” in [TABLE 27.4](#t0025), page 608). Foods are puréed with a food processor or blender. Follow the care plan for these clients. To help clients overcome chewing issues, do the following:

1. • Offer plenty of fluids
2. • Offer small mouthfuls
3. • Give the client time to chew

#### Swallowing Difficulty (Dysphagia)

Clients can have difficulty (dys) swallowing (phagia) (see [Chapters 32](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml) and [43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml)) for many reasons. Certain medications (including chemotherapy) decrease saliva production, resulting in dry mouth. Clients with paralysis may have difficulty swallowing because their throat muscles are affected. A swallowing assessment is completed by a speech pathologist, an occupational therapist, a physiotherapist, registered dietician, or a nurse who has received training in this assessment tool.

#### Warning Signs of Swallowing Difficulty

Signs that your client is having difficulty swallowing include the following:

1. • Frequent clearing of the throat
2. • Gurgling-sounding voice
3. • Delayed or reluctance to swallow
4. • Hiding food in cheeks (pocketing) or holding of food in their mouth
5. • Requiring multiple swallows for one bite
6. • Trouble controlling food and saliva in the mouth.
7. • Wet cough while eating, or episodes of choking on food while eating

Clients who have difficulty swallowing thin liquids often must drink thickened liquids. There are three common consistencies of thickened liquids[8](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur278):

1. •
   Nectar-thickened or easily pourable
   —consistency similar to thicker cream soup, or milkshake
2. •
   Honey-thickened or slightly thicker
   —consistency similar to honey; pours very slowly
3. •
   Pudding-thickened
   —will hold its shape when scooped; usually eaten with a spoon; similar to the consistency of pudding

A client who requires thickeners should not be given anything that melts, such as ice cream or ice cubes. Clients with dysphagia often do not get enough fluids, so it is important to offer fluids frequently throughout the day—every 2 hours or as per the care plan. Thickened liquids are considered part of a client’s fluid intake.

Thick, soft, moist foods are served to clients who have difficulty swallowing. The care plan may include the following measures to help a client swallow:

1. • Help the client sit upright, leaning slightly forward.
2. • Ask the client to lower their chin while swallowing.
3. • Offer plenty of fluids.
4. • Give the client time to chew and swallow before offering more food.
5. • Ask the client to remain sitting for at least 30 minutes after the meal.

When assisting clients who have swallowing challenges with eating, it is generally recommended that you give them one third of a teaspoon at a time. However, being fed such a small amount would involve many spoonfuls, which could tire the client. Follow the care plan regarding the amount you should feed at a time.

Clients with swallowing difficulties are at risk for choking and aspiration. Aspiration is the inhalation of fluid or an object into the lungs. If a client is unable to talk or cough, they may be choking, and you should call for help immediately. If no help is available, follow the emergency measures for choking (foreign-body airway obstruction) that you have been taught in your first-aid and CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation) course.

#### Weakness

Some clients are too weak to chew and swallow their food, and when they do not eat well, they become even weaker and have even less energy to eat. Never force a client to eat. If the client cannot eat, tell your supervisor. To encourage a client to eat, offer frequent, small, high-calorie meals, and serve nutritious drinks, including liquid dietary supplements. Soft foods that do not need much chewing are preferred. Follow the care plan, and do the following:

1. • Allow the client to rest before and after meals.
2. • Use the appropriate feeding utensils. (For example, the Ministry of Health and Long-Term Care [MOHLTC] in Ontario requires that clients be fed using a teaspoon.) Check with your supervisor and the client’s care plan.
3. • Provide a straw to drink (if allowed) so the client does not need to lift the glass. Check with your supervisor and the client’s care plan.
4. • Provide cups, glasses, and utensils that are light and easy to handle.

Clients with vision impairment (see [Chapter 34](../B9780323709392000341/B9780323709392000341.xhtml)) are often keenly aware of food aromas and often can identify the foods served to them. ## Fluid Balance

Harm, even death, can result from too much or too little water in the body, so fluid balance is essential for health. The amount of fluids taken in (intake) and the amount lost (output) must be equal. If fluid intake exceeds fluid output, tissues swell with water—a condition called edema (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)). Edema is common in clients with heart and kidney diseases. Dehydration is a decrease in the amount of water in body tissues, which occurs when fluid output exceeds intake. Common causes are low fluid intake, vomiting, diarrhea, bleeding, excess sweating, and increased urine production (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)).

### Normal Fluid Requirements

An adult needs 1 500 mL of water daily to survive. About 2 000 to 2 500 mL of fluid per day is needed for normal fluid balance, but water requirements increase in certain conditions—such as hot weather, exercise, fever, illness, and excessive fluid loss. Minimum water requirements vary with age (see the Focus on Children: Fluid Requirements and Focus on Older Persons: Fluid Requirements boxes).

### Calculating Daily Fluid Intake

Some clients must have their daily fluid intake calculated to ensure they receive enough fluids each day. Other clients have their daily fluid intake calculated against their daily urinary output (see [Chapter 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml)). The physician or nurse may want a client’s fluid intake and output measured, in which case you will have to keep intake and output (I&O) records. I&O records are used to evaluate fluid balance and kidney function and to plan medical treatment. They also are kept when the client has special fluid orders.

All fluids taken by mouth, as well as fluids given in intravenous (IV) therapy and tube feedings (see [Chapter 28](../B9780323709392000286/B9780323709392000286.xhtml)), are measured and recorded. The obvious fluids are measured—water, milk, coffee, tea, juices, soups, and soft drinks—as well as the fluid content in soft and semi-solid foods such as ice cream, sherbet, custard, pudding, jelly, and popsicles. Output measure includes urine, vomitus, diarrhea, and wound drainage.

#### Measuring Intake and Output

Intake and output are measured in millilitres (mL) or in cubic centimetres (cc). These metric-system measurements are equal in amount.

1. • 30 mL equals 1 ounce
2. • 500 mL is about 1 pint
3. • 1 000 mL is about 1 quart

You need to know the serving size of bowls, cups, glasses, and other containers. The information may be on the I&O record or in the policy manual.

A container called a graduate is used to measure fluids, including leftover fluids, urine, vomitus, and drainage from suction (see [Chapter 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml)). Similar to a measuring cup, the graduate is marked in millilitres or cubic centimetres as well as in ounces ([FIGURE 27.9](#f0050)). Plastic urinals and kidney basins are also marked with measurements.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Foods and liquids contain nutrients, which are substances that are ingested, digested, absorbed, and used by the body. Our bodies need a certain amount of proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, minerals, and water each day.

• Health Canada’s publication Canada’s Dietary Guidelines promotes wise food choices. The Dietary Guidelines are available in 28 languages, and a tailored version is available for Indigenous people in Canada.

• Nutritional requirements differ throughout a person’s life cycle.

• Be aware of the amount of caffeine you and your clients consume daily. Too much caffeine can cause adverse effects.

• Factors that can affect eating and nutrition are personal choices, allergies, food intolerances, culture, religion, finances, appetite, illness, and age.

• In the case of clients with type 1 diabetes, it is very important to record the intake for every meal and snack to ensure that their dietary intake is adequate for the amount of insulin they must take.

• Follow the care plan carefully, especially if you are to assist a client with eating.

• Recording intake and output is necessary when caring for a client who is ill. Harm, even death, can result from too much or too little water in the body.

## Enteral Nutrition

People who cannot chew or swallow often require enteral nutrition, a nutritional formula delivered into the gastrointestinal tract (enteral) through a feeding tube. Gavage is another term for tube feeding. Feeding tubes are used when food cannot pass normally from the mouth into the esophagus and then into the stomach. This need can arise due to cancers of the head, neck, or esophagus; trauma or surgery to the face, mouth, head, or neck; dysphagia caused by paralysis; and coma. As well, people with dementia who no longer know how to eat may require tube feedings. There are various types of feeding tubes:

1. • A nasogastric (NG) tube is inserted through the nose (naso) into the stomach (gastro) ([FIGURE 28.1](#f0010)). A physician or a nurse performs the procedure.
2. • A nasointestinal tube is inserted through the nose (naso) into the small intestine (intestinal) ([FIGURE 28.2](#f0015)). A physician or a nurse performs the procedure.
3. • A gastrostomy tube is inserted into the stomach. A surgically created opening (stomy) in the stomach (gastro) is needed for this procedure ([FIGURE 28.3](#f0020)). It is also known as a G-tube.
4. • A gastro-jejunostomy tube is a combination device that includes access to both the stomach and the jejunum (the middle part of the small intestine). It is also known as a GJ-tube. Typically, tubes are placed into a G-tube stoma (a surgically created opening—see ostomy), with a narrower long tube continuing through the stomach and small intestine. The GJ-tube is used if the client has a high risk for aspiration or when a tube cannot be placed into the client’s stomach. It must be inserted by a physician in a hospital setting.
5. • A jejunostomy tube is inserted into the intestines. A surgically created opening (stomy) in the middle part of the small intestine (jejunum) is needed for
    this procedure ([FIGURE 28.4](#f0025)). It is also known as a J-tube.

### Preventing Problems Related to Tube Feedings

Nasogastric and nasointestinal tubes pass through the esophagus and then into the stomach or small intestine. During insertion, the tube could accidentally slip into the respiratory tract, causing aspiration, which can cause pneumonia and even death. An X-ray is therefore usually taken after insertion to determine correct tube placement.

Coughing, sneezing, vomiting, suctioning, and poor positioning can move the tube out of place. A confused client can also move the feeding tube by pulling on it. The tube can move from the stomach or intestines into the esophagus and then into the airway. In a facility, a nurse checks tube placement before every scheduled tube feeding. In the case of continuous tube feedings, the nurse checks tube placement every 4 to 8 hours, depending on agency or facility policy. To do so, the nurse attaches a syringe to the tube and aspirates gastrointestinal secretions. Then the nurse measures the pH of the secretions. The pH readings will indicate if the tube has moved, since the pH readings for stomach contents and intestinal contents differ.

With nasogastric, gastrostomy, and PEG tubes, aspiration can also occur from regurgitation, which is the backward flow of food from the stomach into the mouth. Delayed stomach emptying and overfeeding are common causes of regurgitation. To prevent regurgitation, the client should be in the sitting or semi-Fowler’s position for the feeding and should remain in this position for 1 to 2 hours after the feeding to promote movement of the formula through the gastrointestinal system and prevent aspiration. The left-side-lying position has to be avoided, as it prevents emptying of the stomach.

The risk for regurgitation is less with nasointestinal and jejunostomy tubes. Formula is given at a slow rate and passes directly into the small intestine. Remember that, during digestion, food passes slowly from the stomach to the small intestine and that the stomach can handle larger amounts of food at one time than the small intestine can.

#### Signs of Aspiration

Aspiration is the inhalation of fluid or an object into the lungs. It is a very serious condition because it can cause breathing problems and infection. It is a major complication of nasogastric and nasointestinal tube feedings. The following are signs of aspiration in a client that should be immediately attended to:

1. • Coughing or choking during the feeding
2. • Change in breathing from normal to difficult, noisy, or rapid and shallow
3. • Wet, gurgly voice
4. • Pale or bluish lips

If you think that aspiration has occurred, stop the feeding immediately, make sure the client is sitting up and the airway is clear, and call for medical help.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur281)

Besides aspiration, which is the major risk, diarrhea, constipation, and delayed stomach emptying can also occur with tube feeding. Report the following immediately:

1. • Nausea
2. • Discomfort during the tube feeding
3. • Vomiting
4. • Diarrhea
5. • Distended (enlarged and swollen) abdomen
6. • Coughing
7. • Complaints of indigestion or heartburn
8. • Redness, swelling, drainage, odour, or pain at the ostomy site
9. • Elevated temperature

### Comfort Measures for the Client with a Tube Feeding

The client with a feeding tube is usually NPO (NPO stands for the Latin term nil per os, which means “nothing by mouth”). When dry mouth, dry lips, and sore throat cause discomfort, clients can suck on hard candy or chew gum, if allowed. Since one nostril is obstructed by the feeding tube, the client may breathe through the mouth, so frequent oral hygiene, lubricant for the lips, and mouth rinses are necessary. Sometimes, an ice bag applied externally to the throat can give relief for a sore throat. Give care as directed by your supervisor and the care plan.

Nasogastric and nasointestinal tubes can irritate and cause pressure on the nose. The nose and nostrils also are cleaned every 4 to 8 hours, depending on the policy of your agency or facility. To ensure that the tube does not exert pressure against the nostril, reposition the tube slightly away from the nostril. Sometimes, tubes alter the shape of the nostrils or cause pressure sores. Securing the tube using tape or a tube holder helps prevent these problems ([FIGURE 28.8](#f0045)). Tube holders have foam cushions that prevent pressure on the nose and eliminate the need for retaping; frequent retaping can irritate the nose. The tube also is secured to the client’s clothes with tape or by looping a rubber band around the tube and pinning the rubber band to the client’s clothes with a safety pin. There should be some slack in the tubing to allow movement by the client, but a tube being too tight or too loose can cause displacement of the tube.

## Intravenous Therapy

Intravenous (IV) therapy—also referred to as IV or IV infusion—involves giving fluids through an injection port, needle (such as a butterfly needle used on infants or children), or a cannula or catheter into a vein. A cannula (sometimes called a catheter) is plastic tubing threaded over a needle. Once the cannula or catheter is inserted into the vein, the needle is withdrawn. Physicians order IV therapy for the following purposes:

1. • To provide needed fluids when a client cannot take fluids by mouth
2. • To reverse dehydration in the body
3. • To replace minerals and vitamins lost because of illness or injury
4. • To provide sugar for energy
5. • To administer medications such as antibiotics
6. • To administer blood and blood products
7. • To provide hyperalimentation—the giving of a solution highly concentrated in nutrients

IV therapy is given in the hospital, outpatient care, subacute care, long-term care, and home care settings. Nurses are responsible for IV therapy—they start and maintain the infusion, according to the physician’s orders, and also give IV medications and administer blood.

### Common Intravenous Solutions

IV solutions vary in volume and contents, and are ordered by the client’s physician or other qualified health care provider. They may be ordered to run intermittently (periodically), such as only when a medication needs to be infused, or continuously, such as to rehydrate a person. Some may have electrolytes (such as potassium) added to replenish what the client’s body has lost.

Specific types of IV solutions are ordered to meet the specific health needs of each client, such as to ensure compatibility if the client is on IV medications. If the client is to have a blood transfusion, they may require a 0.09% NaCl (sodium chloride) solution IV as this is isotonic (and therefore compatible) to bodily fluids such as blood (see [Chapter 32](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml)).

Examples of common IV solutions include 5% dextrose solution, 0.09% NaCl (sodium chloride) solution, or 5% dextrose mixed with a 0.45% NaCl (sodium chloride) solution. Whatever the IV solution that is ordered, it must be sterile, and sterile technique must be maintained to avoid introducing potentially life-threatening bacteria into the body.

### Central Intravenous Sites

The subclavian vein and the internal jugular vein—located close to the heart—are central venous sites. A physician inserts a long catheter—called a central venous catheter or central line—into a central vein. The catheter tip is then threaded into the superior vena cava or right atrium ([FIGURE 28.9, A
 and
B](#f0050)). At times, the cephalic and basilic veins in the arm also are used for IV therapy. Catheters inserted into these sites are called peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs). The catheter tip is threaded into the subclavian vein or the superior vena cava (see [FIGURE 28.9, C](#f0050)). Only physicians and specially trained nurses can insert PICCs. The client is X-rayed after insertion of a central venous catheter or PICC to ensure proper placement.

Intravenous Therapy

Clients receiving IV therapy in their homes often have central venous catheters. The nurse teaches the client and family about administering IV medications and managing the catheter. Because these catheters or cannulas are inserted directly into a vein, you must be very careful when providing care. Ensure that you do not do anything around the insertion point that could dislodge the catheter. Report immediately any redness around the site. If a catheter falls out, apply pressure with sterile gauze until bleeding stops, and immediately inform your supervisor. If you are giving a tub bath to the client, ensure that the IV tubing is kept dry; if your client has a pump, it must be unplugged before the client enters the water. If your client is having a shower, keep the IV site dry by covering it with plastic wrap. Your supervisor will give you directions for care.

### Assisting With Intravenous Therapy

The most common site for an IV is the client’s lower arm or the back of the hand. Follow the safety measures in [BOX 28.1](#b0010) to ensure you provide safe care. As a support worker, you help meet the hygiene and activity needs of clients who are receiving IV therapy, but you are never responsible for starting or maintaining IV therapy. A client who has an IV will also need the support worker’s assistance to dress (see [Chapter 18](../B9780323709392000183/B9780323709392000183.xhtml)), and either the support worker or a nurse should teach the client how to ambulate safely with an IV line (see [FIGURE 28.11](#f0060)). See the Providing Compassionate Care: Enteral Nutrition and Intravenous Therapy box.

BOX 28.1 Safety Measures for Intravenous Therapy

1. • Follow Standard Practices.
2. • The position of the IV needle or catheter must be maintained. Do not move the needle, cannula, or catheter. If the needle or cannula is moved, it may come out of the vein; fluid will flow into the tissues (infiltration), or the flow will stop. If the needle or catheter becomes dislodged, the site will bleed; apply pressure with sterile gauze according to Standard Practices, and contact your supervisor immediately.
3. • Sometimes, the nurse may apply splints or restraints to prevent movement of the extremity ([FIGURE 28.10](#f0055)) and, thus, the movement of the needle or cannula.
4. • Protect the IV bag, tubing, and needle or cannula when ambulating the client. Portable IV stands are rolled along next to the client. When assisting the client to change gowns, care must be taken to prevent dislodging the needle or cannula ([FIGURE 28.11](#f0060)).
5. • Assist the client with turning and repositioning. Move the bag to the side of the bed where the client is lying. Always allow enough slack in the tubing to ensure the needle or cannula does not dislodge due to pressure on the tube.
6. • Notify your supervisor immediately if bleeding occurs from the insertion site. Follow Standard Practices.
7. • Notify your supervisor immediately if there is any leaking of fluid from the IV site.
8. • Notify your supervisor immediately of any signs and symptoms listed in [BOX 28.2](#b0015).
9. • Always be aware of your scope of practice for your province or territory and the policies of your facility or agency.

Complications can occur from IV therapy. Report at once if you see any of the signs and symptoms listed in [BOX 28.2](#b0015).

## Subcutaneous Infusion Therapy

Many clients who receive palliative care or suffer from persistent pain may require frequent injections of pain medications to help reduce their pain. In many instances, the client may still be in pain despite having a topical pain patch. Numerous injections, however, may cause discomfort and possible infections or skin problems around the injection sites. To eliminate the need for frequent injections, some physicians order subcutaneous infusion therapy for their clients. Unlike an IV, in which a needle is inserted into the client’s vein, the medication is given through an injection port or small needle that has been inserted into the client’s subcutaneous tissue layer using Standard Practices. IV tubing is then connected to this injection port or needle, which is then securely taped into place onto the client’s skin ([FIGURE 28.12](#f0065)). Pain medication can therefore be administered into the injection port instead of being injected into the skin.

2. • Puffiness or swelling
3. • Pale or reddened skin
4. • Complaints of pain at or above the IV site
5. • Hot or cold skin near the site
6. • Leaking of fluid from the site

Systemic—Involving the Whole Body

4. • Tachycardia (pulse rate more than 100 beats per minute)

6. • Cyanosis

14. • Confusion

Enteral Nutrition and Intravenous Therapy

1. •
   Dignity.
    Clients who have to rely on feeding tubes to supply their nutrition may be embarrassed by their tube.     Encourage clients to be as independent as possible. If they are able to ambulate, provide a portable IV pole to hold the feeding bag so they can walk while receiving their feeding.
3. •
   Individualized care.
    For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. For example, if the client has any discomfort (see [Chapter 23](../B9780323709392000237/B9780323709392000237.xhtml)), try to determine the type of pain the person has, report it to the appropriate person, and document it in the client record.
4. •
   Preferences.
    Encourage personal choices whenever possible. Allow clients receiving an enteral feeding to choose clothing that would cover their tubing dressing or stoma but also provide access for routine checking.
5. •
   Privacy. Providing for privacy is important when checking the client’s tubing site, especially if the client has tubing that enters into their abdominal area. Close the curtains or the door when inspecting the tubing or when the nurse is providing care to their dressing or stoma site.
6. •
   Safety. To prevent regurgitation after an enteral feeding, ensure that the client is in the sitting or semi-Fowler’s position for the feeding. Keep the client in this position for 1 to 2 hours after the feeding as well to promote movement of the formula through the gastrointestinal system and prevent aspiration. Avoid the left-side-lying position, which prevents emptying of the stomach.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Some clients, because of illness, injury, or surgery, are not able to swallow, eat, or drink and need a feeding tube to provide nourishment and medications.

• Enteral nutrition feeding may be done through a nasogastric tube, a nasointestinal tube, a gastrostomy tube, a gastro-jejunostomy tube, a jejunostomy tube, or a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube.

• Feeding-tube formulas may be commercially prepared or prepared by the facility where the client is.

• The feeding tube can be moved out of place from coughing, sneezing, vomiting, suctioning, poor positioning, or the client pulling on the tube. The nurse checks on the position of the tubing, depending on your employer’s policy.

• Aspiration is the major risk for clients with a feeding tube. Other symptoms to report immediately are nausea, discomfort, vomiting, diarrhea, distension, coughing, indigestion, respiratory distress, increased pulse rate, and complaints of flatulence.

• Physicians order IV therapy to provide needed fluids; to replace minerals, vitamins, or sugar; or to administer medication or blood.

• Sites used depend on the age of the client, the condition of the client, and the purpose of the IV therapy.

• As a support worker, you must be very careful when providing care to a client with an IV. You need to ensure that you do not move or dislodge the needle, catheter, or cannula. Re-insertion of an IV is painful for your client.

• As a support worker, you are never responsible for starting or maintaining IV therapy, but you must report immediately any of the signs and symptoms of IV therapy complications.
