    Other Household Products

    Numerous products in and around the home present an injury risk to children and youth:

    1. • Trampoline-related injuries have become increasingly common in recent years. Falls from the trampoline involving impact with the ground are the most severe, generating almost two-thirds of all fractures and one in five patients admitted to hospital.
    2. • Drownings and near-drownings can occur in association with bath seats, in particular when a child is left unattended.
    3. • Children playing around dangling blind or curtain cords are exposed to a strangulation hazard.
    4. • Furniture, televisions, and large appliances are frequently associated with injuries. Between 1990 and 2007, these items resulted in an average of about 9 000 injury cases per year, 71% of which involved children less than 5 years of age.

    Safety Measures at Home and in the Workplace

    Safety is something we take for granted until someone we know or love is injured. We need to be aware of safety hazards in our homes as well as in our workplaces, wherever that may be. As a support worker, you have the responsibility of ensuring your clients’ and co-workers’ safety as well as your own. Doing so begins with the recognition of common safety hazards (see the Supporting Mrs. Gerhlbardt: Creating a Safer Environment box) and reporting any lapses in safety measures immediately to the proper authority, usually your supervisor.

    Preventing Falls and Injuries

    Falls are the most common cause of accidental injuries in all settings, with children and older people being at greatest risk. While you should always supervise all of your clients, you should pay particular attention to children, clients in wheelchairs, clients with a history of injury, sedated clients, older clients, and cognitively impaired clients, as they are at greatest risk for injuries related to falls. Falls may result in death, serious injuries, or changes in the older person’s quality of life. For example, after a fall, an older person may avoid leaving the house because they fear falling again, but staying in the house may lead to feelings of isolation, anxiety, and depression. It may also lead to increased dependence on others.6

    Falls remain the leading cause of injury-related hospitalizations among Canadian older persons; between 20 and 30% of older people fall each year, and absolute numbers are on the rise.6 Falls can lead to negative mental health outcomes, such as fear of falling, loss of autonomy and greater isolation, confusion, immobilization, and depression. In addition to the negative physical and mental health consequences of falling, there are significant associated financial costs, estimated at $2 billion annually, a value 3.7 times greater than that for younger adults.6

    Clients who live with impairments that affect their abilities to think, remember, communicate, or concentrate, such as brain injuries, mental health disorders, dementia, and other conditions that cause confusion and disorientation, have an increased risk for falling.

    Many facilities have fall prevention programs, which incorporate a variety of strategies to prevent falls. Some of these strategies include the following:

    1. • Clients in facilities are assessed to determine their risk for falls, and those who are at high risk are provided extra supervision and assistance.
    2. • Staff who work with clients receive up-to-date information and training on how to move and care for clients at risk for falls. This education for staff includes training on how to use equipment properly and how to avoid getting injured themselves.
    3. • Methods used to identify clients at risk include a colour-coded bracelet worn by the client and a sign, sticker, or tag placed on the client’s chart, at the nurses’ station, or, in a few agencies, above the client’s bed to indicate that the client is at greater risk of falling. Wherever these signs are used, they should be placed in such a way as to be respectful of the client’s right to privacy.
    4. • Since most falls occur in bedrooms, bathrooms, and on stairs, particular attention is paid to making these areas as safe as possible.
    5. • Since falls sometimes occur when clients rush to get to the bathroom or commode without help, they are repeatedly reminded to ask for assistance.
    6. • Most falls in facilities occur in the evening, between 1800 hours (6:00 p.m.) and 2100 hours (9:00 p.m.), when the hallways may be darker than they were during the day. For this reason, keep hallways well-lit and free of obstacles or clutter.
    7. • Falls also are more likely during shift changes, when most of the staff are trying to listen to the shift report. To remedy this risk, many agencies use overlapping shifts so that staff remains available to check on clients while the shift report is taking place.
    8. • The safety measures listed in the Think About Safety: Measures to Prevent Falls Among Older Persons and Others at Risk box help prevent falls in home and facility settings. Most of these measures are included in fall prevention programs and care plans. Always follow the client’s care plan for specific safety measures. Students and staff alike could benefit from participating in any fall prevention workshops offered by the facility.

    Preventing Falls in Children

    Falls are the leading cause of injuries in children, and accidental injuries are the leading cause of death among Canadian children.5 Infants can easily roll off a change table or bed. Falls out of highchairs and infant seats and falls down the stairs are also common accidents among babies and children. Older children often fall when running or playing. When children are tired, they may not pay close attention to the potential dangers around them and may also have a delay in reaction time, which contributes to risk for an injury. Refer to the Think About Safety: Safety Measures to Prevent Falls Among Infants and Children box for ways to help ensure a child’s safety.

    Using the Call Bell

    Call bells are important safety devices for clients in facilities such as hospitals and long-term care residences. These devices may vary from one facility to another, so it is important to become familiarized with the devices’ use at each facility. A call bell enables a client to call for assistance. Different facilities use different call systems. The term call bell is used in this book when referring to any of a number of call systems that a facility may use. Most systems consist of a cord that is attached to the bed or a chair. A button is at the end of the cord (FIGURE 20.9). To get help, the client presses the button, which causes a signal to ring at the nurses’ station. Or pressing the button may turn on a light above the client’s door and at the nurses’ station (FIGURE 20.10, A). These signals alert the health care team that the client needs assistance.

    Some call bells are also connected to an intercom at the nurses’ station (FIGURE 20.10, B). Using the intercom, a support worker or a nurse can talk with the client who has called for help. In newer facilities, the call bell system may be directly connected to cordless phones carried by the staff. As a support worker in the facility, you would respond directly to the client by phone, and the client can tell you what is needed. However, be careful when using an intercom or a cell phone, as people nearby can hear what you say; keep in mind the client’s right to confidentiality.

    Measures to Prevent Falls Among Older Persons and Others at Risk

    1. • Report throw rugs and small mats that are not secure. They can be secured with carpet tape or nonskid backing. Make sure throw rugs and small mats are not in high-traffic areas or at the top of stairways.
    2. • Report loose floorboards and tiles. Report frayed, torn, or bumpy rugs and carpets.
    3. • Keep traffic areas free of telephone and electrical cords. Cords and wires can be taped to the wall so the client will not trip over them.
    4. • Keep the floors and stairways free of clutter. Never leave items on the stairs.
    5. • Use only nonglare and nonslip floor wax.
    6. • Clean up water and other spills immediately.
    7. • Ensure proper lighting, especially near stairways and bathrooms. Offer to replace burned-out light bulbs.
    8. • Turn on night lights in the halls, especially near the bathroom, in case the client has to get up in the middle of the night.
    9. • Keep a clear path from the bedroom to the bathroom.
    10. • Do not rearrange the client’s furniture.
    11. • Use nonslip rubber bath mats or nonslip strips in bathtubs and showers.
    12. • Make sure the client’s footwear and clothing fit properly. Shoes and slippers must have nonskid soles. Long shoelaces should be avoided. Clothing should not be loose or drag on the floor. Belts must be tied or secured in place.
    13. • Ensure that crutches, canes, and walkers have nonskid tips. Make sure the client uses these devices correctly.
    14. • Remember that clients have the right to live at risk of injuring themselves. For example, even if they understand that their scatter rugs are a potential hazard, they may choose to keep them.
    15. • Check wheelchair brakes. Make sure they can be locked and unlocked. Remind the client to keep the brakes locked when not moving the wheelchair, as the brakes prevent the chair from moving when the client transfers to or from the chair. Lock both brakes before you transfer the client to or from the wheelchair. Do not let the client stand on the footrests. Make sure front wheels point forward to keep the wheelchair balanced and stable. Remove the armrests and footrests (if removable) when the client transfers to or from the wheelchair (see Chapters 14 and 15).
    16. • Encourage clients to wear their glasses or hearing aids, as needed. Reading glasses should not be worn when the client is walking. All devices should be put away safely when not in use.
    17. • Position the client in bed, in a chair, or in a wheelchair properly. Use pillows, wedge pads, or seats, as directed by the care plan.
    18. • Check the client often. Careful and frequent observation is important, especially for persons with risk factors.

    Think About Safety

    Safety Measures to Prevent Falls Among Infants and Children

    1. • Do not leave infants and young children unsupervised. Never leave them unattended in highchairs or on a table, couch, bed, or other high surface.
    2. • Secure the child in a highchair or infant seat. Use both the waist and crotch straps. Lock the highchair tray after securing the child in the chair. Keep highchairs away from stoves, tables, and counters. The child could push off against these and fall backward.
    3. • Do not use baby walkers; they have caused many serious falls and injuries.
    4. • Use safety gates at the top and bottom of stairs. Make sure the child cannot get caught in the slats.
    5. • Keep one hand on a child lying on a scale, bed, change table, or other furniture (FIGURE 20.8).

    FIGURE 20.8 Keep one hand on a child on a change table. 7. • Keep one hand on a baby when changing diapers. Gather supplies before changing diapers or bathing an infant. Place supplies within easy reach. 8. • Keep crib rails up and in the locked position. Frequently check children in cribs. 9. • Make sure there is nothing in the crib that the baby can stand on (such as large stuffed toys or firm bumper pads). Standing on something in the crib could cause the baby to fall over the top of the railing. 10. • Do not let children under 6 years of age sleep or play on the top bed of a bunk bed. 11. • Keep children away from windows. Do not let them sit on windowsills or lean on windows. Window screens are not strong enough to prevent a child from falling out. Do not put furniture underneath or near windows. Children could climb on it to reach the window. 12. • Do not let children run with objects in their hands or mouths (such as baby bottles, soothers, or sticks). The object could cause injury if the child falls. 13. • Take measures to prevent furniture such as TVs, bookcases, dressers, and tables from falling on children. Injuries and deaths can occur when children climb, pull on, sit on, lean on, or try to move furniture. Heavy furniture should be anchored to the wall.

    Focus On Home Care

    Call Bells

    Some home care clients are unable to move out of bed or a particular part of the home, and they need a way to call for help. Tap bells, dinner bells, baby monitors, and other devices are all useful in home care settings. Or you can give the client a small can with a few coins inside that can be rattled to get your attention. Children’s toys with bells, horns, and whistles may also be useful. Whatever the client decides to use, keep the calling device within the client’s easy reach.

    Whenever you finish a procedure and leave the client’s room, make sure the call bell is within the client’s reach. You must do the following:

    1. • Keep the call bell within the client’s reach in the room, bathroom, and shower or tub room. Even if the client cannot use the call bell, keep it within reach of visitors and staff, as they may need to call for help.

A prompt response also helps prevent infection, skin breakdown, pressure ulcers, and falls.

    Preventing Accidental Poisoning

    All children, especially when they are very young, put things in their mouths as part of learning and exploring their world. This natural instinct is one reason hundreds of children every year are victims of accidental poisonings.5 Parents, guardians, and caregivers can prevent many of these accidents by identifying and locking away toxic materials.

    Accidental poisoning is most common in children between the ages of 1 and 4 years, when they are crawling or walking and exploring their environments. Eating substances such as aspirin, cigarette butts, or vitamin pills is a common cause of childhood poisoning. Poisoning also occurs when certain poisonous substances—for example, oven cleaner or pesticide—spill on the skin and are absorbed into the body.5 (See the Think About Safety: Ways to Prevent Accidental Poisoning box.)

    What to Do if You Suspect Poisoning

    In community settings, the phone number for the local poison control centre should always be posted near the telephone, programmed into your cell phone, or carried in your bag. You might suspect poisoning if you find empty pill bottles or hazardous products lying around. You might also suspect poisoning if the client suddenly collapses, vomits, or has difficulty breathing. If you suspect poisoning, follow these steps:

      1. Contact the emergency medical services (EMS) for your area by dialling 9-1-1 on the telephone. The EMS operator will give you clear instructions.
      1. Gather any empty pill bottles or other evidence of poisoning to determine what has been ingested and how much.
      1. Stay with your client. Be sure to remain calm.

    Protecting Children From Burns

    Accidental burns are common in children and can cause significant morbidity (illness) and mortality (deaths). Accidents are the leading cause of deaths in children in Canada.7 Degrees of burns are determined by the extent of tissue damage. Children and older persons are both at great risk for burns.

    Scalding injuries from hot liquids such as coffee or tap water are often responsible for childhood burns.8 For example, a child may get in the way when an adult is carrying a hot drink, and the adult may trip over the child and spill the hot liquid on the child.

    Ways to Prevent Accidental Poisoning

    1. • Read the labels on all household products before buying them, and try to use the least toxic ones. Among the household products generally considered less hazardous are nonchlorine bleaches, vinegar, borax, beeswax, mineral oil, and compressed air drain openers (rather than corrosive liquids).

Poison experts have come across numerous cases of toddlers dragging a chair over to a kitchen counter, climbing onto the counter or even the refrigerator, and opening a cupboard that is then at eye level.
    3. • Do not rely on childproof container lids. These are not always childproof. No bottle top can be made so fail-safe that a child cannot find a way to get it off. It is not unusual for a 2-year-old, left alone for 30 minutes, to break into even the “safest” devices of a manufacturer.
    4. • Keep medicines, pesticides, and even detergents in their original containers. Rewrite any labels that get smudged so that you know exactly what is in each bottle. Never put poisonous or toxic products in containers that were once used for food. Emergency medical staff have heard far too many horror stories of toxic liquid put into an unmarked container and having been mistaken for juice.
    5. • Never refer to any kind of medicine as “sweets.” Even if you are trying to get a reluctant toddler to take his cough syrup, do not present it as something good to eat. Children learn by imitation, so when you are taking your own medications, make sure that they are not watching.
    6. • Never let children play with chipped or flaking paint. Old paint contains lead, which is a toxic substance known to cause neurological damage in children. Remove all chipped paint and consult your local poison control centre or a reputable paint store for ways to remove the lead paint from your walls and woodwork.

    Examples of Materials Found in the Home That Are Toxic

    1. • Household cleaning products. Assume that they are all dangerous. The safest thing to do is to keep them all out of the reach of children or disoriented adults living in the home, including drain cleaner, oven cleaner, toilet cleaner, dishwasher detergent, and rust remover. Even if individuals do not ingest them, they may spray themselves with them, burning their eyes or skin.

    Types of Burns

    Burns can severely disfigure and disable a person and can even cause death. Most burn injuries occur in the home. Infants and children as well as older persons are at highest risk. Burns are described as being first degree (red, painful skin that is not blistered); second degree, which affects the epidermis and dermis layers (resulting in pain, redness, blistering, and swelling of the skin); and third degree, which extends below the dermis to underlying layers and nerves (resulting in blackened, charred skin, which may be numb).

    Causes of burns include the following (also refer to Chapter 40):

    1. • Dry heat—fire, stoves, space heaters
    2. • Moist heat—hot liquids, steam
    3. • Chemicals—oven cleaner, drain cleaner, rust remover, and so on
    4. • Electricity—faulty electrical equipment, live wires, or lightning (FIGURE 20.21)
    5. • Radiation—sunlight

    Burns are more severe and require emergency help in the following circumstances:

    1. • They are located on the head, face, neck, hands, feet, or genitals.
    2. • They are spread over a large area of the body.
    3. • The burned person is under 2 or over 50 years of age or has a pre-existing medical condition (such as diabetes or hypertension).

    The size and depth of the burn also affect its severity.

    Preventing Suffocation

    Suffocation occurs when breathing stops due to lack of oxygen. Brain damage or death may occur, depending on how long the person has been deprived of oxygen, sometimes within 3 to 4 minutes. Common causes of suffocation include choking, drowning, inhaling gas or smoke, strangulation, and electrical shock. Children under 1 year, clients who have difficulties chewing and swallowing, and clients with serious mobility disorder or intellectual disability are at risk for suffocation (see the Think About Safety: Measures to Prevent Suffocation in Adults box).

They have no idea what is happening to them—that this gas, instead of oxygen, is quickly binding to the hemoglobin in their red blood cells, which can be fatal (see Chapter 39).

    Restraints and How to Avoid Using Them

    A restraint is any device, garment, barrier, furniture, or medication that limits or restricts freedom of movement or access to one’s own body. Restraints are rarely used now; only a small number of the types of restraints that were allowed in the past are still allowed in Canada today. Only restraints currently in use will be discussed in this textbook.

    It is important to know that every effort should be made to protect clients without resorting to the use of restraints.     A common misunderstanding is that restraints will decrease agitated behaviour and the likelihood of a person falling. In fact, restraints often make people more agitated, confused, or combative. Clients are also more likely to sustain injury when restrained. People who are restrained may not understand what is happening to them and may try to get out of the restraint.     Promoting Your Personal Safety

    As a support worker, you are sometimes exposed to safety hazards on the job. You could have an accident if your workplace environment is not safe. You could be injured when working with hazardous materials. You might also be at risk for workplace violence.

    Creating a Safe Workplace

    In Canada, each province and territory has occupational health and safety (OH&S) legislation. OH&S legislation is designed to protect employees from injuries and accidents in the workplace. The name of this legislation varies across the country, but in most places it is called the Occupational Health and Safety Act (as in, for example, Ontario: https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90o01). It is revised regularly to reflect changes in policies.

    The details of the legislation also vary from one place to another, but the basic elements are similar. In general, the legislation assumes that all people in the workplace—employers, supervisors, and workers—are responsible for their own health and safety and also have certain rights and duties. To reduce the risk of a client displaying escalating responsive behaviours (see Chapter 38) the support worker should always practice the following steps:

    1. • Follow the client’s care plan. For example, if the care plan calls for two-person care, do not provide care by yourself.
    2. • Watch for verbal and nonverbal signs agitation or anxiety (see Chapter 38). If these signs are present, STOP what you are doing, revise your approach, and, if necessary, call for help.
    3. • Always be aware of your environment. Ensure that you are always situated close to an exit if the client’s behaviour starts to escalate.
    4. • Explain every step you are performing to the client.

    Employers’ and Supervisors’ Responsibilities

    According to OH&S legislation, your employer and supervisor must take every reasonable precaution to protect your health and safety (see the Focus on Home Care: OH&S Legislation box).

    Your employer is responsible for the following:

    1. • Having written policies that promote safety
    2. • Training and educating you about these policies
    3. • Creating a health and safety committee to identify hazards in the workplace and investigate accidents
    4. • Responding to reports of workplace hazards
    5. • Warning you about safety hazards and correcting these hazards, whenever possible
    6. • Reporting all accidents promptly to the government department responsible for occupational health and safety
    7. • Making sure that all necessary equipment (including personal protective equipment such as gloves and masks) is available and is kept in good working order

    2. • Robbery
    3. • Rape
    4. • Kidnapping
    5. • Beating, stabbing, or shooting
    6. • Murder

    Workplace violence can occur in any environment where an employee is carrying out a work-related task, including buildings, parking lots, field locations, homes, and means of travel to and from work assignments. The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (https://www.ccohs.ca/) provides information about healthy workplaces, workplace violence, and harassment.

    Home care workers in particular have personal security risks. They work alone in an unknown, uncontrolled environment and often work early or late hours. Clients, family members, or any person in the home could act violently toward them.

    Think About Safety

    Personal Safety Measures

    When Considering Your Own Safety, Be Sure to Also Take Steps Toward Your Electronic Safety:

    1. • Do not provide personal information about yourself that can give strangers access to you on any website, chat room, or social networking site.
    2. • Take electronic threats directed toward you as seriously as you would any threat received in person. Report it to your local police department. If the threat has come from a co-worker or client, notify your supervisor.
    3. • Do not respond to any electronic threat, as responding may only make the situation worse. If possible, print off a hard copy of the threat or save it electronically to provide proof of receiving the threat.

    When Driving to and From Work, Take the Following Precautions:

    1. • Familiarize yourself with the area where you will be driving. Plan your route in advance, keeping to well-travelled streets and routes that you know. Note the locations of nearby police stations, gas stations, and other public buildings that may be of help in an emergency.
    2. • Let your supervisor and a member of your family know the route you will take.
    3. • Keep with you the phone number of a reliable tow truck company.
    4. • Keep maps or a global positioning system (GPS) in the car.
    5. • Maintain your car in good working order at all times.
    6. • Always use a seat belt.
    7. • Be prepared for winter driving conditions. Keep a survival kit in the car—booster cables, shovel, extra clothing, a blanket, sand, and road flares.
    8. • Keep cash with you for gas or to make a phone call.
    9. • Have a cell phone for emergencies on the road, and program emergency numbers into the phone. Do not use the phone while driving. Pull off the road if you need to make a call.
    10. • Pull over to a safe spot away from traffic if you have car trouble. Put on your hazard lights, and call for help if you have a cell phone. Keep your doors locked and windows rolled up until help arrives. Never leave the car and walk off alone for help.
    11. • Check for places to park. Choose a well-lit, busy area. If using a parking garage, park near entrances or exits and on a lower level. Try to stay close to the parking attendant if possible. Be aware of passengers sitting in parked cars, and avoid parking by these cars if possible. Lock your car doors as you leave.
    12. • Ask for an escort to walk you back to your car after work. Some facilities provide this service. In a home setting, ask a family member to escort you.
    13. • Have your car key ready so you can get into the car quickly. Do not fumble for keys on the way to or at the car. If necessary, you can use your house or car keys as a weapon. Carry them in your strong hand. Have one key extended. If you are attacked, slash at the attacker’s face with the key.
    14. • Check for a possible intruder in the front and back seats before getting into your car.
    15. • If you think someone is following you, cross the street or change directions. Head toward a public building or a lighted house. If you are scared, yell for help.
    16. • Lock the car doors when you get in the car. Keep the windows rolled up.
    17. • Keep purses, backpacks, and other valuables under the seat or near your side. Do not leave them on the seat. They are easy targets for robberies.
    18. • Do not carry valuables. Do not bring your purse or valuables into clients’ homes. Leave them at home or in the car trunk. If an attacker demands something you have, give it to them. The only thing of value is you. Report the crime to the police.
    19. • Carry a whistle or a “shriek alarm.” Carry a travel-size can of aerosol hairspray. Aim for the attacker’s face. If someone tries to force you into a vehicle, do whatever you can to resist being pulled in. Fight back even if you see a weapon (yell, kick, push your thumbs into the attacker’s eyes). You are at much greater risk if you are forced into the vehicle.

    When Visiting a Client, Take the Following Precautions:

    1. • Call your supervisor when you arrive at and leave a client’s home.
    2. • Be cautious in hallways, elevators, and stairwells in apartment buildings. Do not get on an elevator with a stranger who makes you nervous.
    3. • Stay in the centre of the hallways and avoid hidden corners in apartment buildings. If someone threatens you, knock on as many doors as possible and yell to get attention. If you are attacked, pull the fire alarm.
    4. • Pause for a few seconds when entering someone’s home. Assess the environment. Check any threats to your safety, including used syringes, strange odours, clutter, unfamiliar people, or household items that could be used as weapons. Note the location of the phone. Note any obstacles blocking exits.
    5. • Keep your shoes on. In an emergency, you may need to leave in a hurry. In the winter, bring a pair of shoes for indoor use.
    6. • Leave if a client or anyone else is abusive or makes sexual comments or suggestions.
    7. • If you feel uncomfortable or threatened in any way, go to a safe place and immediately call your supervisor.
    8. • Let the client lead the way down corridors, up staircases, and so on.
    9. • Leave an exit route, or place yourself between the exit and the client. Never allow yourself to be cornered. Decide whether you should leave a door open or unlocked. Tell the client not to shut or lock a door if the action makes you uncomfortable.
    10. • Sit in a hard-backed chair. You can get up faster from a firm chair than from a soft chair or sofa.
    11. • Sit with your strong leg back and your other leg forward. This position lets you get out of your seat quickly without using your hands.
    12. • Ask that pets be restrained or kept out of the room if you are nervous about them.
    13. • If a client or family member becomes angry and begins to scream, stay calm. (Refer to the Think About Safety: The ABCDs of Managing Responsive or Challenging Behaviours box in Chapter 38).
    14. • Report any unusual incidents to your supervisor as soon as possible.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • Because of age or physical or emotional health condition, clients may be at great risk for accidents and injuries. It is up to facilities and community care agencies to try to create and maintain a safe environment for their employees and their clients.

    • Older persons, children, and people living with illnesses or disabilities are especially vulnerable to experiencing accidents. Some of their accident risk factors include (1) impaired awareness of surroundings, (2) impaired vision, (3) impaired hearing, (4) impaired taste, smell, or touch, (5) impaired mobility, (6) medication effects, and (7) age.

    • All health care workers should be aware of (1) common poisonous substances and what to do if a poisoning is suspected, (2) ways to prevent burns in older persons and others at risk, (3) ways to prevent suffocation in adults, (4) ways to prevent equipment accidents, and (5) safety measures for preventing fires, including how to use oxygen safely and how to assist in the evacuation of a building in case of a fire.

    • Health care workers must implement safety measures aimed at preventing falls among older persons and other clients at risk.

    • Although you should always supervise all of your clients, you should pay particular attention to children, clients in wheelchairs, clients with a history of injury, sedated clients, older persons, and clients who are cognitively impaired, as they are at greatest risk for injuries related to falls.

    • Restraints can be classified as (1) physical restraints, (2) environmental restraints, and (3) chemical restraints.

    • A restraint is any device, garment, barrier, furniture, or medication that limits or restricts freedom of movement or access to one’s own body. Restraints can cause emotional harm and serious injury.

    • The use of restraints requires a physician’s order after consultation with the client’s family.

    • Support workers should be aware of the many alternatives to using restraints.

    • Occupational health and safety laws require health care employees to understand what hazardous materials are and how to safely handle the ones they may come in contact with. This educational system is called WHMIS, or the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System.

    • Health care workers should be aware of the measures to prevent personal injury both at work and outside of work and be prepared to use them if necessary.

    The Client’s Bed

    For those who are confined to bed, comfort is especially important. Many home care clients have regular beds. Use pillows to help clients sit comfortably in a regular bed.

    Regular Beds

    In clients’ homes, you will see twin-, double-, queen-, and king-sized beds, as well as waterbeds, sofa sleepers, cots, and recliners.     Think About Safety

    Alternating Pressure Mattresses and Side Rails

    The air, gel, or water baffles in an alternating pressure mattress reduce the risk for pressure sores. It is important when making a bed with an alternating pressure mattress to ensure that layers of bed linens do not reduce the effectiveness of the baffle action. Most manufacturers recommend that incontinence pads of any type not be used, and some recommend that even a bottom sheet not be used. If they can be used, bottom sheets should not be tucked under the mattress as they would be with a regular mattress . Instead, they should be allowed to hang down at the sides. If the client needs to be repositioned, a slider sheet (or slider sheet system) designed for this use should be used (see Chapter 14). Bottom sheets that cannot be tucked in can be a source of wrinkles (and pressure areas) under the client’s fragile skin. It is therefore important to pull and straighten the sheet under the client whenever the client is repositioned.

    Any client using an alternating pressure mattress should be checked frequently. There have been reported cases of clients who have died as a result of being wedged between the air mattress and the bed frame.1 Bed rails also can cause injury to the client who is on an alternating pressure mattress. Always follow the client’s care plan regarding their use of bed rails and regularly monitor any client who has side rails. Refer to Chapter 20 for a discussion on side rail safety.

    Linen

    How often linen is changed varies among work settings (TABLE 21.1, page 465). Follow the care plan and your employer’s policy. For example, in most long-term care facilities, linen changes are routinely done on days the client is showered or bathed. In all settings, linen is changed when it is wet, damp, or soiled.

    When handling linen and making beds, follow the rules of Standard Practices. Perform hand hygiene before collecting clean linen. Your uniform is considered dirty, so you must always hold linen away from your body and uniform (FIGURE 21.17). Never shake linen in the air, as doing so could spread microbes. Clean linen should be placed on a clean surface. Never put clean or soiled linen on the floor . If clean linen accidentally touches the floor, put it in the laundry. If you are changing the linens in a semi-private room or a room with four beds, remember that linens for one client should be placed only on that client’s furniture, not on another client’s furniture.

    In order to reduce unnecessary time and steps, as well as to prevent cross-contamination of germs from one client’s room to another (see Chapter 19), you need to plan your steps. Take only the linens you will need into the room. If you find you have taken extra linens into the room, do not place them back in the linen room or on the linen cart—leave them in the client’s room. The linens can then be used the next time linens are changed.

    Focus On Home Care

    Incontinence Pads

    Medical supply stores sell disposable or washable waterproof pads which are used as incontinence pads. Disposable waterproof pads should never be used as a turning pad as they will tear if used for this purpose. If permitted at your agency, washable incontinence pads can be used for turning most clients because they are usually strong and large enough.

    The case manager will discuss the need for these items with the client and family. Some clients place plastic mattress protectors on their beds, but these protectors do not protect the bottom linens (bottom sheet and mattress pad). Never use plastic garbage bags or dry-cleaning bags, as these are not strong enough to protect the linens and mattress. As well, they slide easily and can move out of place, and the danger of suffocation is great if the client’s movements result in the bag covering the client’s nose and mouth. Again, your supervisor and the care plan will tell you what is safe to use for the client.

    Bed Making

    Your job as a support worker will include making beds. No matter what type of bed you make, safety, proper body mechanics, and medical asepsis are important. Follow Standard Practices. BOX 21.1 lists the guidelines for bed making (also see the Focus on Children: Crib Safety box).

    Focus On Children

    Crib Safety

    Cribs, crib linen, mattresses, other linen, and bumper pads all pose many dangers to infants, as they can lead to strangulation and suffocation. Report any safety hazard to your supervisor. Always follow these safety rules:

    1. • The crib mattress must be firm. A soft mattress can cover the baby’s nose and mouth, causing suffocation.
    2. • The mattress must fit snugly in the crib frame. The baby’s head could get caught between the frame and the loose mattress and cause strangulation.
    3. • The space between the mattress and crib sides should be no more than 3 cm. Only two adult fingers should fit in the space. If more than two fingers fit, the mattress is too small.
    4. • The mattress must be at least 65 cm (34 in.) lower than the top of the crib rails to protect the baby from falling out of the crib. The mattress should be lowered even more when the baby can stand in the crib.
    5. • Plastic garbage bags and dry-cleaning bags must not be used to protect the mattress.
    6. • Bumper pads are no longer recommended for use in cribs, as they pose a risk for strangulation of the baby.
    7. • Do not tuck blankets and top sheets under the mattress, as the baby can get caught in them.
    8. • Do not place pillows, fluffy comforters, stuffed toys, or heavy blankets in the crib when the baby is in it, as these items can cause suffocation.
    9. • Check linens for loose threads or stitching, and trim any found, as loose stitching can cause strangulation.
    10. • The space between the side bars should be no more than 6 cm.
    11. • Cribs manufactured before 1986 do not meet safety regulations and must not be used.

    The Open Bed

    An open bed is made shortly before the bed is to be occupied. Top linens are folded back so that the client can get into bed easily (FIGURE 21.28). An open bed is made when the client is out of bed only for a short time, or it is made just before the client goes to bed.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • The basic bed positions are the flat position, Fowler’s positions, the Trendelenburg position, and the reverse Trendelenburg position. The care plan will tell you which position to place the client in. The client’s position depends on their condition.

    • The linens required for bed making and the frequency of linen changes will vary among health care settings. Follow your employer’s policies.

    • Clean linen must be held away from your uniform to prevent transfer of microorganisms, and dirty linen must be removed with gloves and placed into the receptacle approved by your employer.

    • A closed bed is made when the bed is expected to be unoccupied for a while; the top linens are pulled up, with the top sheet folded over the bedspread into a cuff or the bedspread pulled neatly over the pillows, as per facility policy. An open bed is made shortly before the bed is to be occupied; top linens are folded back so that the client can easily get into bed. An occupied bed is made with the client in it.

    • One of the most important concepts in this chapter to remember is that the bottom sheet, next to your client’s skin, needs to be wrinkle free to prevent skin irritation, which can lead to skin breakdown of buttocks, ankles, and elbows and to bedsores.

    • Use good body mechanics to reduce your chance of injury when making a bed.

    Skin is the body’s first line of defence. It protects the body from microbes that cause infection and disease. As a support worker, you need to consider providing good skin care to your clients one of your most important tasks.

    Infants, older persons, and clients who have disabilities are at greatest risk for skin breakdown because their skin can be easily injured due to limited mobility and certain health conditions that make their skin more susceptible to injury. It should be noted that the skin of infants and the skin of older people are quite different from each another. As a support worker, you should also be aware that clients who are diabetic or have circulation challenges (see Chapter 33) tend to have delayed healing times and can be at greater risk for wound and skin infections. For every client, you need to look for signs of skin problems and report them immediately to either the nurse or your supervisor, depending on the workplace. BOX 22.1 lists the common causes of skin breakdown.

    A wound is a break in the skin or the mucous membrane, which becomes a portal of entry for microbes. Wounds have many causes. A surgical incision leaves a wound, for example, and wounds often result from trauma—an accident (such as a fall or vehicular accident) or violent act (such as a gunshot or stabbing) that injures skin, mucous membranes, bones, or internal organs. Other wounds may result from immobility or poor blood circulation. As a support worker, it is important to prevent skin injury by providing frequent and thorough skin care. When injury does occur, infection is a major threat, so wound care is very important for preventing infection and further injury to the wound and nearby tissues. A good source that provides information on wound care is Wounds Canada (https://www.woundscanada.ca/). In addition to the types of wound care listed in this chapter, physicians may also order heat and cold applications for wound care (see Chapter 40) to promote healing and comfort and to reduce tissue swelling.

    The support worker’s role in wound care depends on provincial or territorial laws, job description, and the client’s condition. Whatever your role, you need to know the various types of wounds, how wounds heal, and how to promote wound healing. It is also important to follow Standard Practices and to wear gloves while dealing with any open wound. Wound care protocols may differ between agencies, municipalities, or provinces/territories, so for the sake of consistency it is important that you adhere to the protocols followed by your agency.

    Skin Tears

    A skin tear is a break or rip in the skin. The epidermis (top skin layer) separates from the underlying tissue. The hands, arms, and lower legs are common sites for skin tears. Many older people have very thin and fragile skin, and even slight pressure or friction can cause a skin tear. Friction is the resistance that skin encounters when it rubs against another surface such as clothing, bedding, or another fold of skin. Clients who are most likely to have skin tears are those with very dry and paper-thin skin.

    Causes of Skin Tears

    Skin tears occur from friction, shearing, pulling, or direct pressure on skin. Shearing is the tearing of skin tissues as a result of skin sticking to a surface (usually the bed or chair) and deeper tissues moving downward, exerting pressure on the skin (see Chapter 14). Blood vessels and tissues become damaged, reducing blood flow to the area and increasing the risk for a pressure injury. Shearing occurs when the client slides down in the bed or chair. Skin tears are commonly caused by the following:

    1. • Bumping a hand, arm, or leg on any hard surface such as a bed, bed rail, chair, wheelchair footrest, or table
    2. • Holding on to a client’s arm or leg too tightly
    3. • Repositioning, moving, or transferring a client without a transfer sheet or other nonfriction surface, the absence of which can cause the client’s skin to rub against the surface and even tear
    4. • Bathing, dressing, and other tasks
    5. • Pulling buttons or zippers across fragile skin

    It is always your responsibility to be careful when moving, repositioning, or transferring clients. Skin tears are painful, and they can become portals of entry for pathogens. Tell your supervisor at once if you cause or find a skin tear, bruise, bump, or scrape (see the Supporting Mrs. Nippeskaya: Preventing Further Skin Injuries in a Frail Client box).

    Pressure Injuries

    A pressure injury (also called a decubitus ulcer, bedsore, pressure ulcer, or pressure sore) is any injury caused by unrelieved pressure. It usually occurs over a bony prominence—an area where the bone seems to “stick out” and is compressed under the client or between the client’s skin folds. The shoulder blades, elbows, hip bones, sacrum (the bone in the lower part of the spine), knees, ankle bones, heels, and toes are all bony prominences.

    These bony prominences are called pressure points because they bear the weight of the body in certain positions (FIGURE 22.2). Pressure from body weight can reduce blood supply to the area, which results in a pressure injury. Pressure points that are moist with perspiration or body excretions are especially prone to developing a pressure injury, as well as bacterial infections that are very difficult to treat once they set in.

    Causes of Pressure Injuries

    Pressure, friction, and shearing are common causes of skin breakdown and pressure injuries. Other factors include breaks in skin; poor circulation to an area; moisture (such as perspiration or urine); dry, flaky skin; and irritation from urine and stool. Pressure occurs when the skin over a bony prominence is squeezed between hard surfaces (the bone itself and another surface, such as a chair seat or mattress). In obese people, pressure injuries can develop in areas where friction is caused by skin-to-skin contact. Pressure injuries can also occur in thin people when two bony areas are in direct contact with each other (such as knees or ankles rubbing together). This squeezing or pressure interferes with the blood flow to the skin and underlying tissues, preventing oxygen and nutrients from reaching cells (see the Supporting Mr. Hansen: How Pressure Can Result in Skin Blistering box) and causing the death of the skin and tissues in the area (FIGURES 22.3 and 22.4). In many settings in both the United States and Canada, health care providers use the Braden Scale, which is used to predict the likelihood of a client developing a pressure injury (FIGURE 22.5). It takes into account the client’s sensory function, skin moisture, activity level, nutrition, and likelihood of friction and shear when the client is moved.

    Prevention and Treatment of Pressure Injuries

    Pressure injuries develop over time. The longer pressure is exerted on skin, the greater the risk is that a pressure injury will develop. Preventing pressure injuries is much easier than healing them. Good support care (which includes frequent position changes and ensuring a proper diet with lots of fluid), cleanliness, and skin care are essential. The Think About Safety: Guidelines for Preventing Pressure Injuries box lists guidelines for preventing skin breakdown and pressure injuries. As always, it is important that you also follow your client’s care plan and adhere to the preventive measures described on it.

    Clients at risk for pressure injuries are placed on a surface that reduces or relieves pressure—for example, foam, air, alternating air, gel, or water mattresses. The health care team decides on the best surface for the client.

    A physician orders specific pressure injury treatment—wound care products, medications, treatments, and special equipment—for each client. Your supervisor and the care plan will tell you what to do. On these mattresses, the client seems to float, which allows body weight to be evenly distributed and reduces pressure on bony parts.

Body alignment does not change, but pressure points change as the position changes, and friction is reduced. Some beds continually rotate from side to side and are useful for clients with spinal cord injuries.

    Leg And Foot Ulcers

    Some diseases may affect blood flow to and from the legs and feet, which can cause pain, open wounds, and edema (swelling caused by fluid collecting in tissues). Infection and gangrene can result from the open wound and poor circulation. Gangrene is a condition in which tissue dies and then decays. Gangrenous tissue may be black and have a foul smell (see Chapter 33).

    Clients with leg and foot ulcers need special skin care, as directed by a physician. The nurse uses the care-planning process to prevent skin breakdown on legs and feet.

    Circulatory Ulcers

    Circulatory ulcers (vascular ulcers) are open wounds on the lower legs and feet caused by decreased blood flow through arteries or veins. People with diseases affecting blood vessels are at risk for these ulcers on legs and feet, which can be painful and hard to heal. Depending on where they originate, they are classified as venous ulcers or arterial ulcers.

    Venous Ulcers

    Venous ulcers (stasis ulcers) are open wounds on the lower legs and feet caused by poor blood return through veins (FIGURE 22.13, A ). Blood travelling from the feet to the heart must defy gravity. In people who are active, the use of leg muscles assists the valves in their leg veins to pump the blood upward.

    Unfortunately, in some clients who are not active, who are extremely obese, or who have circulatory problems, blood will pool in the leg veins, or the valves will not close well. As a result, blood will not be pumped back to the heart normally, and blood and fluid will collect in their legs and feet, resulting in edema in their legs and feet. If you compress your fingers into the swollen, edematous tissue and then take away your fingers, you will notice that your fingers have left a skin impression, which is called pitting edema.

    Appearance

    Edema in tissues will give them a swollen appearance. If the edema is fairly recent, skin can also appear shiny and stretched. Edema and venous ulcers are painful and make walking difficult. Venous ulcers may “weep” fluid. Healing is slow, and infection is a great risk. If the edema lasts for a long period, skin will change in appearance and texture. Small veins in skin can rupture, allowing the hemoglobin in blood (which gives blood its red colour) to enter tissues, causing skin to darken and become dry, leathery, and hard. Itching is common.

    Wound Healing

    The healing process has three phases:

    1. • Inflammatory phase (3 days). Bleeding stops, and a scab forms over the wound, which prevents microbes from entering the wound. Blood supply to the wound increases, and the blood brings nutrients and healing substances. Because blood supply increases, signs and symptoms of inflammation—redness, swelling, heat or warmth, and pain—may appear, and there may be some loss of function.
    2. • Proliferative phase (day 3 to day 21). Proliferate means multiply rapidly. Tissue cells multiply to repair the wound.
    3. • Maturation phase (day 21 to 1 or 2 years). The scar tissue gains strength and appears red and raised but eventually becomes thin and paler than surrounding skin.

    Types of Wound Healing

    The healing process occurs through primary intention, secondary intention, or tertiary intention. With primary intention (also called first intention or primary closure), the wound is closed with sutures (stitches), staples, clips, or adhesive strips to hold the wound edges together.

    Secondary intention (also called second intention) is used in contaminated and infected wounds. Wounds are cleaned and dead tissue removed. Wound edges are not brought together—the wound is left gaping. Healing occurs naturally but takes longer, and a larger scar is formed. These wounds have a high risk of infection.

    Tertiary intention (also called third intention or delayed intention) involves leaving a wound open for a time before closing it. Thus, tertiary intention combines secondary and primary intentions. Infection and poor circulation are common reasons for choosing tertiary intention.

    Complications of Wounds

    Many factors affect healing and could increase the risk for complications—for example, the client’s age, general health, nutritional status and lifestyle, and the type and location of the wound. Clients with diabetes are at increased risk for infection. The client might be very old, might have a medical condition (such as circulatory disease or diabetes), or may have a lifestyle risk factor (such as smoking or poor diet) that might slow down the healing process. Other clients may be on certain medications (such as warfarin or heparin) that will prolong bleeding and possibly delay healing.

    Wound healing is dependent on good blood circulation to the wound as well as good nutrition for the client, with a diet rich in fluids (needed for tissue hydration), protein, and vitamin C (needed for tissue growth and repair). The wound must be protected from infection. Sometimes, the client’s doctor may prescribe antibiotics to prevent the development of an infection.

    Hemorrhage

    Hemorrhage is excessive loss of blood within a short period. If bleeding is not stopped, death results. Hemorrhage may be internal or external. Internal hemorrhage, when bleeding occurs into tissues and body cavities, cannot be seen. It leads to a hematoma, which is a collection of blood under skin and tissues. The area appears swollen and has a reddish-blue or grey colour. Shock, vomiting blood, coughing up blood, and loss of consciousness are signs of internal hemorrhage. External bleeding is visible as bloody drainage or through dressings soaked with blood. As with internal hemorrhage, shock can occur.

    Shock is a result of there not being enough blood supply to organs and tissues. Signs and symptoms of shock include low or falling blood pressure; rapid and weak pulse; rapid respirations; and cold, moist, and pale or greyish skin. A client experiencing shock is restless and may complain of thirst. Confusion and loss of consciousness eventually occur.

    Hemorrhage and shock are emergencies, so notify your supervisor immediately, and assist as requested. Remember to follow Standard Practices when in contact with blood. Gloves should always be worn, and gowns, masks, and eye protection are necessary when blood splashes and splatters are likely.

    Infection

    Wound contamination can occur at any time during or after an injury or surgery. Trauma often results in contaminated wounds, and surgical wounds can be contaminated during or after surgery. An infected wound has drainage and is painful and tender to the touch (BOX 22.5). A client with an infected wound may have a fever.

Odour Some wounds, especially those with necrotic tissue (dead tissue), may be very foul smelling, which can embarrass the client and ruin their appetite. • Keep drainage containers out of the client’s sight. • Open windows, whenever possible, to allow fresh air to enter the client’s room. • Ensure that dressing materials are removed promptly and placed in a plastic bag that is tied to contain the odour (see Chapter 19). • Discreetly use room air fresheners to mask any odour. • If possible, move the client into another room for meals.
    9. Temperature Fever is the body’s way of fighting infection by increasing white blood cells and blood flow to the area. Not all clients who have an infection will have a fever when you take the temperature. Some clients (such as older persons or clients with immune challenges) may not have a fever in the presence of an infection. • Take the client’s temperature, according to the care plan (see Chapter 24), and if there is a fever, report it. Often, fever is a sign of infection. • Report any abnormal findings at once. It is common for older persons to have a normally low body temperature, so they may not seem to have a fever even if there is an infection. Observe for other signs that indicate an infection (such as redness or swelling around the wound, tenderness, and warmth to the touch).
    10. Skin Care Clean, dry skin is less likely to harbour pathogens than moist, soiled skin. Pathogens can cause skin infections, which, in turn, can lead to skin injury and breakdown. • Keep the client’s skin clean and dry. Avoid the use of powders, which may dry out the skin too much and may irritate the client’s airway. • After washing the client’s skin, rinse it thoroughly and pat it dry. Do not towel-dry roughly, as this action can actually tear the skin of some frail clients. • Report and record any observations of edema, bruising, swelling, or any other abnormal feature.
    11. Maintaining Skin Integrity Intact skin is the body’s first line of defence against pathogens. It is important that skin is kept intact, as it is easier to prevent a skin infection than it is to cure one. • Transfer or position the client gently. Use equipment properly. Use friction-reducing equipment, such as sliding boards (see Chapter 14). • Never apply straps or boards directly on the skin, as this contact can increase the client’s risk of getting scratched or bruised. • Ensure that the client’s skin remains clean and dry at all times. Follow all of the above steps to ensure the client’s skin is not damaged at any time. • Avoid tight, restrictive clothing.

    Securing Dressings

    Dressings must be secure over wounds to avoid microbes entering the wound and drainage escaping (see the Focus on Children: Dressings box and Focus on Older Persons: Dressings box).

    Tape

    Adhesive, paper, plastic, and elastic tapes are common. Adhesive tape sticks well to the skin, but removing the remaining adhesive can be difficult, and the adhesive can irritate the skin. Some skin may come off with the tape, causing an abrasion. For clients who are allergic to adhesive tape, paper and plastic tapes are used since they do not usually cause allergic reactions. Elastic tape allows easier movement of the body part.

    Tape is applied to secure the top, middle, and bottom of the dressing (FIGURE 22.21). The tape extends several centimetres beyond each side of the dressing. The tape must not encircle the entire body part. If swelling were to occur, circulation to the part would be impaired.

    If the client can tolerate it, a picture-frame dressing—where the tape is applied to all four edges—can be used. This method of wound dressing is less likely to wrinkle or fall off. Many simple bandages already have adhesive tapes on all four edges or are transparent and allow for vapour and oxygen exchange through it (FIGURE 22.22).

    Focus On Children

    Dressings

    Children are often afraid of dressing changes. Tape removal is often painful for them, and the wound’s appearance can be frightening. It is important to ensure that the child is calm and cooperative, or the sterile field could become contaminated. A parent or caregiver should hold the child so that the wound can be reached with ease. Holding or playing with a favourite toy is often comforting to the child. To help decrease the child’s fear and apprehension about dressings, you can suggest that the child apply a dressing to a doll (and remove it).

    Focus On Older Persons

    Dressings

    As we age, our skin becomes thinner and more fragile, so skin tears are more common in older persons. Extreme care is therefore necessary when removing tape. One method that many nurses suggest using is to “peel” the tape off by folding the edge of the tape over and dragging it back. This technique avoids lifting the skin, which can be uncomfortable to the client and can injure the client’s skin.
