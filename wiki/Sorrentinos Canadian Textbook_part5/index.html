    Advocate on behalf of the client’s quality of life by promoting:

    Dignity • Independence • Individualized Care • Preferences • Privacy • Safety

    (see BOX 1.4, on page 17)

    Procedure

    • 1 Stand with your feet apart. Keep your back straight.
      • A broad base of support will prevent you from losing your balance while performing this procedure.
    • 2 Bring the client close to your body as quickly as possible. Use the gait belt if the client is wearing one. If not, wrap your arms around the client’s waist. You can also hold the client under the arms (FIGURE 15.23, A).
      • This will ensure that the client does not fall forward on their face, and you can slide the client down your leg.
    • 3 Move your leg so the client’s buttocks rest on it. This position allows you to slide the client to the floor safely. Move your leg near the client (FIGURE 15.23, B).
    • 4 Lower the client to the floor, letting them slide down your leg. Bend at your hips and knees as you lower the client (FIGURE 15.23, C). Do not bend from the waist.
      • This helps prevent injury to you or the client.
    • 5 After the fall:
      • a If you are in a facility, call for a nurse. Stay with the client, and do not move.
    • b If you are in a home care setting, check for obvious signs of injury. If there are obvious signs of injury, do not move the client; call 9-1-1, and then call your supervisor. Keep the client warm and calm until help arrives.
    • c The nurse will then assess if any injury has occurred to the client or to you.
    • d Many community agencies have policies against individual caregivers’ helping clients off the floor unless the clients have the mobility to do so on their own.
    • Policies exist to prevent injuries or to reduce their severity.

    Report and Record your actions and observations, according to employer policy. Include the following:

    • □ How the fall occurred
    • □ How far the client walked
    • □ How activity was tolerated before the fall
    • □ Any complaints before the fall
    • □ The amount of assistance needed by the client while walking
    • Actions, observations, and basic assessments should be recorded and reported for legal reasons and to keep the rest of the health care team informed.

    Falls

    A client may start to fall when standing or walking. If the client is weak, light-headed, or dizzy, fainting may occur. Falling may be caused by slipping or sliding on spills, waxed floors, or throw rugs or by wearing improper shoes (see Chapter 20).

    When a client is falling, the support worker’s instinct is to try to prevent the fall. However, trying to prevent a fall could cause greater harm. You could injure yourself and the client as you twist and strain to stop the fall. Or you could lose your balance, and both you and the client could fall, causing head, hip, and knee injuries to occur.

    Instead, if a client starts to fall, ease the client to the floor. By doing so, you control the direction of the fall and also protect the client’s head (see FIGURE 15.23 on page 274).

    Your response after the fall will depend on whether you work in a facility with help readily available or in a private home, where you will likely be working alone (see the Focus on Home Care: When a Client Falls box). If a client falls in a facility, do not move the client or allow the client to get up. Remain calm, and reassure the client. Stay with the client and do not move the client. Call for the charge nurse to check the client for injuries, and, later, complete an incident report, as required by facility policy.

    Focus on Home Care

    When a Client Falls

    When working in a private home, you will not have a nurse present to help you if a client falls. Be aware of any procedures required by your employer in the case of a fall. Use the following as a guide.

    If a client starts to fall, ease them to the floor (see the procedure Helping the Falling Client, below.). After the fall, check for the following signs:

    1. • Pain or tenderness
    2. • Swelling or bruising
    3. • Inability to move a limb or difficulty moving a limb
    4. • Bleeding
    5. • Client report of having felt or heard a bone snap or pop

    If you observe any of the above, or if you suspect the client has a head, neck, or back injury, do not move the client . Call 9-1-1 and then your supervisor for help. Keep the client warm and calm, and stay with the client until help arrives.

    If the client is unhurt and is able to assist, help them up. Follow these steps:

    1. • Place a chair beside the client. Have the client use the armrests for support.
    2. • Support the client’s hips and assist the client into a kneeling position, facing the chair.
    3. • Have the client rest their forearms on the chair.
    4. • Tell the client to lift one knee and place their foot on the floor. Assist as required.
    5. • On the count of three, help the client push up, stand, and pivot into the chair, while holding on to the armrests.
    6. • Once the client is in the chair, let them rest before assisting them up again.

    Report the fall to your supervisor. Complete an incident report, according to employer policy.

    Walking Aids

    Walking aids support the body and are ordered by the physician, nurse, or physiotherapist. The type ordered depends on the client’s physical condition, the amount of support needed, and the type of disability.

    Crutches

    Crutches are used when the client cannot use one leg or when one or both legs need to gain strength. Some clients with permanent leg weakness use crutches, usually Lofstrand crutches (FIGURE 15.24), which are made of metal. A metal band fits around the forearm. Axillary crutches, made of wood or metal, extend from the underarm (axilla) to the ground (FIGURE 15.25). A regulated health care provider teaches the client to use crutches safely.

    A client who needs crutches must learn to walk, climb upstairs and climb downstairs, and sit and stand using the crutches. Safety is important, as the client using crutches is at risk for falls. Follow these safety measures:

    1. • Check the crutch tips. Report worn or torn crutch tips to your supervisor, and replace them according to policy. If the tips are wet, dry them with a towel or paper towels.
    2. • Check crutches for flaws—wooden crutches for cracks and metal crutches for bends. All bolts must be tight.
    3. • Make sure the client wears well-fitted, flat, nonskid street shoes.

    FIGURE 15.25 Axillary crutches. iStockphoto/tirc83. 5. • Ensure the client’s clothing fits well. Loose clothing may get caught between the crutches and underarms. Loose clothing can also hang forward and block the person’s view of the feet and crutch tips. 6. • Practise safety measures to prevent falls (see Chapter 20). 7. • Keep crutches within reach of the client when not in use.

    Canes

    Canes are used to help support a side of the body that is unsteady or weak, and to provide balance. Single-tip and four-point (quad) canes (FIGURE 15.26) are available. A cane is held on the strong (unaffected) side of the body. (For example, if the left leg is weak, the cane is held in the right hand.) Four-point canes give more support than single-tip canes do but are harder to move.

    The cane tip should be placed about 15 to 25 cm (6 to 10 in.) to the side of the foot and about 15 to 25 cm (6 to 10 in.) in front of the foot on the unaffected side. The grip is level with the hip.

    The person walks as follows:

      1. The cane is moved forward 15 to 25 cm (6 to 10 in.) (FIGURE 15.27, A).
      1. The affected leg (opposite the cane) is moved forward even with the cane (FIGURE 15.27, B).
      1. The unaffected leg is brought forward and ahead of the cane and the affected leg (FIGURE 15.27, C).

    Walkers

    A walker is a four-point walking aid (FIGURE 15.28). It gives more support than a cane does. Most people feel safer and more secure with a walker than with a cane. There are different kinds of walkers. The standard walker is picked up and moved about 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in.) in front of the client. The client then moves the affected leg and foot and then the unaffected leg and foot up to the walker (FIGURE 15.29, A and B).

    A wheeled walker (also called a rollator) has wheels on the front legs and rubber tips on the back legs (FIGURE 15.30 on page 278). The client pushes the walker ahead about 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 in.) and then walks up to it. The rubber tips on the back legs prevent the walker from moving while the client is walking or standing. There may be a padded seat in the front which allows the client to sit and rest when required.

    Baskets, pouches, and trays can be attached to walkers (see FIGURE 15.28). The attachment is used to carry needed items. These help the client to be more independent and not have to rely on others. The attachment also keeps the hands free to grip the walker.

    The client uses the walker for support when moving from a standing to a sitting position. Assist clients with walkers, as needed (BOX 15.4).

    Braces

    A brace (orthotic) is an apparatus—metal, plastic, or leather—worn to support or align affected body parts or to prevent or correct problems with the musculoskeletal system (see Chapters 32 and 33). A brace is applied over the ankle, knee, or back (FIGURE 15.31). An ankle–foot orthosis (AFO) is positioned in the shoe (FIGURE 15.32); then the foot is inserted, and the device is secured in place with a Velcro strap.

    Skin and bony points under the braces should be kept clean and dry to prevent skin breakdown. Report redness or signs of skin breakdown (see Chapter 22) and any complaints of pain or discomfort. In facilities, the nurse assesses the skin under braces during every shift. The care plan tells you when to apply and remove a brace.

    BOX 15.4 Helping Clients With Walkers to Sit and Stand

    Helping the Client Sit on a Chair, Wheelchair, or Toilet

    1. • Lock the wheels of the wheelchair.
    2. • Ask the client to stand with their back to the chair, wheelchair, or toilet.
    3. • Ask the client to back up with the walker until their knees touch the seat.
    4. • Ask the client to take one hand off the walker and to use that hand to reach and grasp the armrest or grab bar.
    5. • Help the client slowly sit down. The buttocks should be at the back of the seat.
    6. • If armrests or grab bars are not available, support the client as the client sits down. (Use a transfer belt if the client is weak or unsteady.)
    7. • Move the walker away from the client.
    8. • Support the client in the standing position.
    9. • Place your hands under the client’s arms and over the shoulder blades.
    10. • Block the client’s feet and knees with your feet and knees.
    11. • Lower the client onto the seat slowly.

    Helping the Client Sit on a Bed

    1. • Use a transfer belt if the client is weak or unsteady.
    2. • Raise the head of the bed to a sitting position.
    3. • Ask the client to stand with their back to the bed. The client should be near the middle of the bed.
    4. • Ask the client to back up with the walker until their knees touch the bed.
    5. • Ask the client to reach for the bed with one hand. Assist as necessary.
    6. • Help the client sit down slowly.
    7. • Provide for comfort.

    Helping the Client Move From Sitting to Standing

    1. • Place the walker where the client can reach it with ease.
    2. • Let the client position the walker.
    3. • Ask the client to move to the edge of the seat or bed.
    4. • Help the client rise to a standing position, as needed.
    5. • Do not let the client pull themselves up with the walker. They should push on the mattress or chair armrests and then transfer their hands to the walker.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • Being active is important for physical and mental well-being. Inactivity, whether mild or severe, affects not only the normal functions of all body systems but also the client’s mental sense of well-being.

    • Bed rest is ordered by a physician to reduce physical activity, reduce pain, encourage rest, regain strength, promote healing, or a combination of these.

    • Complications of bed rest are contractures, atrophy, orthostatic hypotension, and syncope.

    • A support worker must keep in mind important safety measures when moving a client from a lying position to sitting, from sitting to standing, and from standing to walking, and while using walking aids.

    • Your role as a support worker may include providing range-of-motion exercises to your client to reduce the effects of bed rest and to maintain or improve the mobility of joints.

    • The care plan and the supervisor or physiotherapist will give you instructions for the exercises.

    • If your client starts to fall while walking, you must protect your client and yourself from injury.

    • Walking aids include crutches, canes, walkers, and wheeled walkers, and as a support worker, you will need to know the purpose of each aid. Your client may tell you about any difficulties with the aid. Do not adjust any walking aids without direction from your supervisor.

    Objectives

    Key Terms

    prostheses

    (singular, prosthesis) Artificial replacements for missing body parts. p. 289

    reablement program

    A program designed for individuals who do not need acute care but cannot yet manage at home or are struggling in their home to regain strength, endurance, functioning, and independence.     rehabilitative care

    The process of restoring a person to the highest possible level of functioning through the use of therapy, exercise, and other methods to maintain or slow the decline of functioning in order to maintain the client’s quality of life and independence. p. 283

    rehabilitation robotics

    Robotics and technology used in rehabilitation medicine that include wheelchair biomechanics, new technologies to assess spinal structure and function, assistive robotics, and the use of virtual reality. p. 285

    restorative care

    Preventing health deterioration, maintaining or improving a client’s quality of life, and restoring the client to their previous functional level as much as possible. p. 283

    virtual reality

    The use of computer technology to create a simulated image or simulated environment. p. 285

    Injury or health challenges can affect a person’s ability to lead a productive life. For example, after a stroke, some people are unable to walk or to communicate like they once could. Those clients may benefit from rehabilitation care and restorative care services. People may require rehabilitative and restorative care following an acute injury or illness, or it may be part of the treatment for a lifelong disability, chronic condition or illness, or degenerative condition. Rehabilitation refers to a broad range of interventions that address the client’s medical care needs, therapeutic needs, psychosocial needs, or a combination of these needs and would be part of a client’s care plan. It is delivered in homes, community-based locations, long-term care homes, and hospitals.

    Rehabilitative Care Versus Restorative Care

    Rehabilitative care is the process of restoring a person to the highest possible level of functioning through the use of therapy, exercise, and other methods. The aim of such care is to slow the decline of functioning or support clients in maintaining or functioning at a higher capacity to maintain their quality of life and independence. For some clients, the goal is to return to work, while some others may work toward resuming the self-care needs and perform the activities of daily living (ADLs) that they were able to perform in the past. This type of care is usually provided by nurses, physiotherapists, physiotherapy assistants, and support workers.1

    Restorative care is also aimed at preventing health deterioration and maintaining or improving a client’s quality of life, but it is also aimed at restoring the client to their previous functional level whenever possible. Whereas more health care providers are on the team for a client in rehabilitative care, restorative care usually involves only the client and family, nurses, and support workers. Therapists and other professionals may be consulted as needed.

    Goals of Rehabilitative and Restorative Care

    Most clients largely want to reduce their reliance on others and to become more independent. The goals of rehabilitation and restorative care for any client are based on the client’s condition, and may include the following:

    1. • To restore function to former levels. A full restoration of function is the goal for most clients with acute or temporary conditions. For example, a healthy client with a simple leg fracture aims for full recovery.
    2. • To improve functional abilities. A return to former levels of functioning is not possible for some clients. Instead, they aim to improve their abilities to the best extent possible. For example, they may want to work on mobility skills or carrying out ADLs independently.
    3. • To learn new skills. Some clients need to learn new skills to adjust to their disability or limitations. For example, Mr. Liptak’s larynx was surgically removed because of cancer, and he is not able to speak anymore. His goal is to learn to communicate using, for example, sign language.
    4. • To prevent further disability and illness. Complications that cause further disability and illness must be prevented. For example, immobility can lead to pressure ulcers or contractures, so proper skin care, exercise and activity, good alignment, and frequent repositioning are essential.

    Consider the rehabilitation goals of the following clients:

    Focus on Older Persons

    Rehabilitation

    Rehabilitation often takes longer for older clients than for others. Age-related changes affect healing, mobility, vision, hearing, and other functions (see Chapters 33 and 43). Older clients often have chronic health problems that slow down their recovery. They are also at risk for injuries. Because older clients do not tolerate long or fast-paced programs, their rehabilitation is usually slow.

    For clients who are too weak to perform ADLs, restorative care may involve measures that promote:

    1. • Self-care
    2. • Elimination
    3. • Positioning
    4. • Mobility
    5. • Communication
    6. • Cognitive function
    7. • Preventing unnecessary decline in function

    Emphasis on the Whole Person

    Illness and disability can affect a client’s physical, emotional, social, intellectual, and spiritual health, so restorative care is aimed at the whole person. It addresses all dimensions of health, not just the physical one.

    1. • Physical health. The client’s physical condition and ability to perform ADLs that were assessed by professionals during the rehabilitative process continue to be monitored by the client, the family, and all caregivers. Complications are prevented. ROM exercises are done. The client learns or relearns self-care and mobility skills.

    Clients who are undergoing rehabilitation and restoration of joint and muscle strength and function are often in pain. An important aspect of rehabilitation is learning to cope with the physical pain without misusing or becoming addicted to opioid pain relievers or other substances, such as alcohol or nonprescription drugs.

    1. • Emotional and social health. Emotional and social health can be assessed by professionals. Family counselling and assistance for re-entering the community and workplace are provided as needed.
    2. • Intellectual health. Thinking, speech, memory, and organization are assessed. Measures to improve cognitive functioning are planned.
    3. • Spiritual health. Counselling from spiritual advisors is provided as needed.

Some hospitals offer cardiac and respiratory rehabilitation, and some have programs for complex medical and surgical conditions such as wounds and unstable diabetes.     The Rehabilitation Process

    Rehabilitation can be a short process; for example, a healthy client with a shoulder injury may need to do only a few simple exercises regularly for a few weeks until recovery is complete. However, rehabilitation is often a long, difficult process. The Case Study: Rehabilitation Following a Brain Injury box shows the rehabilitation process for a client who has experienced a serious brain injury.

    The Rehabilitation Team

    Rehabilitation requires a multidisciplinary team effort. Members of the team can vary, depending on the client’s needs and the setting where the rehabilitation is being conducted. The team usually includes the client, family members, physicians, nurses, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, physiotherapy assistants, support workers, and others—who all can help the client regain function and independence. The team meets often to discuss the client’s progress, and changes are made in the rehabilitation care plan, as needed. The client and family attend the meetings whenever possible. The importance of family involvement and support throughout the client’s rehabilitation process cannot be overemphasized.

    All aspects of rehabilitation are interconnected. For example, following a stroke, Mr. Silva learns how to care for himself (physical health) and how to follow instructions (intellectual health), which increases his self-esteem (emotional health); family and friends visit often (social health), and his spiritual advisor visits every week (spiritual health).

    The Case Study: Rehabilitation Following a Brain Injury box shows a rehabilitation team addressing all five dimensions of a client’s health. The client’s quality of life is central to care and treatment. The rehabilitation team encourages the client to control as many aspects of care as possible—being a key member of the rehabilitation team gives the client some control.

    Role of the Family

    Rehabilitation of clients also involves their family members, who need to learn about the illness, injury, or disability and how to care for their loved ones. Family support and involvement in the client’s care can play a crucial part of the client’s rehabilitation success. Care often involves helping the client practise new skills. The family also learns new skills—for example, how to communicate with a spouse who has lost the ability to speak, how to cope with the behaviour of a teenager with a mental health disorder, or how to dress a child with physical disabilities. Counselling is provided, as needed, to help the family cope with changes affecting them. Families dealing with the rehabilitation of a member with a chronic health problem will need constant support from team members because the challenges to a normal family life can be overwhelming, and family members need to know that the team is listening to their concerns.

    Therapy and Training

    The professionals on the rehabilitation team choose the therapy and training needed to meet the client’s goals. The client is taught how to improve a skill or how to perform a task and practises what was learned either independently or with a caregiver or a family member. You may be required to help clients practise skills or tasks.

    The client’s therapy and training may involve learning to use self-help devices. Specific equipment to meet the client’s needs is obtained, and the client is taught how to use it:

    1. • Prostheses.  Prostheses are artificial replacements for missing body parts—for example, feet, knees, lower limbs, upper limbs, hands, breasts, and eyes. The goal for a prosthesis is to be like the missing body part in function and appearance. Artificial limbs are made from a variety of materials such as wood, aluminum, and plastic. Most artificial limbs are powered by the client’s muscles, either by muscles in the stump or by other nearby muscles, so a physiotherapist works with the client to strengthen these muscles. The client learns how to use and care for the prosthesis. As a support worker, you might help a client get used to wearing and using a prosthesis. You might also assist the client with care of the skin at the prosthesis site (see Chapter 33).
    2. • Orthotics. An orthotic is an apparatus worn to support and align parts of the musculoskeletal system and prevent or correct problems associated with it. Examples are splints, foot supports, and knee and back braces (see Chapter 15). The client learns how to put on, use, and care for the orthotic. You might help a client put on an orthotic and practise moving or walking.
    3. • Eating and drinking devices. These include glass holders, plate guards, and utensils with curved handles or cuffs (see Chapter 27).
    4. • Self-care devices. These include electronic toothbrushes, combs, brushes, and sponges with long handles (FIGURE 16.2), as well as devices that help with dressing, cooking, writing, making phone calls, and performing other tasks (FIGURE 16.3).

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Assisting With Rehabilitation and Restorative Care

    1. • Dignity. Treat your clients with respect, and protect their right to dignity. Clients do not need your pity, but they do need encouragement and support. Focus on the positive by reminding them of their progress and highlighting their abilities and strengths. Provide emotional support and reassurance; when clients need someone to talk to, be a good listener.
    2. The process of regaining independence is often very slow, and you may grow impatient with your client when repeated explanations seem to have little or no result. Never show your impatience. Try to understand that having little control over one’s own body movements or functions can be extremely frustrating. For many clients, having to learn new tasks is a reminder of their disability. Give praise when even a little progress is made. Remember that illness, disability, and fatigue can affect a client’s ability to learn. Clients with brain injuries may have a particularly hard time learning new activities.
    3. Clients and family members faced with a condition that requires rehabilitation may experience a feeling of loss for the way their life may change. Emotional aspects of rehabilitation are interconnected with physical rehabilitation. Clients and families can experience all or some of these stages: shock, denial, anger, depression, reaction against independence, and acceptance. Clients and their families may not go through these steps at the same time. Reactions will vary, too, depending on the age of your client and the prognosis. Parents told that their child needs rehabilitation may react differently from an older person’s family dealing with rehabilitation. Report your observations to your supervisor.
    4. • Independence. Independence is a key rehabilitation goal. Encourage the client to perform ADLs as independently as possible. Do not rush the client and allow them time to complete each task. Familiarize yourself with the client’s self-help devices and encourage the client to use them whenever necessary. When assisting the client, apply the methods developed by the rehabilitation team and practise with the client the tasks that must be performed. These methods will help you to guide and direct the client.
    5. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. In order to be effective as possible, the rehabilitation and restorative care plan must be adapted to meet the physical and psychosocial needs of each client.
    6. • Preferences. Ask clients about their preferences, and allow for personal choice whenever possible. Freedom of choice helps clients feel in control. Follow the client’s daily routine and care plan.
    7. • Privacy. Some clients may feel embarrassed to practise skills (such as eating and walking) in front of others. Provide privacy for practising skills, as well as for care procedures and elimination. Keep information about clients confidential.
    8. • Safety. Illness, disability, and fatigue increase the risk for accidents and injuries, so take the necessary measures to ensure safety (see Chapter 20). Never force clients to do more than they are able, and allow time for rest. Follow the care plan for safety measures needed for each client. Keep the client in good body alignment. Use safe transfer methods (see Chapter 14). Perform ROM exercises and turn and reposition the client as directed. Report signs and symptoms of complications such as pressure ulcers, contractures, elimination problems, and depression. In facility settings, make sure that the call bell and overbed table are placed on the client’s unaffected side.

    Overall Benefits Of Rehabilitation And Restorative Care

    Rehabilitation and restorative care can be very beneficial to clients and their families. These benefits include slowing a client’s decline of functioning, maintaining or improving functioning, restoring the client to the previous functional level as much as possible, and preserving the client’s quality of life and independence. However, there are a number of benefits that rehabilitation and restorative care has to Canadians. These include the following:

    1. • Shorter or no hospital stays for clients. Rather than stay in an acute-care hospital for weeks or months to receive rehabilitative or restorative care, clients may be able to go home (or to a short-stay facility) that is closer to their community. In some parts of the country, there are reablement programs established to address this. A reablement program is designed for individuals who do not need acute care but cannot yet manage at home or are struggling in their home to regain strength, endurance, functioning, and independence. This option allows them to stay in their homes longer while they receive rehabilitation or restorative care.3 A respite program provides temporary support to individuals living independently or being cared for by a friend or family member.
    2. n In remote regions, health care workers are able to provide instructions and assessments through the use of video conferencing. This allows clients to remain in their own homes whenever possible.
    3. • Independence for clients. Clients who live at home are able to maintain their independence and familiar surroundings more than if hospitalized.
    4. • Less disruption for families. Clients who are able to be discharged home from hospitals that are located in larger centres have the advantage of being closer to their families. Families are therefore able to visit more often and are not required to travel to see their hospitalized loved ones.
    5. • Cost savings. Acute hospital care is very expensive to taxpayers. Money that can be saved delivering quality client care on an outpatient basis can be reallocated for other care.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • Rehabilitation care is the use of therapy, exercise, and other methods to restore a person to the highest possible level of functioning. Rehabilitation may be necessary due to an acute injury or illness (usually short-term), or it may be part of the treatment plan for a persistent illness or disability (often long-term). Rehabilitation can also maintain or slow the decline of functioning.

    • Restorative care is aimed at preventing health deterioration, maintaining or improving a client’s quality of life, as well as restoring the client to their previous functional level as much as possible. Restorative care is client care that is usually provided by nurses and support workers.

    • Every client will have a different care plan and different team members involved, depending on the goals. As a support worker, you may be the person who has the most ongoing contact with your client, so your input into care conferences and your reports to your supervisor are vital.

    • Therapy and rehabilitation are important to help clients reach their goal of independence. The care plan will establish your role in each step of rehabilitation.

    • Family members are important members of the rehabilitation team. They need to learn about the illness, injury, or disability and how to care for their loved ones.

    • A number of psychosocial elements could affect clients’ reactions to their conditions.

    Personal hygiene promotes comfort, safety, and health. It includes activities to clean the skin, teeth, mouth, genital area, and anus. Good hygiene helps keep teeth, skin, and mucous membranes intact and healthy. When microbes enter the body through breaks in skin or mucous membranes, infections can result. Therefore, intact skin and mucous membranes are the body’s first line of defence against disease. As well as keeping the body clean and healthy, personal hygiene prevents body and breath odours, promotes relaxation, and increases circulation.

    Support workers often assist clients with personal hygiene. Some clients need only minimal assistance, whereas some others may need hygiene care done for them. Illness, disability, and changes associated with aging affect a person’s ability to practise hygiene for themselves. Many factors complicate hygiene and skin care, such as perspiration, elimination, vomiting, drainage from wounds or body openings, bed rest, and activity.

    As well, culture and personal choice affect hygiene practices (see the Respecting Diversity: Personal Hygiene Practices of Different Cultures box). Most clients have established hygiene routines—for example, a bath at bedtime or in the morning, brushing their teeth and washing their face and hands on awakening, and so on. Usually, these and other hygiene measures are done before and after meals and at bedtime. The care plan lists what personal hygiene measures are needed for a client and when to provide them.     Daily Care

    Facilities provide routine hygiene care at set times of the day. The amount of care given at each time and the terminology used vary from facility to facility, and each facility must adjust its procedures to address the various cultural needs of clients. The care plan will give you directions.

    3. • HS care (evening care or PM care)—care provided at bedtime. (HS stands for “hour of sleep.”) HS care is aimed at relaxing the client and increasing comfort. It includes face washing, handwashing (see Chapter 19), oral hygiene, perineal care, back massages, and assistance with going to the bathroom. Bed linens and units are made neat and comfortable, and the client is helped into sleepwear.

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Assisting With Personal Hygiene

    1. • Dignity. Hygiene is a very personal and private matter. Most clients feel embarrassed that someone else needs to provide hygiene care for them, more so when the caregiver is of the opposite sex. Remember, personal hygiene is necessary for the client’s health and well-being. Never show embarrassment or hesitation when assisting with hygiene procedures, as the client is likely to sense your discomfort. Talk to the client before and during the procedure. Let the client know what you will be doing. Listen to what the client has to say about the procedure, and address any concerns or questions. If appropriate, chat with the client about anything of interest. Your calm, professional manner will help put the client at ease. Minimizing exposure of the body during bathing promotes dignity.
    2. • Independence. Allow and encourage clients to do as much self-care as possible. Sometimes, you only need to assist with a task. Give clients enough time to do what they can do independently. Check with your supervisor and the care plan to determine clients’ abilities and limitations.
    3. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. Clients should never be forced to receive personal care. If a client is not cooperative with their personal hygiene, gentle persuasive techniques such as re-approaching and redirecting should be attempted (see Dealing With Bathing Challenges on page 324 and Chapter 38).
    4. • Preferences. Clients usually have hygiene preferences and routines. Routines provide a sense of order and control. Clients need to have a say about when and how a hygiene procedure is done. Personal choice is allowed in such matters as bath times and products used. The care plan should reflect the client’s preferences and cultural practices. If there is a conflict between the care plan and the client’s wishes, inform your supervisor.
    5. • Privacy. Protecting the client’s privacy is very important when assisting with hygiene. All procedures must be done in a private area with minimal exposure of the client’s body. Close doors, privacy curtains, and drapes before giving care. Ask family members or visitors to leave the room when providing hygiene care. If a family member or friend wants to help, the client must consent. Do not unnecessarily expose the client’s body when assisting with the bath. Provide appropriate covers to clients when taking them to and from tubs or shower rooms.
    6. • Safety. Some clients have skin that can be easily damaged. Always be gentle; never scrub the skin or use rough sponges or cloths. Make sure your fingernails are trimmed, and remove rings before giving skin care so as to prevent scratching or tearing the client’s skin. Remember to wash your hands with soap and warm water before and after every procedure. Wear gloves when giving perineal care and oral care and any time you may have contact with blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions. As well, gloves should be worn when you have cuts or rashes on your hands. Always check the water temperature before bathing the client.

    Poor oral hygiene allows the buildup of biofilm. Biofilm—which contains saliva, microbes, and other substances—is a thin film that sticks to teeth and leads to tooth decay or cavities. When biofilm hardens, it is called tartar. Tartar builds up at the gum line near the neck of the tooth, which leads to periodontal disease. Gums become red and swollen and bleed easily. As the disease progresses, bone is destroyed, and teeth become loosened, eventually resulting in tooth loss. The presence of bleeding gums during oral hygiene procedures is usually a sign of inflammation and means more mouth care is needed, not less.

    Illness and disease often cause a bad taste in the mouth. Some medications and diseases cause a whitish coating on the mouth and tongue, and some others cause redness and swelling of the mouth and tongue. Dry mouth is a common result of oxygen administration, smoking, low fluid intake, mouth breathing, and anxiety. Some medications also cause dry mouth.

    Recent research is identifying a relationship between periodontal disease and other diseases. Scientists have found that bacteria that grow in the oral cavity can be aspirated into the lungs and cause respiratory diseases such as pneumonia. Periodontal disease can also increase a person’s risk of heart disease. Some studies have shown that clients with oral disease are at greater risk for thickening of the carotid arteries, which leads to heart disease.

    Your supervisor and the care plan will tell you the type of mouth care and assistance needed for the client. Oral hygiene is given to the client on awakening, after each meal, and at bedtime. Some medications may cause mouth dryness, which in turn, makes swallowing difficult. Some clients practise oral hygiene before meals in order to promote salivation and to improve the taste of their food.

    When giving oral hygiene, you will come in contact with the client’s mucous membranes. The mouth contains many microbes, so you need to wear gloves and follow Standard Practices.

    Equipment

    For oral hygiene, a toothbrush, toothpaste, dental floss, and mouthwash are needed. Ensure that the toothbrush has soft bristles, and replace toothbrushes every 3 months. Use toothpaste that contains fluoride. Clients with dentures need a denture cleaner, a denture cup, and a denture brush or toothbrush. Use only products that are recommended for cleaning dentures to avoid damaging the dentures.

    For clients with sore, tender mouths and for unconscious clients, sponge swabs are used for mouth care. However, be careful to always check that the foam pad is tight on the stick. If the foam pad comes off the stick, the client could choke on it. Lip moisturizers are often used to relieve dry lips.

    Observations

    It is important for you to observe the client’s teeth and gums for signs of early problems, such as the following, and to report them promptly:

    1. • Dry, cracked, swollen, or blistered lips
    2. • Bleeding, swelling, or redness of the gums
    3. • Presence of sores or white patches on the oral mucosa or tongue
    4. • Presence of food or foreign objects on the tongue and in the mouth
    5. • Missing or loose teeth (Remember that these can be a swallowing hazard!)
    6. • Rough, sharp, or chipped areas on dentures
    7. • Foul breath
    8. • Difficulty swallowing or speaking (see Chapters 27 and 35)

    Brushing Teeth

    Most clients can perform oral hygiene procedures themselves, but in long-term care, the majority cannot perform them adequately. Others may need help gathering and setting up equipment. Encourage clients to be as independent as possible according to their abilities and their care plans. In the community, clients may choose to brush teeth in the bathroom, at the kitchen sink, or at the bedside.

    You may have to brush the teeth of clients who are very weak or confused or who cannot use or move their arms. It can be difficult to provide mouth care to clients who are living with dementia, but remember that even a small amount of mouth care is better than none. There is no consensus among dentists as to the proper brushing technique. Some recommend a circular motion, some the up-and-down motion, and others the side-to-side motion. All, however, agree that brushing should start at the gum line (see the Focus on Children: Brushing Teeth box).

    Brushing Teeth

    According to the Canadian Dental Association, parents should consult a health care provider to determine whether their child up to 3 years of age is at risk of developing tooth decay.

    Mouth Care for an Unconscious Client

    Some agencies allow support workers to provide personal care to unconscious clients (see Chapter 47). To prevent aspiration, position the client on one side, with their head turned well to the side (FIGURE 17.4) so that excess fluid runs out of the mouth, and when cleaning the mouth, use only a small amount of fluid.     Denture Care

    Dentures are artificial teeth. A client may have a complete set of dentures or a partial set (FIGURE 17.6). Mouth care is given and dentures are cleaned as often as natural teeth are. Dentures are very costly, so handle them carefully. They are slippery when wet and break or chip easily if dropped onto a hard surface (floor, sink).

    Denture cleaning agents will include the manufacturer’s instructions on how to use them and the water temperature required. Ensure that the dentures are soaked for the recommended time, as damage can occur if dentures are soaked in the cleansing agent for longer than recommended. Also, very hot water causes warping.

    Depending on where you are employed, storage of dentures that are not going to be worn after cleaning varies. Some dentists and denturists recommend dentures be stored in a container and covered with water. If not covered with water, they can warp. Other dentists and denturists recommend storing them dry. Some clients remove their dentures at bedtime. Some clients do not wear their dentures all the time; they may wear them for eating and remove them after meals. Some agencies recommend soaking with the use of denture-cleaning soaking tablets, while other agencies do not permit the use of such tablets. It is important to follow agency policies at all times with regard to cleaning dentures.

    Generally, clients do not like being seen without their dentures and remove them only for cleaning. After you have cleaned the dentures, return them to the client as quickly as possible. Provide privacy for clients who clean their own dentures.

    Bathing

    Bathing helps keep skin as well as the genital and anal areas clean by removing microbes, dead skin, perspiration, and excess oils. A bath is refreshing and relaxing, stimulates circulation, and exercises body parts. During baths, you are able to make important observations about clients, and you have time to get to know them.

    A client may require a complete bath or a partial bed bath, a tub bath, or a shower, depending on the client’s condition, self-care abilities, and personal choice. In facilities, bathing usually occurs after breakfast or the evening meal. The client’s choice of bath time should be respected whenever possible.

    Bathing frequency is a matter of personal choice or employer policy. For example, some clients who live in their own homes may prefer to bathe daily, but in long-term care facilities, legislation may call for clients to have a complete bath once or twice a week and a partial bath daily and as needed. Besides personal choice, bathing frequency is affected by weather, physical activity, and illness. Bathing may need to be more frequent because of increased perspiration caused by high environmental temperatures or a fever. Some illnesses and dry skin may limit bathing to once every 2 or 3 days. Note that, in some cultures, only a family member is allowed to bathe a person.

    Bathwater that is too hot can scald a frail, thin person. Many agencies, therefore, require two people to check each client’s bathwater temperature using a digital thermometer and then record the temperature in the client’s chart.

    Guidelines to observe when assisting with bed baths, showers, and tub baths are listed in BOX 17.1. TABLE 17.1, on page 312, describes common skin care products (see the Focus on Older Persons: Bathing box).

    Focus on Older Persons

    Bathing

    Aging and soap cause dry skin, which is easily damaged. Therefore, older persons usually need a complete bath or shower only two times a week and partial baths on other days. Many facilities no longer allow clients to be bathed using soap, as it may be too drying for their skin. If your facility does use soap, ensure thorough rinsing of the client’s skin afterward. Bar soaps should always be rinsed and placed where the bar can dry. Some older clients may bathe daily but not always use soap. Others may find that bath gels are easier to use than soaps.

    Bath oils should never be added to tub baths, as they can increase the risk of a client slipping and falling. As well, the oil can cause irritation in the delicate mucous membranes of the urethra and vagina.

    Special skin-cleansing products that do not require rinsing are now available. Follow the manufacturers’ guidelines for use. Lotions applied after a bath can help keep skin soft and moisturized.

    The Complete Bed Bath

    The complete bed bath involves washing the entire body while the client is still in bed. It is given to clients who cannot bathe themselves—clients who are unconscious, paralyzed, in casts or traction, or weak from illness or surgery.

    Ask your supervisor about the client’s ability to assist with the bath and about any activity or position limitations. Remember to follow Standard Practices. Also, remember to allow the client to make choices during the bath.

    A bed bath is a new experience for many clients. Some may be embarrassed that another person can see their body, and some fear exposure and loss of privacy. Explain to the client how a bed bath is given and that you will cover the person’s body for privacy. The bed bath procedure below applies to adults. Follow the adult procedure for bathing toddlers and older children. See Chapter 41 for bathing infants. Infants, young children, and older people have fragile skin, so lower water temperatures must be used for them. Ask your supervisor or check the care plan for what water temperature to use. Always remember to test the bathwater before bathing a client.

    Towel Baths

    For a towel bath, usually an oversized towel is used, to cover the client’s body from neck to feet. The bath towel is saturated with a cleaning solution, which contains water, a cleaning agent, and a skin-softening agent. It also has a drying agent so that the client’s body dries quickly. The towel bath is quick, soothing, and relaxing. Clients who live with dementia often accept this type of bath well. Your supervisor and the care plan will tell you when to use the towel bath. Follow employer policies and procedures.

    Bag Baths

    Bag baths are prepared commercially or by the employer. Eight to ten washcloths moistened with a cleaning agent that does not require rinsing are provided in a plastic bag. Before use, the washcloths are warmed in the microwave. Check with your supervisor and manufacturer’s instructions for the microwave setting. A new washcloth is used for each body part, and the skin is air-dried, so towels are not needed.

    Tub Baths and Showers

    Some clients prefer tub baths, and others prefer showers. In both cases, falls and burns from hot water are risks, so ensuring safety is important (see the Think About Safety: Safety Guidelines for Assisting Clients During Tub Baths and Showers box). You must prevent slipping, falls, chills, and burns. Give a tub bath or shower only if it is specified in the care plan. Check with your supervisor and the care plan for special instructions.

    Tub Baths

    Many clients find tub baths relaxing, but they can cause a client to feel faint, weak, or tired. These risks are greater for clients who are on bed rest, so a bath should last no longer than 20 minutes.

    In a facility, you will need to reserve the tub room for the client. Clean the tub before and after use to prevent the spread of microbes and infection, applying your knowledge of the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System (WHMIS).

    Think About Safety

    Safety Guidelines for Assisting Clients During Tub Baths and Showers

    1. • Most agencies have policies that require clients who are being bathed in a tub to be observed at all times. Ensure that all equipment is collected before starting the client’s tub bath.
    2. • Know the right water temperature. Check with your supervisor and the care plan. Usually, the optimal temperature is 40.5°C (105°F). Because of the risk for scalding injuries if the water is too hot, many agencies require two people to check each client’s bathwater temperature using a digital thermometer and then record the bath or shower water temperature in the client’s chart.
    3. • Clean the tub or shower before and after use, as per WHMIS. This prevents the spread of microbes and infection.
    4. • Dry the bathroom or shower room floor.
    5. • Check hand rails, grab bars, hydraulic lifts, and other safety aids. They must be in working order.
    6. • Place a bath mat in the tub or on the shower floor. The mat is not needed if there are nonskid strips or there is a nonskid surface.
    7. • Cover the client for warmth and privacy during transport to and from the shower or tub room.
    8. • Clients who are undressed can easily become chilled. Keep the client covered and warm whenever they are not in the tub or shower.
    9. • Place needed items within the client’s reach.
    10. • Place the call bell (in facilities) within the client’s reach. Show the client how to use the call bell in the shower or tub room.

    Dealing With Bathing Challenges

    Challenges can arise when bathing a client. For example, the client may refuse the bath or be frightened by bathing procedures. The client may urinate or defecate during the procedure. You need to be prepared for these and other challenges.

    The Client Refuses the Bath

    As with all procedures, the client has the right to refuse a bath. You cannot perform a bathing procedure without the client’s informed consent. Sometimes, people feel too ill or too weak to bathe. Some are afraid of falling or getting chilled, and some may just be embarrassed. Listen carefully to the client to learn how you can provide reassurance. Did the client get cold during the last bath? If so, offer to raise the room temperature before you start. If the bathing area has a heat lamp, use it to provide extra warmth during bathing. Does the client think a bath is not needed? If so, remind the client that bathing promotes hygiene, comfort, and relaxation. If the client refuses to be bathed, re-approach the client at a later time to offer the bath. If the client continues to refuse the bath, talk to your supervisor. Do not bathe clients against their wishes.

    The Client Living With Dementia

    Clients who live with dementia are often frightened by bathing procedures. They do not understand what is happening or why and, therefore, may fear harm or danger. Some clients who live with dementia may develop a fear of being bathed or undressed. As a result, they may resist care, become agitated and combative, or shout at you and cry for help.

    The guidelines in BOX 17.1 on page 325 apply when you are bathing clients with dementia. The client’s care plan lists measures to help the client through the bathing procedure. Such measures may include the following:

    1. • Not rushing the client
    2. • Using a calm, pleasant voice
    3. • Diverting the client’s attention (see Chapter 38)
    4. • Calming the client and trying the bath again later

    The Client Cannot Tolerate the Bathing Position

    Some clients cannot sit or lie on their backs or sides for long periods because of disease, injury, surgery, or shortness of breath. Other clients cannot tolerate having their legs repositioned in order to perform perineal care on them. You must adapt the procedure to meet the client’s needs. For example, you may have to adapt your technique to accommodate whatever position the client finds most comfortable, scale down to a partial bath, or ask for help so that the client can be bathed quickly. Follow your supervisor’s directions and the care plan.

    The Back Massage

    The back massage (back rub) relaxes muscles and stimulates circulation. A massage is normally given after the bath and before bedtime. It should last 3 to 5 minutes. Observe the client’s skin before giving the massage. Look for breaks, tears, bruises, reddened areas, and other signs of skin breakdown.

    The prone position is best for a massage. Older persons or people with a disability may find the side-lying position more comfortable. Use firm strokes, and always keep your hands in contact with the client’s skin. After the massage, apply some lotion to the client’s elbows, knees, and heels to keep the skin in those areas soft. These bony areas are most at risk for skin breakdown. Report any reddened areas or signs of skin breakdown at once.

    Back massages as described in this procedure can be dangerous to some clients—for example, those with certain heart diseases, back injuries, back surgeries, skin diseases, and some lung disorders. Check with the care plan and your supervisor before giving back massages.

    Perineal Care

    Perineal care (pericare) involves cleaning the genital and anal areas, which provide a warm, moist, and dark place for microbes to grow. Cleaning prevents infection, odours, and skin breakdown and promotes comfort.

    Perineal care is done at least once every day during the bath. The procedure is also done whenever the area is soiled with urine or stool. Clients with certain disorders need perineal care more often. Your supervisor and the care plan tell you when the client needs perineal care.

    Clients do their own perineal care if they can. Some may need assistance with perineal care. Clients may not know the terms perineum and perineal. Most understand terms such as privates, private parts, crotch, genitals, or the area between the legs. Use terms the client understands, but avoid slang terms or swear words. The terms must always be in good taste professionally.

    While providing care, you may observe a white or yellow substance in the perineal area, which is called smegma. Smegma is a white or yellow substance that naturally occurs as an accumulation of dead skin cells and oily secretions around the foreskin or clitoris. Smegma acts as a lubricant during sexual intercourse. Smegma production begins after birth but becomes most prominent in adolescence (age 14 onward) when it becomes required for sexual function. Smegma production begins to reduce in middle age and continues to decline as the client ages. If allowed to accumulate, smegma can cause a foul odour and other health issues, such a burning, itching, and irritation. Smegma can be controlled easily by thorough proper perineal care on a routine basis.3

    Follow Standard Practices and medical asepsis when providing perineal care. Wear gloves and use the correct product according to employer policy, such as peri-wipes or specially coloured cloths, washcloths, towel wipes, or swabs. Rinse the area thoroughly. Peri-wash does not have to be rinsed unless visible soiling is present.

    When providing perineal care, you should work as follows:

    1. • from the cleanest area to the dirtiest.
    2. • from the front of the client to the back, or anal area.
    3. • from the top to the bottom, since water drips down and brings germs with it.
    4. • from the area farthest away from you, to the area closest to you.

    The urethral area is the cleanest and the anal area the dirtiest, so clean from the urethra to the anal area. Clean around the perineum. The perineal area is very delicate and easily injured. Use warm water, not hot water. Test the water temperature, according to employer policy. It is important to expose only the client’s perineal area in order to maintain the client’s privacy and to reduce the risk of chilling.

    When cleaning the perineum of a client with female genitalia, use your non-dominant hand to hold the client’s labia open as far as possible. Do not move that hand between wipes. With your first stroke, first clean down the far labia. The second stroke should then wipe the closest labia. The third stroke would then wipe down the centre of the perineum. Use a different wipe or corner of the washcloth (or perineal cloth) with each wipe to prevent contamination of the already cleaned area. Pat dry after rinsing to reduce moisture and to promote comfort.

    When washing clients who have soiled themselves, wash the buttocks area first and then the anal area. Some clients who live with dementia may put their hands into their briefs or put their hands in the area you are cleaning. Wash their hands first and give them something—for example, a washcloth or a towel—to hang on to while you clean the area.

    Report and record the following if observed when providing perineal care:

    1. • Odours
    2. • Redness, swelling, discharge, or irritation
    3. • Complaints of pain, burning, or other discomfort
    4. • Signs of urinary or fecal incontinence (see Chapters 29 and 30)
    5. • Presence of hemorrhoids
    6. • A client’s foreskin not retracting

    Menstrual Care

    Menstruation is a client’s monthly bleeding, when blood flows from the uterus through the vaginal opening (see Chapters 31 and 32). Sanitary pads or tampons absorb menstrual blood. Most menstrual periods last 3 to 5 days. Some clients need assistance with menstrual care, as they may be too ill or weak to change their sanitary pads.

    It is usually beyond the scope of practice for a support worker to insert or remove a tampon in a menstruating client. It may be more common for a support worker to assist a client who requires a disposable sanitary pad. Disposable sanitary pads have adhesive strips that hold them in place in the client’s panties. In some hospitals, menstruating clients may use disposable sanitary pads that are held in place with reusable mesh panties. Some sanitary pads containing perfumes can irritate the skin and should be avoided.

    Sanitary pads should be changed often. This promotes good hygiene and comfort and prevents odours and infection. Follow the care plan for when to change the client’s sanitary pad. Frequency depends on the amount of menstrual flow. Usually, the pads need to be changed at least every 3 to 5 hours. However, they should be changed before they become soaked with blood.

    Contact with the client’s blood and mucous membranes is likely, so always wear gloves when removing or applying sanitary pads. Wash your hands, and help the client wash their hands after menstrual care. Dispose of sanitary pads according to employer policy. Also, follow employer policy for disposing of soiled mesh panties.

    Record and report the following if observed while providing menstrual care:

    1. • Odours
    2. • Fever
    3. • Redness, swelling, or irritation in the perineum
    4. • Complaints of pain, burning, difficulty urinating, or other discomforts
    5. • Heavy bleeding—for example, the sanitary pad becomes soaked within 1 hour of application (report this at once)
    6. • Large number of blood clots on the pad (report this at once)

    Personal grooming—which involves having clean hair, nails, and clothes—is important to all people, whatever their nationality or culture. Good grooming not only helps promote emotional well-being and self-confidence but also helps prevent infection and increase comfort.

    Clients’ degree of attention to their own grooming varies. Some are satisfied if their hair is just clean, whereas some others are very particular about the way it is washed and styled. Some clients may be content to have their hands just cleaned, while others may want their nails to be cleaned, manicured, and polished as well. With regard to body hair, shaving and beard grooming are important to most clients.

    When you provide grooming care, keep in mind that it should be done in a considerate manner, respecting the client’s dignity and right to privacy. Most workers understand that certain acts of grooming (such as shaving legs or underarms) need to be done in private, but they neglect to provide the same privacy when shaving a client’s face.

    Hair Care

    How one’s hair looks and feels can affect a person’s emotional well-being. People generally feel better about themselves when they are well groomed. Unfortunately, illness and disability can interfere with hair care, especially when clients are too ill to care for their hair themselves. It is the support worker’s responsibility to assist with hair care (or any grooming for that matter) whenever needed, as stated on the client’s care plan.

    The care plan should address the individual client’s hair care needs. Culture, personal choice, skin and scalp condition, physical and mental health, and self-care abilities should always be taken into consideration for providing hair care. Related information should also be indicated on the client’s care plan. Some common terms related to hair that may be used in care plans include the following:

    1. • Alopecia—hair loss, which may be complete or partial. Male-pattern baldness occurs due to aging or heredity. In women, hair thins with aging. Cancer treatments (radiation therapy to the head and chemotherapy) often cause alopecia in clients. Other causes include skin disease, stress, poor nutrition, pregnancy, some medications, and hormone changes. In many cases (but not all), the hair grows back once these issues have been resolved.
    2. • Hirsutism (from Latin hirsutus, meaning “shaggy, hairy”)—excessive and increased hair growth in women in locations where the occurrence of visible, coarse hair is normally minimal or absent (such as on the face or neck). It refers to a male pattern of body hair and is, therefore, usually a cosmetic and psychological concern. However, hirsutism may be a sign of a more serious medical problem, especially if it develops well after puberty.
    3. • Dandruff—excessive amount of dry, white flakes on the scalp, in the hair, or on the shoulders. It often causes itching of the scalp and sometimes affects the eyebrows and ear canals as well. Use of medicated shampoos usually can solve the problem. Always ensure that the hair is sufficiently rinsed when shampooing, regardless of the type of shampoo used.
    4. • Pediculosis—infestation with lice, which are small, insectlike parasites that live on the human body, most commonly on skin, hair, and the genital area. Lice (singular, louse) feed on human blood and lay their eggs on body hair and in clothing (FIGURE 18.1).

    Lice do not cause dangerous infections but may carry pathogens that cause more serious diseases such as trench fever and typhus. Lice tend to be a problem found primarily in overcrowded areas or places that have inadequate bathing and laundry facilities, such as among homeless people or in military or refugee camps. All humans are equally at risk for infestation by lice, but older people are more prone to develop complications from it.

    Lice are parasites, organisms that derive their nutrients from host organisms. Their bites cause severe itching in the affected body area. Pediculosis capitis is the infestation of the scalp (capitis) with lice, and pediculosis pubis is the infestation of the pubic (pubis) hair with lice. Both head lice and pubic lice attach their eggs to hair shafts. Pediculosis corporis is the infestation of the body (corporis) surfaces with lice.

    Lice eggs (nits) attach to clothing and furniture and can easily spread to other people through clothing, furniture, bed linen, and physical contact, as well as by the sharing of combs and brushes. While they can be difficult to remove, all lice and their eggs must be destroyed with due diligence.

    Lice can be eradicated by using medicated shampoos, lotions, and creams (following the manufacturer’s directions carefully). Several applications of these products as well as manual removal of the nits are usually necessary. In addition, clients (and anyone they have shared items with) will need a thorough tub bath and must have their clothing and linens washed in hot water. Items that cannot be washed should be thoroughly vacuumed and placed in a sealed, airtight bag and stored for several weeks until the lice are destroyed. If you find any signs of lice you need to report these to your supervisor immediately, and follow your employer’s policies.

    Brushing and Combing Hair

    Brushing and combing hair are part of the client’s daily routine and are usually done in the morning (after the bath or shower) and at bedtime. However, as a support worker, you should assist with brushing and combing, as needed, during the day; for example, some clients may want their hair styled before visitors arrive.

    Encourage clients to do their own hair care and assist them as needed. It is your responsibility to perform hair care for clients who cannot do it by themselves. Clients should be allowed to decide how to brush, comb, and style their hair (see the Focus on Children: Grooming Preferences box).

    You should never cut a client’s hair ; only a professional barber or hairstylist should cut the client’s hair. Some facilities have barber shops or hair salons where clients can have their hair shampooed, cut, and styled. Some hairstylists make visits to homes or to long-term care (complex-care) facilities to provide their services.

    Clients who have had chemical treatments (permanents [“perms”] or straightening) may have dry, somewhat brittle hair that is more prone to tangling, so it should be treated differently from hair without such treatment. Chemically treated hair should be brushed gently toward the hair ends, starting at the ends and working up toward the roots with each brush stroke. Chemically treated hair may get flattened during sleep, so it may need to be recombed or “fluffed up” after the client wakes up. You may use a hair pick on hair that has been permed, to reduce the risk of breakage.

    How to Brush or Comb Hair

    Before giving hair care, you should place a towel over the client’s shoulders to protect the client’s clothing.

    For a client who is unable to get out of bed, give hair care before changing the pillowcase. If it has to be done after a linen change, place a towel over the pillow or over the client’s shoulders (if the client is in the sitting position) to collect falling hair, as hair on the client’s clothing or pillowcase is unsightly and, in some situations, poses the risk of being inhaled or swallowed.

    Focus on Children

    Grooming Preferences

    Hairstyles are important to adolescents as well as other school-aged children. Do not make judgements about any child’s hairstyle. Style hair in a manner that pleases both the child and the parents. Take care not to impose your hairstyle preferences on your client.

    When brushing or combing hair, start near the bottom of ends of the hair, and brush or comb to the hair ends (FIGURES 18.2 and 18.3). Slowly work your way up the hair. Handle hair gently to avoid hurting the client or damaging the hair. Always brush slowly, never pulling or tugging at the client’s hair. This could pull out individual hairs at the root follicles and would be very uncomfortable for the client. If you find that the client has matted or tangled hair, discuss with your supervisor what can be done to untangle it. If you are directed to comb or brush through the matting and tangling, take a small section of hair near the ends, and gently comb or brush through to the ends. Work up to the scalp, brushing or combing in small sections, and eventually you will be able to brush or comb from the scalp to the hair ends. Never cut hair to remove tangles.

    Some Examples of Cultural Differences With Respect to Hair

    The hair of Black people is extremely fragile, so you should be very gentle and careful with hair care to avoid unnecessary breakage and hair loss. Always use a wide-tooth comb or pick when combing the hair, and avoid using fine-tooth combs because they tend to snag and pull out curly or kinky hair. If possible, use a good-quality, natural boar brush. Curly or kinky hair needs a lot of moisture, so you need to avoid using drying products such as hairspray, mousse, or holding gels.1

    Black textured hair tends to produce less sebum (oily secretion created by the sebaceous gland) compared with the hair of White people. To add oil to dry hair, use products that contain moisturizers, such as leave-in conditioners and styling lotions.1 Note that products that claim to be created for “curly hair” are not necessarily suitable for Black curly hair.

    To groom any curly, coarse, and dry hair, use a wide-tooth comb, start at the neckline, comb the hair, and work higher, lifting and fluffing hair outward. Continue until you reach the forehead. Wetting the hair or applying a conditioner or petroleum jelly may make combing easier.1

    The client may have certain practices or use special hair care products, and these should become part of the client’s care plan. The client can guide you about using these when you are giving hair care (see the Respecting Diversity: Corn Braids and Respecting Diversity: Dreadlocks boxes).

    Women who wear hijabs need to take special care of their hair to keep it strong and healthy since the hair and scalp may not be regularly exposed to fresh air and vitamin D, absorbed through sunlight. To maintain hair health while respecting the client’s need for privacy and dignity, here are a few tips:2

    1. • Provide privacy for the client at all times. Consult with the client or their family to find out how to ensure that the client’s preferences are respected.
    2. • Do not tie hair up while it is wet. Try to allow it to air-dry prior to pulling it back.
    3. • Try to get sunlight on the hair and scalp for at least a half-hour each week.
    4. • When possible, remove the hijab, underscarf, and hair tie, and run fingers through the hair and scalp to encourage circulation.
    5. • Wash underscarves regularly.
    6. • Trim hair ends regularly to get rid of split ends.
    7. • Avoid tying the hair too tightly to prevent straining the hair roots, which can lead to hair loss.

    Respecting Diversity

    Corn Braids

    Styling hair in corn braids is a common practice among some cultural groups. The braids are left intact even while shampooing. Remember that you should never do or undo braids without the client’s consent, and consent may also be required by your supervisor. Some agencies have permission forms that the client has to sign regarding hair care, so know your agency’s policy.

    Caring for corn braids differs from caring for hair that is braided into a single large braid. If the client has a single braid, the braid should be undone each night, as the braid would cause discomfort if the client were to lie on it.

    Respecting Diversity

    Dreadlocks

    Dreadlocks are coils of hair that are twisted, rubbed, or backcombed together in a specific way. They should not be combed out. Dreadlocks should be washed with a residue-free product; washing frequency depends on the client. If the client perspires considerably, frequent washing will reduce the salt buildup that sweat can produce on the client’s scalp. For clients who do not perspire a lot, hair should be washed about once a week. The client’s care plan will clarify the specifics of hair care.

    Gently massaging the client’s scalp for a few minutes each day will help to release natural oils from the sebaceous glands in the client’s scalp. The client’s care plan will specify the frequency and kind of care that is required.

    Who Can Cut a Client’s Hair?

    You should never cut a client’s hair unless you have been specifically instructed to do so. In many long-term care facilities, the client (or the substitute decision maker) is required to sign a consent form giving permission for a haircut. Some facilities provide the option of an onsite salon where clients may pay to have their hair cut, washed, and groomed.

    1. • Do not attempt to cut a client’s hair if the client is agitated or uncooperative.
    2. • Cut the smallest amount of hair possible to get rid of the matted hair.
    3. • Make every attempt to ensure that the client’s hair appears attractive. For instance, the haircut should not be lopsided and uneven, drawing attention to the client’s hair.
    4. • Use the correct type of hair shears (scissors). Never cut hair with scissors meant for other purposes, as they can tear the hair, causing discomfort to the client.

      1. Give the client a folded washcloth to hold over their eyes.
      1. Wet and rinse the hair using a water pitcher or handheld nozzle.

    Leaning Back

    If the client prefers to lean back over the sink, follow these steps:

      1. Place the chair or wheelchair so it faces away from the sink.
      1. Place a folded towel over the sink edge to protect the neck (FIGURE 18.4).
      1. Help the client tilt their head back over the edge of the sink. Use extreme care when bending the client’s head back, to prevent artery dissection or other life-threatening consequences.
      1. Give the client a folded washcloth to hold over their eyes (FIGURE 18.5).
      1. Wet and rinse the hair using a water pitcher or handheld nozzle.

    Lying Down

    If the client has had recent spinal injury or surgery, they may be required to lie on a stretcher while the hair is shampooed at the sink, instead of being shampooed in bed. The following steps must be followed:

      1. Place the stretcher in front of the sink; lock the stretcher wheels and apply the safety straps to prevent the client from rolling. Follow the care plan for side rail use.
      1. Place a waterproof plastic bag under the client to minimize the chance of getting wet. Drape the edge of the bag into the sink.
      1. Place a folded towel over the edge of the sink.
      1. Place a folded washcloth over the client’s eyes (see FIGURE 18.5, A).
      1. Wet and rinse the hair using a water pitcher or handheld nozzle.

    Assistive Devices

    Assistive devices may make shampooing a client’s hair easier. For example, you could use a plastic cape to drape over the client’s shoulders and into the sink, or you may use a wheelchair shampoo rinse tray, which is made of plastic and clamps onto the back of the wheelchair, extending over the sink. The client’s head rests directly on it. The water runs down the tray and into the sink (see FIGURE 18.5), and the tray’s edges keep the water from spilling over the sides onto the floor.

    Shampooing in Bed

    Clients who cannot tolerate sitting long enough to have their hair washed at the sink may have their hair shampooed while in bed. To begin, the client should be moved as close to the edge of the bed as possible to ensure that the client remains in proper body alignment and to help reduce the amount of stretching you have to do. Place a folded cloth over the client’s eyes when shampooing. Ask the client to assist in maintaining comfortable placement of the cloth, since it may move during this procedure. Place a shampoo tray under the client’s head to protect the linen and mattress from getting wet. The tray also drains the water into a basin placed on a chair next to the bed (see FIGURE 18.5). Use a water pitcher to wet and rinse the hair (see the Focus on Home Care: Shampoo Trays box).

    Focus on Home Care

    Shampoo Trays

    You can make a shampoo tray from a plastic shower curtain, a tablecloth, or even a plastic drop cloth used for painting. However, try to avoid using plastic garbage bags, as they slip and slide too easily and are not sturdy. To make a shampoo tray, first place the plastic under the client’s head. To prevent water from spilling over the sides, make a raised edge by rolling up the plastic sheet around the edges. Place the ends of the plastic into the basin to direct the water into the basin. Check the care plan, and consult with your supervisor to confirm that this option is best before starting the procedure.

    Shampooing a Client’s Hair in Bed

    Dignity • Independence • Individualized Care • Preferences • Privacy • Safety (see BOX 1.4 , on page 17)

    Pre-Procedure

    • 1 Identify the client, according to employer policy.
      • This eliminates the possibility of mistaking one client for another.
    • 2 Explain the procedure to the client.
    • 3 Practise proper hand hygiene.
    • 4 Collect the following supplies:
      • □ Two bath towels
    • □ Face towel or washcloth
    • □ Shampoo
    • □ Hair conditioner (if requested by client)
    • □ Bath thermometer
    • □ Pitcher or nozzle (if needed)
    • □ Shampoo tray (if needed)
    • □ Basin or pail (if needed)
    • □ Waterproof pad (if needed)
    • □ Gloves (if needed)
    • □ Comb and brush
    • □ Hair dryer
    • By collecting all the necessary equipment beforehand, you will not have to leave your client’s side and can eliminate wasting time and energy in obtaining the forgotten equipment.
    • 5 Arrange the items close at hand.
      • Planning ahead eliminates unnecessary steps and is less tiring for the client.
    • 6 Provide for privacy. Practise proper hand hygiene.

    Procedure

    • 7 Position the client as close to the edge of the bed as possible for the method you will use. Place the waterproof pad and shampoo tray under the head and shoulders, if needed.
      • Positioning the client close to you reduces your risk for back strain while washing hair.
    • 8 Place a bath towel over the client’s shoulders or over the pillow.
      • This protects the client’s bedding and clothing.
    • 9 Brush or comb client’s hair to remove snarls and tangles.
      • It is easier to detangle dry hair than wet hair.
    • 10 Raise the bed rail if used.
      • This decreases the risk of the client accidentally falling out of bed.
    • 11 Obtain water. Water temperature should be about 40.5°C (105°F). Test temperature using a bath thermometer, according to your employer’s policy. Allow the client to test the water temperature with their hand.
      • This ensures that the client is comfortable with the water temperature and helps you avoid burning the client with water that is too hot.
    • 12 Lower the bed rail if used.
    • 13 Put on gloves if needed.
      • Gloves are needed if the client has scalp problems of any kind.
    • 14 Ask the client to hold a dampened face towel or washcloth over their eyes, making sure that it does not cover the nose or mouth.
      • A moistened cloth will not slip off as easily as a dry cloth would.
    • 15 Use the pitcher or nozzle to wet the hair.
      • Shampoo will spread better and more evenly on wet hair.
    • 16 Use a small amount of shampoo.
      • Too much shampoo is wasteful and is difficult to rinse out of the hair. If left on the scalp, it can cause dryness and flakiness.

    • 17 Work up a lather with both hands. Start at the hairline. Work toward the back of the head. If the client is leaning forward, start at the back of the head and work toward the hairline.

      • The hairline is usually the most oily or soiled area and should be lathered the longest.
    • 18 Massage the scalp with your fingertip pads.
      • Massaging the scalp is relaxing for the client. Using your fingertip pads will reduce the chances of scratching the scalp with your fingernails.
    • 19 Rinse the hair.
      • Rinsing removes oil- and dirt-saturated shampoo.
    • 20 Repeat steps 15 through 18.
    • 21 Rinse the hair completely.
      • This ensures that no trace of shampoo is left in the hair or on the scalp.
    • 22 Apply conditioner following the directions on the container.
      • Conditioners make the hair easier to comb.
    • 23 Squeeze water from the client’s hair.
      • This decreases drying time.
    • 24 Cover the hair with a bath towel.
      • This helps remove excess water and keeps the client warm.
    • 25 Dry the client’s face with the towel.
    • 26 Help the client raise their head if appropriate.
      • Many clients have weakened neck muscles. Assisting them will make the movement more comfortable for the clients and may decrease the risk for neck injury.
    • 27 Rub the hair and scalp with the towel. Use the second towel if the first one becomes wet.
      • This helps remove excess water and keeps the client warm.
    • 28 Comb or brush hair to remove snarls and tangles.
    • 29 Dry and style the hair as quickly as possible.
      • This helps prevent the hair from drying unstyled.
    • 30 Remove gloves if used. Practise proper hand hygiene.

    • 31 Provide for safety and comfort.
    • 32 Place the call bell within reach.∗
      • This provides an easy, safe way for the client to contact staff if necessary.
    • 33 Follow the care plan for bed rail use.∗
      • This reduces the risk for client injury.
    • 34 Remove privacy measures.
    • 35 Clean and return equipment to its proper place.
      • This makes it easier to find for future use.
    • 36 Discard disposable items.
      • Discarding used items decreases the risk for pathogen spread.
    • 37 Follow employer policy for handling dirty linen.
      • Proper linen disposal will decrease the risk for pathogen spread.
    • 38 Practise proper hand hygiene.

    Shaving

    Clients shave for comfort and a sense of well-being. You may be asked to remove unwanted hair in clients by shaving, depilation (hair removal by tweezing or through chemical hair removers), or waxing. However, you must be aware of what your client’s care plan has specified, and consult with your supervisor if you have any questions about the technique you should use.

    Depending on their conditions, clients may prefer to shave themselves or may require your assistance. Shaving may be done at the sink, at the bedside, or in bed. You may need to use electric or manual shavers, depending on the client’s condition, preference, and what is specified in the care plan (see the Think About Safety: Guidelines for Shaving Clients box).

    Think About Safety

    Guidelines for Shaving Clients

    1. • Some employers do not allow support workers to shave diabetic clients, clients with ingrown hair or skin irritations, clients who are combative, or clients who take medication for blood clotting, under any circumstances, so you should always follow your employer’s policy. Shaving can easily cause injuries.
    2. • While shaving a client, always follow Standard Practices, such as practising proper hand hygiene and wearing gloves.
    3. • Because of the risk of transmitting pathogens (see Chapter 19) from one client to another, the same razors or shavers should not be used between clients. All clients should have their own razors and shavers.
    4. • Follow the care plan for the type of shaver used for each client.
    5. • Protect bed linens by placing a towel under the part that is being shaved. If you are shaving the client’s face, place a towel over the client’s shoulders to protect their clothing.
    6. • Soften the client’s facial hair before shaving by applying a warm, moist cloth onto the skin. If using a manual razor, use shaving cream (after applying the warm, moist cloth) to soften the skin and hair, making it easier to shave.
    7. • Encourage the client to do as much as is safely possible.
    8. • Stretch the skin taut, as needed.
    9. • Shave in the direction of hair growth when shaving the face and underarms.
    10. • When shaving the leg, shave up from the ankles, against the direction of hair growth.
    11. • Rinse the shaved area completely.
    12. • Shaving can potentially cut, nick, or irritate the skin. Ensure that you shave carefully and only after properly preparing the client’s skin and hair.
    13. • Follow Standard Practices to prevent contact with blood. Handle razor blades and disposable shavers extremely carefully to protect yourself from cuts; discard them in the sharps container (see Chapter 20).
    14. • Apply direct pressure to nicks or cuts on the client (wear gloves).
    15. • Report nicks, cuts, or skin irritation to your supervisor at once.
    16. • Discard disposable razors and blades in the sharps container. Handle them carefully.
    17. • Follow employer policy for disposing of sharps.
    18. • If using an electric razor, make sure the shaver is in good working order before each use. Follow the safety rules for electrical equipment (see Chapter 20).

    You may be given the following shaving instructions for certain clients:

    1. • Manual shavers are generally not used on clients with dementia. However, some clients may be startled by the loud buzzing sound that an electric razor makes, or they may not be familiar with the type of razor you are using. These clients may not understand what you are doing and may resist care and make sudden movements. It is important to introduce the razor to the client on every use, with a gentle and nonthreatening tone of voice and body language. Most agencies allow only the use of electric shavers for these clients because of the increased risk for the client to make sudden movements that could cause serious nicks and cuts.
    2. • Manual shavers with open blades should never be used on clients receiving medications that slow down blood clotting (anticoagulants). In these clients, a nick or cut could cause serious bleeding. Electric shavers are preferable for clients taking these types of medications. If the client’s care plan does not state what type of razor to use, you need to ask your supervisor.
    3. • Electric shavers are not used on clients receiving oxygen. A spark from the electric shaver could cause a fire.
    4. • If an electric razor scratches the client’s face, have the shaver repaired at once. Report and record the scratches; most agencies and facilities require that an incident report be completed.
    5. • Electric shavers should be emptied after every use.
    6. • Never rinse out an electric razor under a running tap unless the manufacturer’s instructions specify to do so. Electric razors should also never be immersed in water.

    Electric Shaver Maintenance

    Simple cleaning routines performed on a regular basis can help prolong the life of the electric shaver and can reduce skin irritation and the risk for injury to the client.3

    1. • Many problems with electric shavers can be avoided by simply removing the cutting head and tapping the hair particles into a tissue after each use. Discard the tissue.
    2. • Most electric shavers come with a cleaning brush, which also should be used after each shave to remove hair particles that cannot be removed by blowing on them.
    3. • Some manufacturers advise spraying the shaver every week or so with a special spray to both lubricate and degrease the cutting blades.
    4. • In addition, cutters and the foil surface should be replaced periodically (according to the manufacturer’s directions).

    Caring for Moustaches and Beards

    Beards and moustaches need daily care to prevent food, and mouth and nose drainage from collecting in the hair. Daily washing and combing usually are enough to keep beards and moustaches clean. Ask clients how they would like their beard or moustache groomed. Never trim or shave the beard or moustache without the client’s consent.

    Shaving Legs and Underarms for Clients

    The practice of shaving legs and underarms varies among cultures and individuals. Some clients shave only the lower legs, whereas others shave to mid-thigh or the entire leg.

    Clients’ legs and underarms should be shaved after the bath, when the skin is soft. To save time, collect the client’s shaving items along with the bath items. When shaving, use soap and water or a shaving cream to work up a lather. Rinse the razor in a kidney basin, not in the bathwater. The guidelines in the Think About Safety: Guidelines for Shaving Clients box also apply when shaving the underarms and legs.

    Pubic Hair

    Pubic hair is found in the frontal genital area, in the crotch, and on the top insides of the legs. In most cases, pubic hair begins to appear within 3 years following puberty. With aging, a person will have less body hair, and the body hair may turn grey or white, much like the hair on the head. Many individuals consider the care of pubic hair to be an important part of grooming, so they may choose to remove it. Some cultures, such as Muslim cultures, have rules that insist on the trimming or removal of pubic hair.
