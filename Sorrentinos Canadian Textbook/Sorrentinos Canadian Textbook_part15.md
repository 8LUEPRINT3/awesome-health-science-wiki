## Supporting the Infant, Child, or Teen

As a support worker, you will be expected to provide care and support to people of all ages, including the young. The young person that you are assigned to support may be a family member of your primary client (see the Supporting Jane Smith: Families as Clients box on page 1010) or may be your assigned client. Always consult your supervisor if you have questions or concerns when working with children ([FIGURE 42.1](#f0010)).

When providing support to the young, you must always remember that they have rights, too. Promote the priorities of support work when working with children. When dealing with both the young and their families, you must remember to follow the principles of DIPPS (Dignity, Independence, Individualized care, Privacy, Preferences, and Safety) just as you would for any other client (see the Providing Compassionate Care: Working With Children box).

### The Support Worker’s Role

As a support worker, your goal is to provide a stable, secure, and safe atmosphere for the family. Such an
environment is very important for children who have illnesses and for those who are experiencing stress when a family member is ill.

## Assisting Infants and Children to Meet Nutritional Needs

Infants and children require proper, nutritional food to grow and develop each body system to its maximum potential. The nutritional requirements for children are discussed in [Chapter 27](../B9780323709392000274/B9780323709392000274.xhtml). However, getting children to eat properly is often an ongoing challenge for parents and caregivers. As a support worker, you should be aware that a number of children in Canada face nutritional challenges.

Communication

1. • Use active listening skills. Maintain eye contact and concentrate on what is being said.
2. • Watch for nonverbal communication cues from the child, such as frowning, lack of eye contact, and lack of smiling.
3. • Provide nonverbal communication by way of comforting the child with a touch on the shoulder or arm or a hug (according to acceptable custom and culture).
4. • Speak to children in a manner that is appropriate for their developmental level or age. If the child (or teen) lives with a developmental delay, speak using clear instructions, being careful about your body language and nonverbal cues. Ask the child’s family for suggestions on how to best communicate with the child.
5. • Answer the child’s questions simply, honestly, and clearly. Difficult questions about a family member’s illness or death should be handled carefully. Ask your supervisor for guidance in advance.
6. • Offer praise for something well done.
7. • Give encouragement when the child attempts to improve behaviour, even when there is only slight progress.
8. • Use positive suggestions rather than negative words. Avoid using “Don’t” and “No.”

Rest, Sleep, Play, and Exercise

1. • Maintain the child’s bedtime and naptime routines. Routines and rituals provide comfort and, therefore, are usually very important to children. Going to sleep at the right time is also very important for most children because they will become overtired, overactive, or cranky without enough sleep.
2. • Supervise playtime. Encourage active exercise, if allowed.
3. • Playtime activities can be helpful to encourage activity, social interaction, and development. Choose activities that are age-appropriate. Refer to helpful sites such as An Early Start to Physical Activity for Babies, Toddlers and Preschoolers (<https://www.gov.mb.ca/health/activeliving/tools_resources/early_years/kids_in_motion.pdf>).
4. • Avoid taking sides when a disagreement occurs among children.
5. • Do not give extra attention to one child and ignore others.
6. • Ignore tattling if it is used to get attention.
7. • Distract the child if they begin to misbehave. If possible, play with the child or provide a different toy or activity.

Mealtime

1. • Prepare foods that the child will eat. Follow the care plan. Check whether the child has any food allergies or sensitivities (see [Chapter 27](../B9780323709392000274/B9780323709392000274.xhtml)).
2. • Do not force a child to eat. When the child feels full, the meal is over.
3. • Serve meals and snacks on time. Many children become cranky if they do not eat on time.

Here are a few of the many challenges that children and their families face and that a support worker may find stressful:

1. • More than 1.2 million Canadian children live in poverty, including one in five under the age of 7 years. Children from economically poor families are more likely to live with chronic health challenges and have difficulties at school and are less likely to feel good about themselves.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur503)
2. • One fifth of school-aged children spend some time alone at home and tend to make decisions by themselves about what and when to eat. Almost 30% of these “latch-key” kids are between 6 and 9 years of age.
3. • Obesity appears to be increasingly prevalent among Canadian children, mainly because of their limited physical activity and high-calorie food choices. Obesity is excessive fat accumulation that puts a person’s health at risk.
4. • Most young people, especially girls, are concerned about their appearance and weight and may feel a need to diet. Some have low self-esteem, which
   increases their risk for eating disorders. By Grade 9, only 26% of girls report having high self-esteem, compared with 40% of boys.
5. • About 13% of Canadians speak a language other than English or French at home and come from backgrounds that are different from the dominant culture; their cultural diversity is reflected in their eating patterns.

### Helping a Child Eat Well

#### Setting the Stage for Pleasant Mealtimes

1. • The family should eat together as often as possible. A regular mealtime gives family members a chance to talk and relax together and helps them develop healthy attitudes about food (see [Chapter 27](../B9780323709392000274/B9780323709392000274.xhtml)).[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur504)
2. • The meal table should be viewed as a positive, conflict-free zone. Problem solving and difficult discussions should be saved for a different time and place.
3. • Family members should avoid distractions such as reading, playing with toys, watching television, or answering the phone. The family should relax, eat, and talk together instead.
4. • Adults must teach and model good table manners and respectful behaviour.

#### Avoiding Power Struggles and Learning to Trust the Child’s Food Choices

Most children self-correct their undereating, overeating, and weight issues when the power struggle is taken out of their mealtimes. Parents and caregivers should avoid using comments such as “Eat at least one bite of the vegetable,” “That’s a lot of bread you’re eating,” “Clean your plate,” and “No seconds.” Children have an internal hunger gauge that controls how much they eat. By overriding the child’s signals, parents and caregivers will prevent the child from easily tuning in to that internal hunger gauge.

If the child skips certain foods, eats lightly, or eats more than the parents or you as the support worker would like them to, do the following:

1. • As the support worker, check the family’s diet, and ask: Are there any foods that the family eats that are not good for them? Is the child watching the behaviours of adults? After consulting with your employer and if you are permitted to, you can politely make suggestions to the family to improve the situation.
2. • Let the child decide when they are full. You can remind the child of the next scheduled meal or snack time: “Just make sure that you’ve eaten enough to hold you till snack time, which is after we get back from your swim class.” This reminder can help the child make a wise decision about what and how much to eat now.

Although it can be tempting to give in to the child’s demands, if you give consistent messages to them about eating and mealtimes, the child will eventually become more comfortable with the division of responsibility.

Gradually, the child’s eating habits will balance out. You will notice that as long as you provide nutritious choices, the child will eat a healthy variety and amount of food each week. Try to relax through this change in roles, and you will see that the child relaxes, too.

#### Adjusting Your Approach According to the Child’s Age

1. •
   Feeding an infant. From birth, infants follow their internal hunger and fullness cues. They eat when they are hungry, and they stop eating when they are full. Experts recommend that newborns be fed on demand and not on a schedule.
2. •
   Feeding a child. As you introduce new foods to a young child’s diet, you are encouraging a love of variety, texture, and taste in foods. The more adventurous a child feels about foods, the more balanced and nutritious their weekly intake will be. Remember that it is normal to need to present a new or different food as many as 15 times or more before a child will be comfortable trying it. The key is to offer the new food in a relaxed manner without pressuring the child.

#### Getting Help for a Child’s Eating Habits

As the support worker providing care to a child, if you are worried about the child’s eating habits, you can call your supervisor, who may suggest that you ask the family doctor for help. Calling your supervisor is especially important if the child has a major change in appetite or weight—for example, eating too much or too little, or gaining or losing weight. Other concerns might include the following:[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur505)

1. • Eating issues have turned the family’s mealtimes into a battleground.
2. • The child may have an eating disorder (see [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml)), such as anorexia nervosa (eating too little) or bulimia nervosa (eating too much and then vomiting the food and possibly abusing laxatives or enemas to reduce the likelihood of absorbing food to avoid weight gain).

Your supervisor (or the child’s doctor) can advise you on the actions you can take or direct you to seek out assistance from someone with specific expertise, such as:

1. •
   Registered dietitians
   , who teach people about nutrition, develop diets to promote health, and specialize in counselling to help treat food-related issues, including eating disorders
2. •
   Pediatricians
   , who may have special training and experience in caring for children with eating issues
3. •
   Therapists or counsellors
   , who can help the family cope with power struggles over eating
4. •
   Psychiatrists
   , who can provide counselling and medication
5. •
   Pediatric gastroenterologists
   , who can rule out or treat conditions of the digestive system that could be causing the eating disorder
6. •
   Pediatric endocrinologists
   , who can rule out or treat hormone-related conditions that can lead to weight issues

## Reye’s Syndrome

In cooperation with the Canadian Paediatric Society, Health Canada issued a warning about ASA (acetylsalicylic acid, commonly referred to as Aspirin) and products containing ASA, advising that it not be used for treating symptoms of the flu or chicken pox in persons younger than 20 years of age.[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur494) In addition, manufacturers of products containing ASA are now putting warnings on their labels saying that a doctor should be consulted before giving ASA to children or teenagers who have the flu or chicken pox.

These warnings are based on research that has linked the use of ASA to treat the flu or chicken pox and the development of a condition called Reye’s syndrome, which involves inflammation of the brain. Reye’s syndrome is a rare but often fatal condition that can occur during the recovery stage of the flu or chicken pox.[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur494) A health care provider will advise when it is appropriate to treat the child’s fever and suggest ways to do so. The symptoms of Reye’s syndrome are as follows:[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur494)

1. • A viral illness (such as the flu or chicken pox) that seems to last longer than in others who have the same illness
2. • Frequent or persistent nausea and vomiting
3. • A change in personality, confusion, agitation, or combativeness (being eager to fight)
4. • Coma
5. • Seizures

If these symptoms occur in a child or teenager who has been given ASA for the flu or chicken pox, you should seek medical attention immediately.

## Discipline

When supervising or caring for children, you need to reinforce the rules of acceptable behaviour in the home. The system of rules that governs how we act is called discipline. Discipline is a positive way of teaching responsible behaviour. It sets limits and provides guidelines so that children can learn how to behave in an appropriate way. A support worker should never use physical force, such as spanking or pinching, at any time.

Discipline should be consistent. Each time a rule is broken, the consequence should be the same. For example, Noah (age 4 years) forgets to remove his boots and gets the floor dirty. He must get a cloth and help clean the floor, and he is not allowed to play until he does so. Noah knows the rules and what will happen if a rule is broken. The child feels secure when they know the caregiver’s expectations.

Recognize the child’s efforts when they try to follow the rules of the household. Praising these efforts encourages acceptable behaviour. Being positive with children is always more effective than being negative. For example, if Noah gets the floor dirty, it is better to say, “Let’s clean up the mess” than “Shame on you for making that big mess.” After he cleans up the mess, you could say, “Thank you for doing such a fine job.” This show of appreciation would help to make Noah feel good about his accomplishment. Praise and acknowledgement also promote a child’s dignity and show respect for the child.

Your role in disciplining the child is to:

1. • Know the rules of acceptable behaviour in each family situation
2. • Ask an appropriate family member to clarify the rules if you are unsure
3. • Reinforce existing rules
4. • Be consistent when using discipline
5. • Praise the child’s efforts at following the rules

Some parents may have very few rules of discipline, or the existing rules may seem too harsh or too loose. In such a case, you should contact your supervisor. New rules may need to be set, but you should not set discipline rules without the guidance of your supervisor.

### Punishment

Punishment is a harsh response that occurs when a discipline rule is broken.
Punishing a child for failing to follow the rules of the household is not your responsibility.
 If a family member asks you to do so, explain that it is against your agency’s policy to carry out punishment. Ask the family member to contact your supervisor if they have further questions. Record this information, and tell your supervisor about the situation.

## Supporting the Child Who Demonstrates Challenging Behaviours

Children raised in troubled environments may become troubled themselves. Children who display challenging behaviours may be verbally aggressive, resistant to rules and regulations, or have difficulty forming bonds with others.

### Why Do Some Children “Act Out”?

Children of all ages sometimes express their feelings of anger, frustration, fear, guilt, or shame through their behaviour because it is the only way they know how to convey what they feel. Some children display emotional or behavioural outbursts, whereas others will demonstrate that they feel overwhelmed, tired, or physically unwell. Children may behave this way if there are too many changes in their lives; if they have recently experienced painful losses; or if they have problems at school, such as difficulty learning, too much peer pressure, or experience bullying.

Some children may have suffered or witnessed physical abuse, emotional abuse, or sexual abuse, or they may be using drugs or alcohol. In cases in which children have been hospitalized for much of their lives, they may have difficulty accepting limits, especially if their parents have tended to overindulge them, as parents are tempted to do if they think the child may die or if they feel a need to reduce their own feelings of guilt regarding the child’s illness.

It is the support worker’s responsibility to provide care in a nonjudgemental and supportive manner no matter the reason for a child “acting out.” Some ways to successfully manage a child who displays challenging behaviours are discussed in [BOX 42.2](#b0015).

## Preventing Infections

When children start child care or school, it may seem as if they get sick all the time. This pattern is normal, as all children’s immune systems are developing, and their resistance to infection develops only after exposure to a multitude of germs ([see Chapter 17](../B9780323709392000171/B9780323709392000171.xhtml)). Young children in large groups are breeding grounds for organisms that cause illnesses. Little hands rub drippy noses and then transfer infectious agents to other children or to shared toys.

Children with physical or developmental delays may be even more prone to infectious illnesses, as they may have a fragile immune system. As a support worker, you have a responsibility to ensure that you always practise hand hygiene (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)) to prevent spreading infections from yourself to these children. By simply following proper hygiene practices, you may reduce the likelihood that a contagious infection will keep the child home from school or child care. The guidelines for hand hygiene should also be followed in your own household to reduce transmission of germs and infectious illnesses from your client’s home to you or your own family.

### Prevention of Childhood Infections

When a child sneezes or coughs, the child sprays germs into the air, which land on other children, who are then infected. A child’s hands can become vehicles for carrying viruses and bacteria from toys and other frequently handled objects to other children’s eyes, noses, or mouths, which are the usual points of entry for germs that cause illness.

The single most important thing anyone can do to prevent illness is to wash their hands thoroughly and frequently. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has recommended that people wash their hands with soap and warm water for 20 seconds—about as long as it takes to sing the “Happy Birthday” song twice. Alcohol-based hand sanitizers come in disposable wipes or in gel form, require no water, and help keep hands clean (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)).

### How Long Should Sick Children Stay Home?

Each facility generally has its own rules, but most will not let children attend school or child care if they have a fever of more than 38°C (100.4°F), are vomiting,
have diarrhea, have tested positive for COVID-19, or have been in contact with a person with COVID-19. In addition, some facilities require that children with strep throat or bacterial conjunctivitis (pink eye) be on antibiotic therapy for 24 hours before returning.

BOX 42.2 Twelve Principles for Supporting a Child Who Displays Challenging Behaviours

* 1. Be specific about your expectations, and stick to them!
* 2. Put first things first. Keep your focus on important issues, and choose your battles wisely. This reaction will teach the child to make good decisions, not to win power struggles.
* 3. Praise, praise, and praise some more! Positive reinforcement (encouraging a behaviour by rewarding the desired behaviour after it is demonstrated) is much more effective than negative reinforcement (encouraging a behaviour by penalizing when that behaviour is not demonstrated).
* 4. Be knowledgeable about the child’s specific challenge(s) and difficulties. The child’s parents, siblings, or caregivers may be able to assist you with finding solutions.
* 5. Follow the client’s care plan for the amount of time and activity you should provide for the child. If you can, spend some one-on-one time focusing only on positives.
* 6. Be specific about positive and negative consequences (“rewards and punishments”), and follow through with them consistently. You should also consistently ensure that your words and actions are straightforward and clear so that the child cannot misinterpret you.
* 7. Act quickly, and speak calmly. Never yell, scream, or swear, as these behaviours will only aggravate the situation.
* 8. Give bonuses, and focus on the positive. For example, if a child puts his clothes away, reward him by placing a gold star on his “Activities” sheet. Display the sheet where others can see it.
* 9. Seek out the middle ground. Know when to negotiate and when to stand firm. For example, if a child wants to watch television for more time than usual and her request is reasonable, and if the parent permits it, negotiate with her the length of time she can have.
* 10. Practise forgiveness and patience. Children need to know that they are still valued and cared for in spite of their naughty behaviour.
* 11. Use others for support, and seek assistance when appropriate. Consult your supervisor if the child’s behaviour worsens or if you are unsure about what you should do.
* 12. Remember to take care of yourself. You have needs, too! Pay attention to your own cues that you may be losing patience. It is better to silently count to 10 than to lose your cool!

#### Resistance Comes With Time

Despite the best efforts, most children will get sick, especially during the first few years of contact with larger groups of children. Most children’s immunity does improve with time, with school-age children gradually becoming less prone to common illnesses and recovering more quickly from the illnesses they do catch. If you have any questions about the child that you are supporting and their immunity, you should ask your supervisor.

### The Top Five Reasons Children Miss School or Child Care

* 1.
  Colds.
   The most common childhood illnesses are upper respiratory infections (URIs), the medical term for colds and other viral ailments that affect the throat, nose, and sinuses. Whereas adults, on average, experience 2 to 4 colds a year, children typically experience 6 to 10. Children also tend to have more severe and longer-lasting symptoms than adults do.
* Studies have shown that there is no benefit in treating children’s colds with antihistamines, decongestants, or cough suppressants. If a child you are looking after is ill, you should discuss the appropriate medication to reduce fever. Children should never be given Aspirin because it may trigger Reye’s syndrome, a rare but potentially fatal disease (see page 1010).
* 2.
  The stomach flu (gastroenteritis).
   The second most common childhood illness is gastroenteritis, more commonly and incorrectly known as known as “stomach flu.” This childhood illness causes vomiting and diarrhea and can lead to dehydration, particularly in very young children. Signs and symptoms of dehydration include the following:
* 1. • Excessive thirst
  2. • Dry mouth
  3. • Little or no urine, or dark yellow urine
  4. • Decreased tears
  5. • Severe weakness or lethargy
* Your client may be required to drink an oral rehydration solution (e.g., special frozen treats or Pedialyte), which can help replace lost fluids, minerals, and salts.
  (These items would be on the client’s care plan and should not be given unless specified there.)
   When food is re-introduced, easy-to-digest items such as broth, toast, bananas, and rice should be given first. The client should avoid eating dairy products, which can be hard to digest.
* Often, parents assume that any kind of stomach upset in a child is the result of an infection, when the real culprit could be simple indigestion or constipation. Some children get stomach aches when they are worried about things either at home or at school. The dread of facing a bully or taking a test, for example, can make a child’s stomach hurt. It is important for a doctor to determine the cause of a child’s symptoms before prescribing treatment.
* 3. Ear infection (otitis media). Ear infections most often occur in children under the age of 2 years, but they can also be common between the ages of 5 and 6 years, triggered by the respiratory illnesses picked up in kindergarten or Grade 1. Colds or allergies cause congestion, which may squeeze shut the child’s eustachian tube, the tiny drainage pipe for the middle ear. Fluid trapped in the middle ear can become a breeding ground for viruses or bacteria.
* It can be difficult to distinguish between ear infections caused by bacteria and those caused by viruses. The distinction is important, however, because antibiotics will cure bacterial—but not viral—infections. And using antibiotics when they are not necessary has serious consequences, most notably the emergence of bacterial strains with built-in resistance to drugs that fight infection. For that reason, doctors today often wait to see if an ear infection will clear up on its own before prescribing antibiotics.
* 4.
  Pink eye (conjunctivitis).
   Pink eye (conjunctivitis) is an inflammation of the clear membrane that covers the white part of the eye and lines the inner surface of eyelids. When caused by viruses or bacteria, conjunctivitis is highly contagious. It is typically treated with antibiotic eye drops or ointment. Warm or cool compresses may ease the child’s discomfort.
* 5.
  Sore throat
  . Most sore throats are caused by viruses and are usually associated with other respiratory signs and symptoms such as a runny nose and cough. But about 15% of children’s sore throats are caused by streptococci, bacteria that cause strep throat.
* Fevers above 38°C (100.4°F) are common with strep throat, and swallowing can be so painful that the child may have difficulty eating. Antibiotics are required to combat strep throat. Left untreated, the bacteria can eventually trigger an abnormal immune response, which can lead to rheumatic fever. Complications of rheumatic fever include damaged heart valves and stiff, swollen joints.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur485),[6](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur486)

### Vaccinations to Prevent Serious Infectious Illnesses

Canadian babies and children require routine administration of vaccinations to be able to fend off contagious infections and illnesses such as measles, mumps,
pertussis (whooping cough), diphtheria, tetanus, certain types of meningitis, and polio.[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur487) Vaccines are so effective that most of the diseases that they help prevent have now become rare in Canada (see [BOX 42.3](#b0020): How Do Vaccines Work?)

BOX 42.3 How Do Vaccines Work?

1. • Vaccines help lower a child’s risk of infection. They work with the body’s natural defenses to develop immunity to disease.
2. • Vaccines are made with a tiny amount of killed (dead) or weakened germs that imitate an infection. Once in the body, these germs cause the immune system to react by creating antibodies. The antibodies will remain in the child’s body and quickly recognize the germs. This gives them the ability to “remember” how to fight the germs if and when they encounter the live germs.
3. • A child’s boosted immune system will help destroy the germ threat before it becomes dangerous, thereby preventing disease or lessening its impact.
4. • If the child is not vaccinated against those germs, they can get very sick. This is because their immune system is not prepared to handle the germs, allowing germs to multiply and result in disease.
5. • Vaccines currently available in Canada can protect children against 14 serious diseases. Most of these childhood vaccines provide over 90% protection against the disease.
6. • While most children develop strong immunity following vaccination, some children may only develop partial protection. There are several factors that can influence the effectiveness of vaccines, including:
7. 1. • The age of the person being vaccinated
   2. • The overall health status of the person being vaccinated
   3. • The person’s immune response to the vaccine
   4. • The degree of match between the vaccine components and the type of bacteria or virus circulating in the population
8. • In rare cases, those who have developed only partial immunity after vaccination can become infected and develop disease. However, vaccination helps prevent severe illness and serious outcomes, including death.

Source: Adapted from Government of Canada. (2019). How vaccines work? <https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/vaccination-children.html#how-vaccines-work>.

Updated immunization schedules are available from family doctors, local health units, or the Public Health Agency of Canada website, at <http://www.phac-aspc.gc.ca/publicat/cig-gci/p01-12-eng.php#tab1>. This website will ask for your province or territory and the child’s date of birth.

#### What Would Happen If We Stopped Vaccinating?

Another way to protect people with partial or no immunity is through high vaccination coverage in the population. When large groups of people are vaccinated, they create “community immunity” (also known as herd immunity). The larger the number of people who are vaccinated in the community, the lower the risk of infection for those who are not vaccinated and those who developed only partial immunity from the vaccine. This means that when you are vaccinated, you protect yourself as well as those around you.[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur487)

Community immunity helps protect those at high risk of developing disease and severe complications or death, such as:

1. • Infants and children who are too young to be fully vaccinated
2. • Adults 65 years of age and older
3. • People with health conditions that affect their immune system (such as those undergoing chemotherapy to treat cancer or other serious health conditions)[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur487)

Vaccines have successfully and dramatically lowered the rates of disease in countries with strong vaccination programs. Some diseases, like polio, have been eliminated from Canada and the rest of the Americas because of vaccines. Unfortunately, polio, like other childhood diseases, continues to pose a threat due to its continued prevalence in other countries around the world.

When vaccination rates are kept at high levels in the population, the occurrence of disease is low and outbreaks are rare. On the other hand, when vaccination rates are low, disease rates go up and we are no longer protected from outbreaks and severe disease outcomes, including disability and death.[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur487)

In recent years, vaccination has become a subject of controversy. However, in countries where fewer people were immunized, the diseases quickly returned. For example:

1. • In 2000, the number of measles cases in Ireland increased from 148 to more than 1 200 in just 1 year because the vaccination rate had been reduced to 76%, and several children died as a result.
2. • In 1999, there was a large outbreak of rubella (German measles) in Nebraska. All 83 adults who were affected had not been vaccinated; most of them came from countries where rubella vaccine is not routinely administered. Rubella is extremely dangerous during pregnancy and can cause the unborn infant either to die or to become severely handicapped.[8](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur488)
3. • In 2019, there were confirmed outbreaks of measles in the Northwest Territories, British Columbia, Alberta, Ontario, Quebec, and New Brunswick.[9](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur489) Measles is highly contagious and can lead to serious complications or death for children under the age of 5 years.

#### Why Do People Choose Not to Immunize Their Children?

There are parents who do not immunize their children for medical, religious, or ideological reasons. In 1998, The Lancet (a medical journal that is highly regarded throughout the world) published a study based on fraudulent (falsified) data linking the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine to autism. Although this finding has been disproven, many people have lost confidence in the MMR vaccine as a result of this article.

In Canada, only Ontario, Manitoba, and New Brunswick have legislation that mandates children be vaccinated in order to attend school.[10](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur490) For a child to attend school without immunizations in those provinces, a notarized statement of the reasons for opting out of vaccinations must be provided to the school by the child’s physician or nurse practitioner. In the case of an outbreak, children who are not vaccinated may be excluded from child care facilities or schools at the discretion of the Medical Office of Health.

#### Do the Benefits of Vaccination Outweigh the Possible Adverse Effects?

The short answer is yes. If there were no vaccines, there would be more cases of disease, more serious adverse (harmful) effects from disease, and more deaths.
The diseases that vaccines help prevent lead to pneumonia, deafness, brain damage, heart conditions, blindness, and paralysis in children who are not vaccinated.
 Canadian children are very fortunate to have vaccines that prevent diseases that still kill and disable children in some other parts of the world every day. The risks of not being vaccinated are much greater than any risk from vaccination itself.

Key Points

• Support workers provide care and support to people of all ages, including the young. A young person that you are assigned to support may be your assigned client and have physical or developmental challenges or they may be a family member of your primary client.

• As a support worker, you need to adjust your approach to providing care to a child according to the child’s age.

• Your responsibilities will vary in each family situation but usually will include (1) developing positive relationships with all family members, (2) maintaining the existing rules of behaviour, (3) maintaining daily routines as much as possible, (4) being alert to situations that may create stress or cause harm to the family, and (5) reporting any issues to your supervisor.

• In regard to children, support workers are responsible for helping the child to (1) eat well, (2) prevent injuries, (3) prevent falls, (4) prevent burns, (5) prevent accidental poisoning, and (6) prevent infections.

• As a support worker, you should be aware of the general guidelines for caring for children in the home regarding (1) communication; (2) rest, sleep, play, and exercise; (3) mealtime; and (4) discipline.

• Your role, as a support worker, in disciplining the child is to (1) know the rules of acceptable behaviour in each family situation, (2) ask an appropriate family member to clarify the rules if you are unsure, (3) reinforce existing rules, (4) be consistent when using discipline, and (5) praise the child’s efforts at following the rules.

• Generally, children with signs or symptoms of illness can return to school when they (1) have no fever, (2) can eat and drink normally, (3) are rested and alert enough to pay attention in class, and (4) have completed any period of medically recommended isolation.

• The top five reasons children miss school or child care are (1) colds, (2) gastroenteritis (stomach flu), (3) ear infection (otitis media), (4) pink eye (conjunctivitis), and (5) sore throats.

• Support workers should be aware of the reasons some children “act out.”

Gerontology is the study of the aging process. Geriatrics is the branch of medicine that provides care for older persons. These changes, however, are usually gradual. Emotional and social changes also occur, and most older people adjust to these changes well and live healthy and happy lives. Older people may continue to live in their own homes by themselves or with their partners or live with their adult children or other family members. ## Caring for Older Clients

Mainstream North American culture tends to overvalue youth, as reflected in media messages that stress the importance of looking and acting young. These are examples of ageism (see [Chapters 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml) and 12). There are ads for hair dyes that cover grey hair and lotions that reduce wrinkles so that people can look younger than they are. Media images often also stereotype older people. Many greeting cards and cartoons portray older persons as grouchy, unproductive, simple (not very intelligent), and slow. Remember, older people are not all alike. Every person—young,
middle-aged, or old—is unique, and every person develops and ages in their own way. Treating older people as if they are boring, useless, or childlike is practising ageism.

Older people can think and make decisions for themselves. Failure to recognize this fact can result in your making a person feel that they are being treated like a child. How would you feel if a younger person made your decisions for you? Would you feel insulted or angry? Would you feel useless? Treating older people disrespectfully threatens their dignity and sends the message that they are no longer useful, productive members of society.

Older people are often coping with the loss of loved ones, friends, and health. The loss of dignity and self-esteem in addition can be too much for them to bear, and they face the risks of withdrawal from social contact and depression (see [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml)). As a support worker, by treating older clients with respect, you help them maintain emotional and social health.

To care for older clients effectively, you need to understand the emotional, social, and physical changes that occur with aging. Clients often must live with more than one chronic, debilitating, or life-limiting condition. It is therefore important that support workers be aware of each client’s health status and needs, and to observe and provide basic assessment of each older person in a holistic manner. Support workers must also follow the individual care plan for each older client (see the Providing Compassionate Care: Supporting Older Clients box).

Supporting Older Clients

1. •
   Dignity.
    Show respect for your older clients. Avoid using terms, gestures, or a tone of voice that could be considered patronizing (see [Chapters 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml) and [9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)). Ask your clients how they would like to be addressed. Never assume you can use a client’s first name, even if you have heard your co-workers use it. Do not talk about the client with others. Do not exchange glances with co-workers when reacting to something an older client has said or done.
2. •
   Independence.
    Help clients only when necessary. Respect their routines. Do things the way clients are used to doing them. Allow time for rest and avoid rushing them.
3. •
   Individualized care.
    Regardless of the setting of where the care is taking place, for care to be truly client-centred, the client’s care plan must be adapted to fit the client’s personal choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. For example, it is important to use terms that are familiar to the client.
4. •
   Preferences.
    Older clients have the right to make choices, so you must have their consent for all procedures. Immediately report all changes in your client’s behaviour and health to your supervisor.

## Emotional and Social Changes

Greying hair, wrinkles, and slowing movements are physical reminders that one is growing old, and retirement and the deaths of partners, family members, and friends are social reminders. Physical and social changes have emotional effects on older persons and also have an impact on their basic needs for love and belonging and self-esteem.

### Retirement

Most people look forward to their retirement. People usually retire at the age of 65 years, but some may choose to retire earlier or work after they turn 65. Retirement is a reward for a lifetime of work. The person has earned the right not to work any longer and can now relax and enjoy life. Travel, leisure, spending time with family, and doing only what one wants to do are retirement “benefits.” Increasingly, retired people are interested in furthering their education.

Most people enjoy their retirement, but some are not so fortunate. Some are forced to retire because of persistent illness or disability, and poor health can make retirement very difficult. Retirement is often a person’s first real experience with aging.

Work has social and emotional effects. It helps meet the basic needs of love and belonging and self-esteem and brings fulfillment and a feeling of usefulness. Many people enjoy a sense of pride, identity, and status associated with their occupation or title. They take pride in a day’s work or a job well done. Friendships form; co-workers share daily events; and leisure time, recreation, and companionship often involve co-workers. Some people thus need work for emotional and social fulfillment, and for such people, retirement can be hard. Some retired people, therefore, take on part-time jobs or volunteer work ([FIGURE 43.2](#f0015)) because such activities promote a sense of purpose, usefulness, and well-being.

![An older man assists an elderly lady to keep her vote in a ballot box.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000432/main.assets/f43-02-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 43.2 These retired people are volunteering. (Can Stock Photo Inc./lisafx.)

Retirement allows many people the opportunity to pursue hobbies and activities that they have always enjoyed or the time to take up new ones. However, for people who have never cultivated interests or hobbies outside of their job, retirement can cause a very abrupt and upsetting change of lifestyle that they have difficulties adjusting to.

It is important to record and report any signs of depression that you observe in your client or that your client expresses after retirement. Your client may feel lonely and isolated from former co-workers and friends.

### Reduced Income

Retirement income is often less than half of a person’s full income during their work years. For some retired people, the Canada Pension Plan (CPP) may be the only source of income, but, unfortunately, CPP has not kept pace with the rising cost of living. More people qualify for the Old Age Security pension (OAS) than CPP. OAS is a monthly payment available to most people 65 years of age and older who meet the Canadian legal status and residency requirements. Employment history is not a factor in determining eligibility for OAS. A person can receive
OAS pension even if they have never worked or are still working.[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur492)

Being an older person and being retired do not mean one has fewer expenses. Older people still need to make rent or mortgage payments, buy food and clothing, spend money on transportation and utilities, and pay taxes. They must also pay for medications that are not covered under government drug plans.

Reduced income during retirement may force lifestyle changes such as limiting social and leisure activities and finding cheaper housing or moving in with children or other relatives. Some older people must rely on family for money or needed items; others live in poverty if they do not have an adequate pension income or assets. While the cost of living is rising in Canada, the old-age pension is not increasing at the same rate, so the gap between income and need is widening each year.

Some older people do not face a reduction in their income because when they were working, they were able to provide for their retirement through their savings, investments, retirement plans, and insurance. For these older people, the retirement years are financially comfortable.

### Social Relationships

Social relationships change throughout life (see the Respecting Diversity: Older Persons From Different Cultures box). Children grow up, leave home, and have their own families. Some of them live far away from their parents. Older friends and family move away, die, or become disabled. Some older people have regular contact with children, grandchildren, brothers and sisters, nieces and nephews, and other relatives and friends. But some others may feel isolated and lonely because of separation or distance from their children, their peers’ dying (see [Chapter 47](../B978032370939200047X/B978032370939200047X.xhtml)), and an overall lack of companionship.

Some older people develop mobility issues, which may interfere with their pursuing interests that they previously enjoyed, independently or in groups, such as gardening or boating or bicycling. Others may begin to feel isolated if they are no longer able to drive and must learn how to get around using public transportation. Some older people live in remote areas of the country and may not have easy access to stores or doctors’ offices. In some situations, the support worker may be one of the few people who regularly visits with them.

![icon](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000432/main.assets/icon02-9780323711654.jpg)
 Respecting Diversity

Older Persons From Different Cultures

Some older people who have moved to Canada from another country may not speak English or French. In Canada, they may have lived and worked in a community of people from their home country. When family and friends move away or die, these people may be left with no one to talk to. If the health care workers also do not speak their language or understand their culture, these older people can feel lonely and isolated.

Some older persons born into Indigenous communities may experience this isolation, too. They may have grown up, been educated, worked, and socialized within this same Indigenous community, and reached older age with the same peer group. They will have depended on the support of their family and community in a way that people outside their community may not understand. It is also important to remember that an Indigenous client may be an abuse survivor from the residential school system, so that client (or their family) may have lasting emotional effects from this experience that may affect their care.

As a support worker, you need to realize the possible effects on your clients of being removed from their support system in their community and placed in a long-term care facility. They could feel very isolated. You need to be sensitive to clients in this situation.

Most older people adjust well to the changes occurring in their lives. Many find fulfillment in spending time on hobbies, participating in religious and community activities, and developing new friends. Grandchildren can bring great love and enjoyment, and taking part in family activities not only provides companionship but also can help the older person to feel useful and wanted.

### Estate and Health Care Planning

While some people do financial and health care planning when they are young, there are many others who go through their lives without ever preparing their will, or deciding who to appoint as their substitute decision maker for their personal care or property (see [Chapter 5](../B9780323709392000055/B9780323709392000055.xhtml)). Unfortunately, many older people (or their families) are forced to consider a number of things as they (or their loved one) age or experience health challenges. These considerations may include the following:

1. • Deciding how personal items or properties would be dispersed
2. • Appointing a substitute decision maker for personal care or for property (see [Chapter 5](../B9780323709392000055/B9780323709392000055.xhtml))
3. • Choosing their preferences for end-of life care (see [Chapter 47](../B978032370939200047X/B978032370939200047X.xhtml))
4. • Making funeral arrangements

### Children as Caregivers

Sometimes, parents and children exchange roles, and the adult child cares for the aging parent. This role exchange and dependency on their children can make some older persons feel more secure but make others feel unwanted, in the way, and useless. Some may feel that they have lost their dignity and self-respect, so tensions may develop between the child, parent, and other household members.

From the other perspective, being a caregiver to a parent can be very stressful. The caregiver often has to meet the needs of the parent as well as those of their own children. If the parent has a medical condition, the caregiver has even more responsibilities; they may need to quit work to care for the parent, and the resulting loss of income may cause financial difficulties. Lack of privacy is also a common challenge for both the caregiver and the parent.

### Death of a Partner, Significant Other, or Pet

As a couple grows older, the chances that one partner will die before the other does increase. Women usually live longer than men do, and most women will therefore become widows.

Even when a person tries to be emotionally prepared for a partner’s death, when death occurs, the loss can be devastating (see [Chapter 47](../B978032370939200047X/B978032370939200047X.xhtml)). No amount of preparation is ever enough to cope with the emptiness that results. With that one death, the surviving partner loses a friend, lover, companion, and confidant, so the grief is great and can lead to serious physical and mental health challenges. The grieving partner may lose the will to live, may refuse to eat, may consume unsafe amounts of alcohol, prescribed or unprescribed medications, or may even attempt suicide. Support from family, friends, and religious, social, or recreational groups is very important at this time to help the surviving partner cope.

Some older people can also be devastated by the loss of a close friend or a pet that has been a constant companion for many years. As with the death of a partner, many older people experience profound feelings of sadness and loss in these events.

## Depression in Older Persons

Depression is
not
 an inevitable part of aging, but because of life changes that older persons face—losses of friends, long-term pets, or loved ones; retirement; persistent health challenges, loss of independence; and increased isolation—many older people experience depression.[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur493),[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur494) Signs and symptoms of depression are listed in [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml). While people can experience
depression at any age, depression in older persons may go unrecognized because many of the signs and symptoms occur gradually or may be mistaken for normal signs of aging. In some older people, depression can also indicate other physical or mental health issues such as dementia (see [Chapter 38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml)).

If your client exhibits any signs or symptoms of depression, it is important that you report them to your supervisor. Once depression is recognized, the treatment for it in the older person involves mainly psychological therapy, instead of medication. The client’s physician may also prescribe antidepressant medications, but the client must be closely monitored to observe for adverse effects.

## Physical Changes

Certain physical changes are a normal part of aging and occur in everyone ([BOX 43.1](#b0010)). Some changes are a result of lifestyle choices. Physical processes slow down, and energy level and body efficiency decline. The rate and degree of change vary with each person. Influencing factors include diet, general health, exercise, stress, environment, and heredity. Most of these normal changes are gradual and thus not immediately noticed. Some older people also experience physical changes caused by disease, illness, or injury (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)).

Normal aging does not mean impaired health,
 so quality of life does not have to change with aging. The older person can adjust to the normal changes that occur with age, but some do have at least one persistent illness.

### The Integumentary System

In older adulthood, the skin loses its elasticity, strength, and fatty-tissue layer. As a result, the skin thins and sags, and folds, lines, and wrinkles appear. Dry skin develops because of decreased secretions from oil and sweat glands, so the skin becomes fragile and is easily injured. Skin breakdown, skin tears, and pressure injuries are some of the risks that older persons face (see [Chapter 22](../B9780323709392000225/B9780323709392000225.xhtml)). Brown spots, often called “age spots” or “liver spots,” appear on the skin, usually on the wrists and hands.

As a person ages, blood flow to the skin decreases, resulting in fewer or less effective nerve endings in the skin. The loss of nerve endings interferes with the older person’s ability to sense heat, cold, and pain. Bruising and delayed healing can result from a decreased number of blood vessels.

The loss of fatty tissues beneath the skin increases sensitivity to cold, so the older client needs to be protected from drafts and extreme cold. Sweaters, lap blankets, socks, and higher thermostat settings are often needed.

Nails become thick and tough, and feet usually have poor circulation. A nick or cut can lead to a serious infection (see [Chapter 18](../B9780323709392000183/B9780323709392000183.xhtml) for a description of nail and foot care).

Dry skin is easily damaged and causes itching, so older persons with dry skin should avoid having daily showers or tub baths. A complete bath once a week and partial baths on the other days should be enough to maintain hygiene. When caring for an older client, wash the client’s face with water only, as soap can dry the skin too much. Use mild soaps when washing the other parts of the body. Lotions, oils, and creams are often recommended to prevent drying and itching. Deodorants usually are not needed because sweat gland secretion decreases (see [Chapter 17](../B9780323709392000171/B9780323709392000171.xhtml) for a discussion on hygiene). Be sure to follow the care plan for these clients.

Older clients may complain of cold feet. For warmth, provide socks that are not too tight, to avoid cutting off circulation to the toes. Do not use hot-water bottles and heating pads on the client because of the risk for burns. Fragile skin, poor circulation, and decreased sensitivity to heat contribute to the increased risk for burns. A doctor’s order is necessary for applying heat or cold to your client. When applying either, you must check the area every 15 minutes (see [Chapter 40](../B9780323709392000407/B9780323709392000407.xhtml)). If the area where heat is applied turns red, report it immediately to your supervisor. If the area where you are applying cold appears blue, report it immediately to your supervisor. Report to your supervisor immediately if your client complains of any discomfort.

Most older people have grey or white hair, and some of them may choose to colour their hair. Hair loss and thinning occur in both men and women as they age. Thinning occurs on the head, in the pubic area, and under the arms. Facial hair grows above the lips and on the chin of some older women. Decreased scalp oils lead to dry hair in older persons. Brushing can help stimulate circulation and oil production. The frequency of shampooing is a matter of personal choice and should be done as often as necessary for the client’s hygiene and comfort. Follow the care plan for the client.
Note that some Indigenous people believe their hair should not be put in garbage receptacles but that it should be collected from hairbrushes, sinks, drains, and so on and burned in a sacred fire or in a ceremony.

### The Musculoskeletal System

Muscles atrophy (shrink) and decrease in strength with age. Bones lose strength, become brittle, and break easily, so sometimes just turning in bed can cause fractures (broken bones). Vertebrae shorten, joints become stiff and painful, and hip and knee joints may flex (bend) only slightly. These changes result in a gradual decrease in height, loss of strength, and decreased mobility.

Activity and an appropriate diet can help slow the rate of these changes, so older people need to be as active as possible. A regular exercise program, including range-of-motion (ROM) exercises, is helpful; bathing, dressing, grooming, and other activities of daily living (ADLs) are forms of physical activity that are also helpful (see [Chapter 15](../B9780323709392000158/B9780323709392000158.xhtml)). In addition, an older person’s diet should be high in protein, calcium, and vitamins.

Remember, the bones of older persons can break easily, so protect these clients from injury and take measures to prevent falls (see [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)). Turn and move your client gently and carefully. Some older clients need help and support for getting out of bed or walking.

### The Nervous System

Aging affects the senses—hearing and vision impairment may occur (see [Chapters 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml) and [34](../B9780323709392000341/B9780323709392000341.xhtml) for more details). The senses of taste and smell may become dull; touch, sensitivity to pain and pressure, and the ability to feel heat and cold are reduced. These changes increase the risk for injury, as the older person may not feel pain when ill or injured, or pain may be perceived as only minor. Protect older clients from injury (see [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)), provide skin care, check for signs of skin breakdown, and prevent pressure injuries from developing (see [Chapters 17](../B9780323709392000171/B9780323709392000171.xhtml) and [22](../B9780323709392000225/B9780323709392000225.xhtml)).

Aging affects brain cells, causing them to shrink; also, blood flow to the brain is reduced. These changes affect response time, energy levels, and memory, so reflexes become slower. The client may tire more easily. Forgetfulness may occur but is not a normal part of aging. The reduction in blood flow to the brain may also cause dizziness, which increases the risk for falls. As a support worker, you need to take measures to prevent falls (see [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)). Remind your older clients to rise slowly from their bed or chair to help prevent dizziness.

Sleep patterns may change with age. Some older people have difficulty falling asleep or wake often during the night, so they may rest or nap during the day to prevent fatigue.

### The Circulatory System

The heart muscle weakens with age, so the heart pumps blood through the body with less force. This decreased blood force may not cause problems during rest, but when activity, exercise, excitement, or illness increases the body’s needs for oxygen and nutrients, a weaker heart may not be able to meet them.

In the older person, arteries narrow and become less elastic, so less blood is able to flow through them, causing poor circulation in many body parts. A weak heart must work harder to pump blood through narrowed vessels. Clients with severe circulatory conditions need rest periods throughout the day. They should not engage in activities that cause overexertion—for example, walking long distances, climbing many stairs, or carrying heavy things. Personal-care items, television controls, and other needed items should be kept close at hand.

Moderate daily exercise, however, stimulates circulatory, respiratory, digestive, and musculoskeletal functions and helps prevent blood clots (thrombi) in the leg veins. Some older people are able to engage in activities such as walking, biking, golf, tennis, swimming, and other forms of exercise. Active or passive ROM exercises are necessary for clients on bed rest (see [Chapter 15](../B9780323709392000158/B9780323709392000158.xhtml)). Follow the client’s care plan for exercise and activity levels.

### The Respiratory System

Respiratory muscles weaken with age, and lung tissue becomes less elastic. These changes may not be noticed at rest, but difficult, laboured, or painful breathing (dyspnea; dys means difficult, and pnea
means breathing) may occur with activity. Older clients may lack the strength to cough and clear the airway of secretions, which puts them at risk for respiratory infections (such as pneumonia) and other diseases that can be life-threatening.

The following measures can help promote normal breathing in older clients:

1. • Avoiding use of heavy bed linens over the client’s chest, which can prevent normal chest expansion
2. • Encouraging the client to turn, reposition, and breathe deeply to help prevent respiratory complications that can arise from continuous bed rest
3. • Resting in the semi-Fowler’s position (see [Chapter 14](../B9780323709392000146/B9780323709392000146.xhtml))
4. • Encouraging the client to be as active as possible

### The Digestive System

Changes in the digestive system caused by aging include the following: (1) Salivary glands produce less saliva, causing difficulty swallowing (dysphagia); (2) secretion of digestive juices decreases, resulting in difficulty digesting fried and fatty foods; and (3) peristalsis (the involuntary contractions of intestinal muscles) decreases, so the stomach and colon empty at a slower rate, leading to flatulence and constipation. Other physical changes such as dulled senses of taste and smell can affect appetite and the enjoyment of food. Some older persons may have lost teeth from illness or lack of adequate dental care. This loss of teeth and the presence of ill-fitting dentures can make chewing difficult (see [Chapter 27](../B9780323709392000274/B9780323709392000274.xhtml) for a discussion on diet changes that help older people address eating and digestive issues).

### The Urinary System

Kidney function declines with age. Blood flow to the kidneys is reduced, and the kidneys atrophy. As a result, the removal of body wastes is less efficient, and urine is more concentrated. Not drinking enough fluids can also cause urine to become more concentrated.

Since bladder muscles weaken and the size of the bladder decreases, it is able to hold very little urine, resulting in urinary frequency or urgency. Some older persons experience nocturia, which is the need to urinate several times during the night. In some others, urinary incontinence (the inability to control passing urine) may occur, but it is not a normal part of aging (see [Chapter 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml)).

In older men, the prostate gland enlarges, which causes pressure on the urethra. As a result, difficulty urinating and frequent urination are common issues.

Older people, especially those with female genitalia, are at risk for urinary tract infections (UTIs). To avoid them, clients should drink adequate fluids, including water, fruit juices, and milk. As a support worker, you need to allow clients personal choice in their beverages. Most fluids should be ingested before 1700 hours (5:00 p.m.) to help reduce the need to urinate during the night. Bladder control training programs may be necessary for those with urinary incontinence. However, fluids should never be restricted just to reduce the need to urinate at night (see [Chapter 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml) for a discussion of urinary elimination).

### The Reproductive System

In older persons with male genitalia, the hormone testosterone decreases. Since the decrease in this hormone affects physical strength, sperm production, and reproductive tissues, it has a negative impact on sexual activity. It takes longer for erection and orgasm to occur, and orgasm is weaker than in younger years. The erection is lost quickly after orgasm, and the time between erections lengthens. Older persons with male genitalia may need to have their penis stimulated for sexual arousal. These changes can result in decreased frequency of sexual activity.

During perineal care, your client with male genitalia may develop an erection, which is a normal physical reaction to the wiping of sensitive perineal nerve endings. Bringing attention to the client’s physical reaction may cause them embarrassment. Simply provide privacy by covering the penis with a towel and continue wiping in another area as needed. When the erection is over, you may resume cleansing the client’s perineal area.

Mental and physical fatigue, overeating, and excessive drinking can also affect the ability to have erections. Some men fear performance impairment and may therefore avoid sexual activity.

In those with female genitalia, menopause occurs around 50 years of age. At menopause, an individual with female genitalia stops menstruating, and the reproductive years come to an end, as ova cease to be produced. Female hormone (estrogen and progesterone)
levels decrease, and this reduction affects reproductive tissues. The uterus, the vagina, and the external genitalia all begin to atrophy, and breasts become less firm. Intercourse may be uncomfortable or painful because of thinning vaginal walls and vaginal dryness. Older individuals with female genitalia also experience changes in sexual excitement. Arousal takes longer, and the time between arousal and orgasm is longer. Orgasm is less intense, and the pre-excitement state returns more quickly.

Frequency of sexual activity decreases for many older intimate couples because of weakness, mental and physical fatigue, pain, persistent illness, and reduced mobility.

## The Older Person and Sexuality

Love, affection, and intimacy are needed throughout life, as sexuality is part of the whole person. These needs do not disappear ([FIGURE 43.4](#f0025)); older people have the right—and it is normal—to be sexual and to form sexual relationships.

Some older people are not able to have sexual intercourse, but this inability does not mean that they have lost their sexual needs or desires. They can express their feelings in other ways—for example, hand holding, touching, caressing, and embracing—that can bring closeness and intimacy.

Residents’ Sexual Rights

Couples of any description who are in an intimate union in long-term care facilities are allowed to share the same room if one is available. Long-term care legislation in some provinces even requires it (see [Chapter 5](../B9780323709392000055/B9780323709392000055.xhtml)). A couple who have lived together for many years have the right to stay together in a facility when possible and can share the same bed if their conditions permit. Sometimes family members are embarrassed by the sexual expression of their parents and may not see the need to provide privacy for their parents’ need for sexual expression.

It is important to demonstrate respect for the couple by always knocking before you enter their room. If you do happen to find clients engaging in intimate activity, show respect by politely excusing yourself from the room, closing the door, and allowing the couple some uninterrupted time together. Do the same for any client you find masturbating. Politely excuse yourself, provide privacy, and allow the client some uninterrupted time alone. It would be disrespectful and unprofessional to bring up this sexual activity to the client(s) or your co-workers in a joking manner.

In some cases, couples may have lived apart and may not want to share a room. As a support worker, you must respect their decision. After losing a sexual partner through death or divorce, a resident may develop a relationship with another resident. This couple, too, have the right to spend time together.

As a support worker, you must keep in mind that clients who are cognitively impaired could become victims of sexual abuse. They could mistake someone else for their spouse. If you find an inappropriate person in your client’s room, ensure that you record and report the finding to your supervisor.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Retirement affects older persons in many ways. Some people may be happy to retire and look forward to more leisure time, whereas others may have been forced into retirement because of a persistent illness or a disability. As a support worker, be sensitive to your client’s reactions and their effect on the family.

• Financial concerns can cause stress to your client and their family. In most cases, income will be reduced after retirement, and in some cases, expenses may go up because of lack of insurance coverage for medications. Older clients still need to deal with the expenses of living.

• Social interactions change as a person ages. Children grow up and may move away. In some cases, they become the caregivers for their parents. A client who has been living in their home and then needs to move to supportive housing may lose contact with friends.

• Depression is not a normal part of aging. As a support worker, you need to look for the signs and symptoms of depression in clients and report any to your supervisor. Psychological therapy is the main treatment for depression in the older person.

• Keep in mind that numerous changes occur in an older person’s body systems. Your supervisor will direct your care for your client. Changes occur in the (1) integumentary, (2) musculoskeletal, (3) nervous, (4) circulatory, (5) respiratory, (6) digestive, (7) urinary, and (8) reproductive systems of older persons.

• Love, affection, and intimacy are needed throughout life, as sexuality is part of the whole person. There are ways you, as a support worker, can respect and promote your client’s sexuality.

Occasionally, you may also be required to take care of clients’ pets, such as walking the dog if the client is unable to. Whatever your tasks are, they will be specified on each client’s care plan. Some clients are on
cytotoxic drugs
 (primarily used to treat cancer) such as antineoplastic drugs (see [Chapter 45](../B9780323709392000456/B9780323709392000456.xhtml)), so it is important to be aware of your responsibility for the safe handling of any such items that have come into direct contact with the client. See the Think About Safety: Guidelines for Safely Assisting Clients Who Are on Cytotoxic Drugs box.

You will not be assigned heavy housecleaning tasks, such as washing windows or cleaning carpets. In some areas, clients must pay for any home management services unless they are also receiving personal support. Hospitals and long-term care facilities might also have support workers do basic housekeeping tasks. However, most facilities have a housekeeping staff. This chapter focuses on home management in community care settings.

Guidelines for Safely Assisting Clients Who Are on Cytotoxic Drugs

It is possible that you will be responsible for a client who is being treated with a cytotoxic drug such as an antineoplastic (or chemotherapy) drug (see [Chapter 45](../B9780323709392000456/B9780323709392000456.xhtml)). Items that have come into direct contact with cytotoxic drugs or the body fluids from a client who is receiving them can potentially pose serious health risks to others for up to 7 days after discontinuation of the medication. This is known as cytotoxic contamination. Examples of items that can be contaminated include incontinence products, toileting surfaces, eating utensils, linen, and clothing.

Recommendations for providing safe care include the following:

1. • Wear protective gloves while handling waste containers. Cytotoxic waste must be handled differently than regular garbage, and must be disposed of according to provincial regulations.
2. • Use proper personal protective equipment (PPE), which includes:
3. 1. • Protective gloves made of vinyl or nitrile rubber. Gloves should be changed frequently, or immediately if punctured, cut, or torn. It is also recommended that workers wear two pairs of gloves at a time, for additional protection.
   3. • A moisture-resistant, long-sleeved gown with elastic cuffs.
4. • Discard gloves and gowns after each use.
5. • Wear gloves and a disposable gown to provide personal care and to handle soiled laundry. Place the laundry into a waterproof bag that is separate from other laundry.
6. • Place all sharps in puncture-proof containers before bagging.
7. • Encourage the client to use a separate toilet, urinal, or commode chair. Personal items should be stored at the client’s bedside.
8. • Flush toilet contents twice after each use.
9. • Clean all reusable equipment and surfaces twice, using a hospital-grade disinfectant.
10. • Encourage men to void while sitting, to prevent splashing contaminated urine.
11. • Advise the client to discard their toothbrush after completion of their drug therapy.
12. • Follow all workplace policies regarding hazardous waste disposal of cytotoxic drug preparation.
13. • Refrain from wearing your protective clothing outside of the preparation area.
14. • Provide proper training of any staff who may handle cytotoxic drugs or waste by-products created by their use, which includes the following:
15. 1. • Hazards of cytotoxic drugs
    2. • Use and disposal procedures
    3. • Safe client care
    4. • Proper use of protective equipment
    5. • Spill procedures
    6. • Maintenance of the facilities and equipment

## The Support Worker’s Role in Home Management

A clean and orderly setting is important for health and safety. Dust, dirt, and damp areas promote the growth of microbes, and clutter can cause falls and serious injury. People who have illnesses or disabilities, those recovering from surgery or injuries, new parents, caregivers tending to sick family members, and older persons may not be able to do regular housekeeping.

### Who Pays for What Services?

The client, family, and case manager decide what household tasks need to be done and list them in the client’s care plan. Clients are usually responsible for paying for these services out of the money they are allotted for home care. In some circumstances and in some provinces and territories, however, the funding agency is billed directly by the agency providing the services, without the client’s involvement. Whereas tasks such as regular cleaning and laundry are funded by home care services, some tasks, such as pet care, are not funded and so are paid for separately by the client or family.

While as the support worker you must complete the tasks listed in the care plan, there will be occasions when you will have to prioritize tasks. For example, one of your tasks is to straighten and clean the bathroom after you have helped Mrs. Jacob with personal care. However, since Mrs. Jacob is incontinent of urine, you need to provide skin care and help her change into clean clothes. The client’s immediate personal care needs take priority.

Whatever the household task, remember the principles of compassionate care (see the Providing Compassionate Care: Home Management box).

Home Management

* •
  Dignity
   is the state of feeling worthy, valued, and respected. All people deserve a neat, clean, peaceful setting, but illness, disability, surgery, or injury may limit a person’s ability to clean. Helping with home management promotes comfort, emotional well-being, and dignity. Treat your client’s home and belongings with respect and care.
* •
  Independence
   is the state of not depending on others for control or authority. For some clients, accepting help means a loss of control. Respect your client’s wishes for independence and control. As they recover, some clients gradually assume responsibility for home management. Occupational therapists help some clients learn new ways to perform household tasks. For example, Mr. Ho, who had an arm amputated, is trying to make his bed using techniques he learned in occupational therapy, but his progress is slow. Do not make the bed for him. Be patient, and praise him for trying and for the amount of work he has done.
* •
  Individualized care.
   For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. In some circumstances, although you may be expected to do tasks that are listed in the client’s care/service plan, some tasks may not be done if the client’s condition suggests they are inappropriate or if the client refuses to have them done.
* •
  Preferences
   are the rights to select or the act of selecting someone or something over another or others. Follow your client’s preferred methods for household tasks, which will be listed in the care plan. Respect that all people have different preferences and standards. For example, Mrs. Neal may not show any interest in having a neat bedroom, but she may not tolerate even a tiny stain on her blouse. Use the client’s preferred cleaning products—for example, a client may use vinegar and water instead of a commercial floor cleaning product.
* •
  Privacy
   is the state of being free from unsanctioned intrusion, as well as the degree to which an individual can determine which personal information is to be shared with whom and for what purpose. It is one of the most important and comprehensive of all human rights, and yet it is one of the hardest to protect.
* Some clients or families may view your presence as an intrusion. Find out if there are any areas of the home that the client considers private or “off limits” to you. If there are rooms, parts of rooms, or items that a client does not want touched or cleaned, respect these wishes. Never judge or criticize a client’s housekeeping standards. Never talk to others about the cleanliness of a client’s house. Doing so would be unprofessional and unethical.
* •
  Safety
   is the state of being free from actual, threatened, or imagined danger, risk, or injury. Some cleaning solutions have Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System (WHMIS) labels (see [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)). Read these and other product labels carefully. Follow all use and storage instructions. Do not let people walk on mopped floors, which may be slippery and cause a fall. Put away buckets and cleaning solutions when you are finished with them. Use proper personal protective equipment (PPE) at all times. Store cleaning solutions out of reach of children and of adults with dementia. Clear away clutter. If the client’s safety is at risk, talk to your supervisor about this.

### Assisting the Client With Tasks Involving Their Money

As a support worker, you may occasionally be required to accompany your client to do personal banking, buy groceries, or pay bills. When assisting your client with these tasks, you should keep the following points in mind:

1. • This duty must be clearly written in the client’s care plan and must follow the policies of your
   employing agency. The care plan should specify exactly what your responsibilities are. Some clients with severe arthritis, for example, may just need assistance with handling their money because they cannot hold it without dropping it. Others may need you to pick up some groceries and provide an account of the purchases.
2. • Your supervisor should be able to answer any questions that you might have about your agency’s policies regarding financial responsibilities.
3. • You should not agree to anything that is not specified on your client’s care plan. If you have any concerns or questions, consult your supervisor.
4. • All money that you handle should be recorded in the appropriate manner. Some agencies, for example, record all transactions on a page to which the support worker is required to attach receipts. When you are given money to purchase an item, you will be responsible to record how much cash was given to you and the amount of change that you are returning. You should be aware of your particular responsibilities in this matter.
5. • Always provide receipts for any transaction that you did on your client’s behalf. If your agency’s policy is to notify your supervisor before and after banking, you should do so promptly.
6. • Remember that the client’s personal identification number (PIN) and bank account numbers are private, and you should NOT have access to them. If the client has difficulty typing in the numbers, for example, a banking associate should be asked to do so. You should step far enough away that you do not hear the numbers.
7. • Many agencies do not allow support workers to assist the client in doing personal banking and, instead, request that family members (or a court-appointed representative) take on this responsibility. This policy protects support workers from being accused in the case of any money being lost. However, if you are self-employed and are asked to assist the client with personal banking or shopping, you will not have a supervisor to contact if a situation arises. It is important that you have a clear understanding of what your expected duties are and that you keep very accurate records of what you have done and where the client has put their money.

### Dealing With Conflicting Demands

Many people have set routines and want things done in certain ways. However, because of time constraints or for health and safety reasons, it may not be possible for you to follow the set procedure. Or you may have to deal with conflicting demands if the care plan says one thing but the client (or the family) wants something different (see the Supporting Mrs. Yeung: Conflicting Home Management Demands and Supporting Mrs. Washington: Part 1: Using the Client’s Home Equipment Safely boxes).

If you are asked to do additional tasks that are not in the client’s care plan, it is important that you inform the client (or the family) in a professional, respectful, and calm manner that this task was not identified on the client’s care plan. However, without making any commitments to the client related to the request, you should try to identify why the request was made and report this request to your supervisor. Listen to your client; if a conflict arises between the client’s preferences and the care plan, discuss the conflict with your supervisor. Also, discuss with your supervisor any complaints the client may have.

## Getting Organized

Usually, home management tasks are assigned in addition to personal care and other tasks. The key to completing home management tasks as well as your other duties is to use your time wisely. Review the time management skills described in [Chapter 8](../B9780323709392000080/B9780323709392000080.xhtml). Follow the guidelines in [BOX 44.1](#b0010) and these general points:

1. •
   Set priorities.
    Follow the care plan. Do the most important tasks first.
2. •
   Set a routine.
    Discuss the routine with your client. Agreeing on the routine will help you manage your time.
3. •
   Gather all supplies beforehand.
    By doing so, you will avoid wasting time and energy.
4. •
   Use your time well. Start with tasks that have waiting periods or that run automatically. For example, if laundry and personal care activities are on your list, start the laundry first. Leave soiled linens to soak while you help your client with their bath.
5. •
   Finish tasks and put items away.
    Finish tasks that you have started. After finishing, put all items away in their proper places.
6. •
   Set time limits for each task.
    Having a time limit helps you stay organized and finish on time.
7. •
   Focus on the task.
    Do not let your mind wander. For example, do not carelessly wipe a counter while talking to your client. Focus on the person first. Then clean the counter properly.
8. •
   Put the client’s needs first.
    Remember, your first priority is to promote the client’s comfort, well-being, and safety. Follow the client’s care plan.

## Using Cleaning Products Safely

To avoid harm to clients (and to their family), it is important to follow the safety guidelines listed on any cleaning product label. Cleaning products can cause harm if not used and stored properly. If you have any questions about the safety guidelines, you can look the products up on your smartphone or ask your supervisor. Remember the following points:

1. •
   Read all labels carefully.
    Follow manufacturers’ instructions. Be familiar with the WHMIS hazard labels (see [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)).
2. •
   Wear the appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE).
3. •
   Never mix cleaning products.
    Some products contain chemicals that create poisonous fumes when mixed. Mixing anything containing bleach and ammonia, for example, produces a deadly gas called chlorine. Remember to read product labels and to follow the instructions on the container.
4. •
   Choose cleaning products that are safest to use.
    Choose products that are ecologically friendly. Many people have sensitivities or allergies to scents or to the products themselves, so avoid using highly scented products or harsh chemicals.
5. •
   Wear utility gloves when using cleaning products.
    Do not use disposable gloves. They are too thin and can easily puncture; therefore, they would not protect your skin from harsh chemicals.
6. •
   Use caution when using cleaning products and chemicals to avoid splashing.
    If any chemical does splash and land on your skin (or in your eye), you should know how to treat it; therefore, you should always read the label before you use the product.
   Report any splashes to your supervisor.
7. •
   Never use products in unlabelled containers.
8. •
   Store products in their original containers.
9. •
   Keep cleaning products away from food.
10. •
    Keep cleaning products out of reach of children and away from adults with dementia.
11. •
    Use products only for their intended purpose. Some products can harm the surfaces you are trying to clean!
12. •
    Keep aerosol cans away from heat sources.
13. •
    Ask the client before using a strong cleanser on a surface.
14. •
    Rinse strong, abrasive cleansers immediately after use.
15. •
    Do not scrub vigorously.
     You could damage a delicate surface.

### Cleaning Bathrooms

Bathrooms need special attention because microbes easily grow and spread in damp places, so family members must keep bathrooms clean. ### Mould and Mildew

Mould is a microscopic fungal parasite, and mildew is the black discoloration caused by mould that can cause a foul odour. People living in homes that have mould and damp conditions are more likely to experience the following:[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur495)

1. • Eye, nose, and throat irritation
2. • Coughing and phlegm build-up
3. • Wheezing and shortness of breath
4. • Worsening of asthma symptoms

Surfaces in all rooms should always be dried well to prevent growth of mould and mildew. Surface mould, or small to moderate amounts of mould, can be cleaned with water and dish detergent. Do not use bleach to remove mould. You should wear protective equipment, including waterproof utility gloves, eye protection, and a mask, when removing mould. If there is a lot of mould present, your supervisor may discuss with the client the need to address the underlying cause. Professional mould abatement specialists may be required to remove extensive mould growth. Sensitive individuals should not be in the same room and may choose to leave the house until the mould is removed.[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur496)

## Doing Laundry

When you are doing laundry, your goal is to clean clothing items without damaging them. Many clients’ houses and apartments will have laundry facilities. If not, a laundromat has to be used, and you will need the right amount of change or a designated prepaid card to operate the washing machines and dryers. Find out if the client keeps change or has a card for this purpose.

Ask your client (or family member) which detergent, bleach, and fabric softener to use. Also, find out if there are any special laundry instructions. For instance, are garments air-dried or dried in a dryer? Follow your client’s preferences. [BOX 44.6](#b0035) describes how to do laundry properly.

### Laundry Soiled With Body Substances

Soiling from blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions is an
infection control issue
. You should always follow employer policy and Standard Practices (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)) when handling, transporting, or processing laundry soiled with these substances. Do the following when handling laundry soiled with body substances:

1. • Practise hand hygiene frequently.
2. • Wear gloves at all times when handling the soiled laundry. Remove the gloves properly (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)) and discard them in the appropriate container.
3. • Place soiled laundry in leak-proof plastic bags. Secure bags to prevent spillage.
4. • Keep soiled laundry away from other laundry.
5. • Double-bag laundry if the outside of the first bag becomes contaminated with body substances.
6. • If the client has frequent watery or loose stools, or soils the bedding and clothing frequently, that client may be ill and may require medical attention. Report this observation immediately to your supervisor! Soiled linens should always be double-bagged and labelled as a biohazard.
7. • Clients on chemotherapy who are incontinent of either urine or feces must have their linens double-bagged separately from all other laundry items and labelled as a biohazard. Check the care plan, as this linen may have special care instructions.

### Removing Stains

Numerous stain treatment products are on the market, and most do remove stains effectively when their instructions are followed carefully. If the client does not have any stain-removing products and you are asked to remove stains from clothing, alternative cleaners (such as a paste of baking soda and water) might be useful. You should never attempt to remove stains without the client’s permission. Also, apply the stain treatment on an area that is not readily seen (such as an inside seam) to ensure it will not damage that article of clothing.

Stains caused by bodily fluids (urine, vomitus, or blood) or solids (stool) should always be rinsed in cold water. Rinsing them in hot water causes the protein in the bodily fluid or solid to change, much like how an egg changes its appearance when it is cooked. Without soaking in cold water, stains will be almost impossible to remove in the future.

When removing stains, handle chemicals carefully (see [BOX 44.7](#b0040)). Follow the label instructions. Remember that both chlorine bleach and ammonia are poisonous. Each of them, by themselves, can burn or irritate the eyes and skin. Take care not to inhale ammonia fumes; open a window when using ammonia.
Remember never to mix chlorine bleach and ammonia because doing so creates a toxic gas.

Before using bleach, check the care instructions on a garment. Also, be aware that some people are allergic to chemicals in cleaning products. Check the care plan. If you are not sure, ask your supervisor.

## Recycling Items and Composting

Most municipalities in Canada encourage residents to recycle their waste cardboard and paper, cans, jars, and other items. You should be aware of the items that are allowed to be recycled in the client’s area, as each municipality has its own rules and specifics regarding recycling.

Some areas specify that certain types of waste must be disposed of in a container of a specific colour. For example, one municipality may insist that paper and cardboard be placed in a red container, cans and jars in a blue container, and wet waste that can be composted in a green container. If the client lives in an apartment building, you may be responsible for taking the recycling items to the assigned disposal area and putting them into their appropriate bins ([FIGURE 44.4](#f0025)).

Recycling and composting reduces the amount of waste that would eventually fill our dumpsites and landfills and is an environmentally responsible action all of us can participate in.

## Performing Tasks not Included on the Client’s Care Plan

Occasionally, you might be asked to perform a task not included on the client’s care plan. Some of the tasks might seem easy to do, but others might not. For example, one client may ask you to make a sandwich, even though meal preparation is not on that client’s care plan. Another client may ask you to wash the windows so that the client can clearly see the birds coming to the birdfeeder.

You should remember that you will not be paid for performing any tasks that are not on the client’s care plan. Some employers frown on the support worker providing “free” services (i.e., services that the client would normally have to pay your agency for you to perform). Some agencies’ insurance companies will not compensate you if you are injured while performing a task that is not on your client’s care plan. You should also be aware that if you provide “free” services, the client will probably expect the same services from other support workers in the future!

Some clients (or their friends or family) will see you as a source of free services (see the Supporting Mrs. Washington: Part 4: Adhering to the Task List box). You should know how to respond to the client or others when you are asked to perform a task that is not on the care plan. Remember that you should always be professional, calm, and respectful if you must refuse someone’s request for unpaid services.

### Consult With Your Supervisor

Before performing a task that is not on the client’s care plan, it is always a good idea to consult your supervisor. Your supervisor is aware of your agency’s policies regarding such situations. Occasionally, your supervisor will say that it is “up to you,” especially if the request is not time-consuming or does not require special skills. For example, your client may ask you to
feed the bird while you are near the canary’s cage. This task is neither complicated nor time-consuming, so your supervisor might very well allow you to do it. Other favours, however, can be time-consuming and may put you behind in your schedule if you have other clients to see that day.

Some “favours” asked may be beyond your scope of practice, and you should
never be persuaded to perform them
. For example, since Mrs. Patel’s arthritis is acting up and she cannot handle her insulin syringe, she asks you to help her with it. In this situation, it is important that you call your supervisor immediately and consult with them, as there is considerable risk of harming the client. Your supervisor may go to the client’s house to perform the task or send a nurse over instead.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• The support worker may be required to assist in home management services—tidying and cleaning rooms, vacuuming, dusting, washing dishes, making beds, doing laundry—to enhance the health and well-being of a client. Support workers are not assigned heavy housecleaning tasks such as washing windows or cleaning carpets.

• Some clients are on cytotoxic drugs (primarily used to treat cancer) such as antineoplastic drugs (see [Chapter 45](../B9780323709392000456/B9780323709392000456.xhtml)), so it is important to be aware of your responsibility for the safe handling of any items that have come into direct contact with the client. The support worker’s tasks will be specified on each client’s care plan.

• Cleaning products should be used safely and stored properly. Remember the following points: (1) read all labels carefully; (2) never mix cleaning products; (3) wear utility gloves when using cleaning products; (4) never store products in unlabelled containers; (5) store products in their original containers; (6) keep cleaning products away from food; (7) be careful never to splash bleach or other chemicals; (8) keep cleaning products out of the reach of children and persons with dementia; (9) use products only for their intended purpose; (10) keep aerosol cans away from heat sources; (11) ask the client for permission before using a strong cleanser on any surface; (12) rinse off harsh or strong cleansers after using; and (13) do not scrub a surface vigorously.

• Specific guidelines for home management and cleaning include (1) clearing away clutter; (2) working from higher to lower; (3) working from far to near; (4) working from dry to wet; (5) working from cleanest to dirtiest; (6) changing cleaning cloths and water frequently; (7) using a damp cloth for dusting; (8) rinsing and drying washed surfaces; and (9) avoiding soiling a clean floor.

• Dry all surfaces well to prevent growth of mould and mildew. Surface mould can be cleaned with water and dish detergent. Do not use bleach to remove mould.

• You should be aware of specific stain-removing guidelines when doing laundry.

• You should be aware of the recycling guidelines in the client’s residential area.

## Scope Of Practice: The Support Worker’s Role

As a support worker, your role in medication management depends on your provincial or territorial legislation, your employer’s policy, and your training and education. (Refer to [Chapter 5](../B9780323709392000055/B9780323709392000055.xhtml) for more information about the laws that govern support workers across Canada.) Ensure that you are clear about what your duties include and that you have been taught how to perform each of the tasks that you are expected to do. In many areas, for example, oxygen therapy is considered to be a medication, since there is great potential risk for harm to a client. Check with your employer or supervisor should you have any questions.

Your role may involve one or more of the following (see the [Think About Safety: Guidelines for Assisting Clients With Medications](#b0035) box):

1. • Reminding the client to take a medication
2. • Bringing medication containers to the client
3. • Bringing pre-poured medications, prefilled syringes, blister packs, or dosettes (pillboxes) to the client
4. • Ensuring the medication is the correct one
5. • Reading the prescription label to the client
6. • Loosening or removing container lids or opening blister packs
7. • Checking the dosage on the medication label with the client’s medication administration record (MAR)
8. • Providing water or other fluids, as needed
9. • Supervising the client as the client places the medication into their hand, measuring spoon (or oral syringe), or cup.
10. • Steadying the client’s hand while the client places medications or administers eye drops, nasal sprays, and other medications
11. • Documenting in the client’s MAR the medications given, according to your agency’s policy

You are not responsible for monitoring the outcome of the drug therapy. The physician, nurse, or case manager has this responsibility. However, you must observe for and report any changes in the client’s condition or behaviour.

It is the role of the nurse or pharmacist to provide health teaching to the client about each medication they are taking. Clients then should be able to take
medications accurately and know the medications’ desired effects, when and how to take the medications, any adverse effects to watch for, and foods or other medications to avoid. If your client does not have this information, notify your supervisor.

BOX 45.1 Assisting With Versus Administering Medications

Assisting with medications and administering medications are two very different functions. Assist means “to help”; administer means “to give.”

Assisting with medications involves helping clients self-administer medications. For example, you hand them their medications, or you open the bottles or packages for them. Such tasks are strictly a mechanical function; that is, you perform the functions clients would normally perform with their own hands and feet to obtain the medication.

Administering medications involves measuring medications or getting them into the person’s body, and it requires special judgement and knowledge. Clients and residents in facilities usually have medications administered to them, and some home care clients also need health care providers to administer their medications. However, administering medications is beyond your scope of practice. In some provinces and territories,
in some special circumstances
, you may be asked by your supervisor to perform a controlled act, such as giving an insulin injection or administering some forms of medication to certain clients.
You must never just assume this responsibility, as it is beyond your usual scope of practice. If you are asked to administer medication, it is your employer’s responsibility to ensure that you are formally trained, supervised, and monitored by a registered staff member. It is then your responsibility to ask questions for clarification and to safely follow the instructions given. This function must be in your job description, as part of your employment agreement. Remember that you are ultimately responsible for any medications that you administer to a client!

A small mistake when assisting with or administering medications can cause serious harm to the client. Follow employer policies and provincial or territorial laws to protect your clients, your employer, and yourself. When assisting with medications, follow the “rights” of medication management (page 1071). Check the care plan to see how much assistance the client requires.

## How Medications Work in the Body

A medication (drug) is a chemical substance that causes a specific effect on the body and is prescribed for and taken by people to obtain various desired results in the body. Some medications are made in a laboratory, and others are created from mixing specific natural ingredients together by a traditional healer. See [BOX 45.2](#b0015)
: Traditional and Modern Medication Practices.

Some medications might treat illness or pain—for example, ibuprofen to reduce the pain of a headache. Other medications promote health—for example, a stool softener to help reduce discomfort during bowel movement. And some medications prevent illness—for example, an antibiotic to prevent an infection after surgery.

BOX 45.2 Traditional and Modern Medication Practices

Traditional Indigenous approaches to healing are based in a worldview that integrates the mental, social, spiritual, physical, and ecological dimensions of health and well-being of people. The goal of traditional healers has always been to attain for their “patients” a biochemical balance that could restore wellness, provide rapid recovery, and eliminate the acute or chronic adverse effects associated with modern synthetic medications. A client’s family who want to use traditional remedies should be encouraged to inform the team so they are all aware and there are no potential interactions with other allopathic/Western medicines.

In addition to herbal treatments, traditional healers have often used other therapeutic remedies and practices, such as special diets, fasting, massage, the use of enemas, and heat and cold therapies such as sweat baths, hydrotherapy, and mud, clay and charcoal poultices (see [Chapter 40](../B9780323709392000407/B9780323709392000407.xhtml)). Health care workers who are unfamiliar with traditional healing methods can learn from seeing some of these practices done, such as the application of pitch to a wound.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur500)

Throughout most of the twentieth century, most traditional healers and their treatments were generally looked down upon by governments, scholastic institutions, and medical doctors. It was assumed that such practices would either fade away or simply be totally displaced by “superior” high-technology medicine. This has been proven wrong, however. In an article written for The Globe and Mail, Kevin Berube wrote:

“Many of the medicines found in plants, trees, herbs, soils and water that are used in First Nations healing have counterparts on conventional pharmacy shelves. These traditional or natural medicines are used to combat cold, flu, joint pain, gastrointestinal ailments, headache, insomnia, infection, skin conditions, blood diseases, cancer and many other illnesses. As with any medicine, a traditional healer recommends a specific dosage, preparation, and administration. Throughout Canada, a vision for healthy communities in all rural, remote and northern communities is replacing the western medicine disease model and returning to traditional medicine practices based on wellness.”[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur502)

### Drug Action

Medications enter the body in a variety of ways. How the medication enters the body (so that it can start working) is known as the route. [TABLE 45.1](#t0010) lists some types of medications and their routes. Medication such as injections and intravenous medications are not included in the table because you will not be responsible for assisting with or administering them.

Depending on the route, the body must absorb and distribute the drug so that the drug can work. Absorption and distribution are also necessary to metabolize the drug so that the body can rid itself of the remaining materials (molecules of the drug the body no longer needs)—that is, excrete them from the body. For example, the blood pressure pill that a client swallows must enter the stomach, be broken down into small molecules, enter the bloodstream (absorption), and travel throughout the body to reach its intended tissues (distribution) so that it can cause the intended effect. After the drug has done what it was intended to do in the body, it must then be broken down into smaller waste particles and sometimes converted into other substances the body can use (i.e., metabolization, which usually takes place in the liver), and then be removed from the body, usually in the stool or in the urine (excretion).

### Drug Interactions

The way that medications interact with other drugs or substances in the body (e.g., alcohol) is also important, as mixing these substances may cause an undesirable effect on the client, known as a drug interaction. For example, a client might take an over-the-counter (OTC) medication, herbal supplement, or vitamin, and, depending on its nature, it might interact with the prescription medication that the client’s doctor or nurse practitioner prescribed.

The combined effect of two drugs being greater than the effect of either drug given alone is known as drug synergism. This combined effect can cause the client to become extremely drowsy, hyperactive, or nauseated. When combined, some medications can even affect that client’s heart function or blood pressure. For example, taking an antihistamine when a narcotic is active in the person’s system will make a person much more drowsy than if only one of the medications was taken.

Conversely, some medications will counteract the actions of other drugs when combined; this is called drug antagonism. For example, an antacid might prevent an antibiotic or birth control pill from being absorbed in the stomach.

Clients on prescribed medications should never drink alcohol or take OTC medications without first consulting a doctor, pharmacist, or nurse, as serious harm may result from combining medications and other drugs or alcohol.

Taking more than the prescribed dosage of medication or taking too many medications is called polypharmacy and can cause serious health issues. Suspected polypharmacy should always be reported to your supervisor. See the Supporting Mrs. Lefcoe: The Effect of Polypharmacy on a Client box.

## Types Of Medications

Medications come in many forms (see [TABLE 45.1](#t0010)). Clients may take:

Clients might ask support workers to purchase or obtain OTC medications or alternative remedies for them. Refuse respectfully but firmly. Inform your supervisor of the client’s request.

Medications can have topical, local, or systemic effects, according to where in the body the medication works. A cream that you rub on your skin might have a topical effect—that is, the cream works only on the skin surface. The pill that you take for your headache might have a systemic effect—that is, it might also help relieve pain elsewhere in the body.

### Factors Affecting Drug Action

Drugs should be stored in a dry, safe place so that they do not undergo any chemical change. When managing a client’s medications, you must read the label on the drug container and follow the instructions carefully, such as if the container needs to be shaken well prior to use.

The same drug can affect two people very differently, because one person’s body cells differ from those of another person, according to age, body size, gender, genetic factors, emotional state, and physical condition. Opioid Analgesics and Naloxone

1. • When taken carefully in prescribed dosages for extreme pain, opioid analgesics (such as fentanyl, morphine, oxycodone, or codeine) are usually safe for the client. However, if taken in higher-than-ordered doses or if mixed with another opioid narcotic, they can depress the client’s respiration rate and depth of respiration by targeting certain receptors in the brain.
2. • Naloxone (also called Narcan) is a fast-acting drug used to temporarily reverse the effects of opioid overdoses. It works by kicking opioids off the receptors in the brain and binding to those receptors instead. This reverses the effects of opioids on the body.
3. • Naloxone can restore breathing within 2 to 5 minutes.
4. • Naloxone only works with opioids in the system. It will not work with other type of non-opioid substances.
5. • While naloxone is only active in the body for 20 to 90 minutes, the effects of most opioids last longer.
6. • This means that the effects of naloxone are likely to wear off before the opioids are gone from the body, which causes breathing to stop again.
7. • Naloxone may need to be used again, depending on the amount or type of opioid taken, or how the opioids were taken (for example, oral, injection).
8. • Naloxone is safe for clients of all ages. It does not create dependence.

### Drug Allergy

A drug allergy is an abnormal response to a drug caused by the body fighting or attacking the drug (which is called an antigen when such a response occurs) by releasing chemicals called antibodies. Antibodies attack the antigen and can cause symptoms ranging from mild (skin rashes, swelling, puffiness, nasal drainage, itchy eyes) to moderate (fever, wheezing, extreme weakness, nausea, and vomiting) to severe (anaphylactic shock, severe low blood pressure, cardiac arrest).

Anaphylaxis—from the Greek ana (without) and phylaxis (protection)—is a severe sensitivity to an antigen (allergic reaction) that occurs rapidly and causes a life-threatening response involving the whole body. This reaction can lead to difficulty breathing and shock and can ultimately lead to death. In severe cases, anaphylactic shock can occur within seconds. Signs and symptoms include the following:

1. • Severe restlessness
2. • Increased anxiety
3. • Possible combativeness (from struggling to breathe)
4. • Sweating
5. • Shortness of breath
6. • Low blood pressure
7. • Irregular pulse
8. • Respiratory congestion
9. • Swelling of the larynx (laryngeal edema)
10. • Hoarseness
11. • Dyspnea

Anaphylactic shock is an emergency, and the emergency medical service (EMS) system must be activated immediately. The client needs special medications such as an epinephrine auto-injector to reverse the allergic reaction. Until emergency help arrives, keep the client lying down, ensuring that the airways are open. If cardiac arrest occurs, you must initiate cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) at once.

## Documentation

Refer to [Chapter 26](../B9780323709392000262/B9780323709392000262.xhtml) to review different forms of documentation, including electronic documentation. The client’s medication needs and your responsibilities are detailed in the care plan as well as on the client’s MAR, which also serves as a record for actions taken. The exact form of the MAR varies by setting and employer. It always contains at least the following:

1. • The client’s name
2. • The name, dose, and administration instructions for each medication
3. • A place to sign or initial after administering the medication

The MAR may also contain extra information, such as the client’s allergies, expected adverse effects, and special instructions ([FIGURE 45.4](#f0025)).

In facilities, a nurse is responsible for signing or initialling the MAR, and in some, the MAR for each client is printed out daily. It is the role of the nurse to provide health teaching to the client about each medication they are taking.

In community settings, the MAR is kept only when it is necessary to track specific medications and dosages. Clients who administer their own medications may not need the MAR, but when one is required, it is kept in the client’s home. If a nurse administers the medications, the nurse signs the MAR. If the client self-administers medications, the client signs the MAR as taught by the nurse. Observe as the client records the information on the MAR after the medication has been taken. Some clients may not be able to write because of physical disability or vision disorders, and you may then need to do the recording for these clients.

Follow your employer’s policies and procedures for recording. Ask your supervisor for help if needed.

## The 10 “Rights” of Medication Management

To help clients take medications accurately and safely, know and follow the 10 “rights” of medication management. Of course,
in addition to these rights, each client has the right to refuse any medication
. The 10 rights include the following:

* 1.
  The right medication.
   Be sure you are assisting the client to take the right medication. The name of the medication is printed on the prescription label on the medication container. Read the label carefully three times for each medication ([FIGURE 45.5](#f0030)). Make sure it is the same medication listed in the care plan and on the MAR by checking twice.
* 2.
  The right person.
   Make sure that you have identified the correct client by looking at the client’s full name (both first and last names). In some homes and facilities, two clients may have the same name. Make sure you are assisting the right client. Identify the client according to employer policy.
* 3.
  The right dose.
   The dose is listed on the prescription label, the care plan, and the MAR—for example, “Take one tablet daily” or “Apply ointment to left elbow twice a day.” The correct amount of medication must be taken. For example, if Mrs. Jong removes two tablets from the container, and the prescription label and her care plan say that she has to take only one tablet at a time, you must point that out to her. When the medication is in liquid form, the dose measurements may be in imperial (ounces), household (teaspoons or tablespoons), or metric (millilitres) units. Be sure the client does not measure in teaspoons when the prescription calls for millilitres. Make sure the client uses measuring spoons and measuring cups—not household spoons or cups—to measure doses.
* 4.
  The right route.
   The prescription label, care plan, and MAR list the route (see [TABLE 45.1](#t0010), on page 1062).
* 5.
  The right time.
   For medications to work properly, they must be taken at the correct time. Medications may have to be taken once a day or two, three, or four times a day, as stated on the prescription label. For example, Mrs. Jong’s prescription label states that the medication must be taken three times a day. The care plan and MAR state the exact times that the client needs to take the medication: 0600 hours, 1400 hours, and 2200 hours. Ensure that medications are given at the specified times. Taking medications at the wrong time of the day or taking them too close to or too far from meals and other medications can reduce their effectiveness and can even cause serious adverse effects. Assist the client to take all medications at the right time. If you go on an outing with the client, bring along the medications that must be taken during that time.
* 6.
  The right education.
   Some medications have to be taken when the stomach is empty—that is, 1 hour before or 2 hours after a meal. Other
  medications need to be taken at mealtimes to reduce stomach irritation and promote absorption. The prescription warning labels for these medications will say “Take on empty stomach” or “Take with food or milk.” Sometimes, clients must avoid certain foods or beverages when taking medications. Often, it is strongly recommended that alcohol and tobacco be avoided. Follow the warning labels on the prescription containers and the care plan. Some medications (such as cough medicines or sublingual tablets) must be taken after all other oral medications are taken. It is important to know the order for taking several medications at one time. If you are uncertain about the order that medications should be taken, you should clarify it with your supervisor. Clients may be confused about when to take their medications. For example, a client may say, “I can never remember. Do I take two tablets at 3 o’clock, or three tablets at 2 o’clock?” Tell your supervisor if there is evidence of confusion, and ask the nurse to reinforce any health teaching they may have given about taking medications properly.
* 7.
  The right day. Not all medications are taken daily; some are taken every second or third day. You need to check the MAR to ensure that the medication the client is taking is meant for that day.
* 8.
  The right reason.
   Medications should be taken for the right reason. A client saying, for example, “I took an extra pain pill to help me go to sleep” may indicate that the client is unaware of the risks associated with taking medication incorrectly. This situation should be reported to the supervisor.
* 9.
  The right expiry date.
   Just like foods, medications have expiry dates and should never be taken beyond their expiry date. In time, some medications either lose their potency (strength) or change chemically and become toxic for the client. If you have doubts about the expiry date, you should always check with your supervisor before assisting with that medication.
* 10.
  The right documentation. Any medication that is given must be documented properly, according to your agency policy. Medications are usually documented in the MAR. It is your responsibility to document any medication that you have assisted the client to take. If you have any questions about documenting, you need to ask your supervisor (who should be a nurse) to clarify it for you.

BOX 45.4 Canadian Labelling Requirements for Medications

In June 2015, plain-language labelling regulations took effect for prescription drugs. As of June 2017, the regulations also apply to nonprescription drugs.

The regulations state that the information required on the label must be (1) prominently displayed, (2) easy for the purchaser to understand, and (3) expressed in plain language. Additionally, the text and graphics on the label cannot impede the comprehensibility (clarity) of the information on the label.

The regulations are not retroactive, but existing products are expected to conform to the new regulations as packaging is updated.

Another measure included in the regulations for consumer safety is a brand name assessment, which aims to ensure that a new drug product would not be confused with another medication sold under a similar name.

## Reporting Medication Errors

If you believe that you have made a drug error (or you notice a drug error that might have been made
by someone else), you need to report it to your supervisor immediately! It is the supervisor’s responsibility to assess the situation and to determine the next course of action.
Deliberate failure to report a medication error is possible grounds for immediate dismissal (losing your job) or civil action (being charged with an offence in a court of law).
 You are required to report the error even if you think it might get you into trouble!
You can be in more trouble for failing to report the error than for making the error itself!

Errors are caused by problems in technique (such as dropping a pill down the sink by accident), by omission (such as forgetting to give a medication), by incorrectly documenting a medication, or by failing to follow one or more of the “rights.”
A drug error can cause severe harm to the client, so errors must always be reported!

Usually, an incident report must be filled out by you and your supervisor and a copy kept with the client’s chart. Incident report statistics can also identify if there is a problem that is something beyond your control (such as how the medication is labelled by the pharmacy), so it is important to include as many details as possible in the incident report. * 8 Check the label on the suppository for right medication, right person, right dose, right route, right time, right day, right reason, and the right expiry date. Compare the label with the MAR and the care plan.
* + This review reduces the risk for client injury.

* + The descending colon lies on the left side of the body (see [FIGURE 32.28](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml) on page 743), so in this position, gravity will allow the suppository’s medication to enter it from the anus and rectum.

* 12 Apply water-soluble lubricant to the suppository. Do not use petroleum jelly.
* + Petroleum jelly is not water-soluble.

* 15 Observe as the client inserts the suppository and wipes the anus with toilet tissue.
* 16 Have the client remove and discard the glove into the waste container.

* + Remaining in this position will allow the suppository to dissolve and the medication to be absorbed.

* + This should be performed only if it is within your scope of practice for your province or territory and you have been properly taught the correct procedure by a nurse.
  + Inserting any foreign object (such as an enema tip) into a client’s anus and rectum can cause injury to the client. Proper training in performing this procedure will reduce the risk for client injury.

* 20 Insert the tip into the client’s rectum, up to the end of the tip. (Remember to follow the manufacturer’s instructions.)
* 21 Gently squeeze the contents of the enema into the client. Ask the client to take slow, deep breaths during this part of the process.
* + Squeezing gently will decrease the risk for abdominal cramping.
* 22 Frequently check on the client’s level of comfort throughout the procedure. If the client develops cramping,
  stop immediately
  , and consult your supervisor.
* + Abdominal cramping may be a sign of rectal injury or another serious medical condition and should be investigated before proceeding.

* + This is to reduce the risk for pathogen spread.

* + The upright position ensures better bowel evacuation than the lying position.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Assisting with and administering medications are different functions. Assist means “to help”; administer means “to give.”

• A small mistake when assisting with or administering medications can cause serious harm to the client.

• Medication management involves helping clients self-administer medications. It is strictly a mechanical function. As a support worker, you do what clients would do themselves if they could to obtain the medication.

• Administering medications is usually done in facilities and for some home care clients, but it is beyond your scope of practice. It involves measuring medications or getting them into the person’s body.

• In some provinces and territories,
in some special circumstances
, your supervisor may authorize and train you to perform a controlled act (e.g., giving an insulin injection).
Never assume this responsibility, as it is beyond your usual scope of practice.

• Medications are chemical substances that cause a specific effect on the body. Medications are taken or prescribed to (1) treat an illness, (2) promote health, or (3) prevent illness.

• All health care workers involved with administering or assisting with medications should be aware of drugs’ effects and their classifications and the responsibilities associated with medications.

• Medication routes include (1) oral (solid, semi-solid, or liquids), (2) topical, (3) optic (eye), (4) otic (ear), (5) parenteral, (6) inhalation, and (7) suppository.

• The rights of clients related to medication administration by support workers include the right medication, right person, right dose, right route, right time, right day, right reason, right expiry date, and right documentation.

• It is important for support workers to know the correct procedure for medication management and if this is within their scope of practice.
