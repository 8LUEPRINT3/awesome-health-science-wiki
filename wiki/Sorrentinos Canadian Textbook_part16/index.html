    Chapter 46: Working in Acute Care

    Objectives

    After reading this chapter, the learner should be able to do the following:

    • Describe how to promote well-being during the admission, transfer, and discharge procedures.

    • Explain what to do before, during, and after a physical examination.

    • Identify the equipment used during a physical examination.

    • Describe how to prepare a client for an examination.

    • Describe the possible examination positions and how to drape the client for each of these positions.

    • Explain guidelines for assisting with a physical examination.

    • Describe the common fears and concerns of surgical clients.

    • Explain how clients are physically and psychologically prepared for surgery.

    • Describe how to prepare a room for the postoperative client.

    • List the postoperative signs and symptoms to report to the nurse.

    • Explain how circulation is stimulated after surgery.

    • Describe how to meet the client’s hygiene, nutrition, fluid, and elimination needs after surgery.

    • Perform the procedures described in this chapter.

    • Describe what to do in an emergency situation.

    More and more hospitals are now employing support workers to provide basic bedside care to clients and to assist the health care team. In many municipalities, support workers are now being hired to assist acute-care health care teams for hospitalized clients. This chapter focuses on supporting the client during acute-care transitions, assisting with physical examinations, supporting the client having surgery, and how to assist in an emergency situation. In some provinces and territories, hospitalized people are called “patients,” while in other provinces, they are referred to as “clients.” For consistency, we have used the term clients.

    The role that support workers play and their scope of practice when caring for clients in an acute-care setting (such as a hospital) differ somewhat from their roles and scope of practice in other settings (see Chapter 3). For example, there may be a need for more tasks to be delegated in an acute-care setting (BOX 46.1). Also, because acute-care clients (such as those who are undergoing investigative procedures or having surgery) can develop life-threatening health challenges quickly, it is important to observe for and immediately report changes in the client’s condition to the nursing staff. As always, it is also important to stay within your scope of practice and not attempt anything that you have not been delegated or taught to do.

    Supporting the Client During Transitions

    Clients often need to move from one environment to another—for example, into a facility (admission) or out of a facility (discharge) or from one room or unit in a facility to another (transfer).

    Transitions are difficult for most people. Do you remember how you felt the last time you moved? You might have felt sad if you had to leave friends and family behind, you might have felt strange and disoriented (out of place) in your new environment, or you might have experienced both feelings.

    BOX 46.1 Challenges of Working in an Acute-Care Setting

    Many support workers find the fast pace of an acute-care setting to be challenging and exciting, while others feel that they do not have enough time to get to know each patient. Following are some pros and cons of working in an acute-care setting, as reported by support workers:

    Pros

    1. • “Fast-paced and never boring”
    2. • “Never predictable, so you have to expect the unexpected”
    3. • “I like the 12-hour shifts on my unit”

    Cons

    1. • “I am constantly being asked to do things that are beyond my scope of practice; I guess doctors do not know what a support worker does.”
    2. • “I feel like I am a glorified porter. I never do any of the things I went to school for.”
    3. • “We get sent to units that are short-staffed all the time, so I never know who I am working with or the routine on the floor.”

    Clients should feel safe, comfortable, and relaxed in their environment, which may be any of the settings described in Chapter 3, including private homes, assisted-living dwellings, apartments, units in long-term care facilities, and hospital rooms. Consider the following examples:

    1. • Mr. Kremer, 88, moves from his home to a long-term care facility, but he feels like a stranger there. Each time you approach him, you introduce yourself to him, explain your role, and what you will be doing. You make his room feel more like home by helping arrange the possessions he has brought with him. You introduce him to other staff and clients. He begins to feel more secure and comfortable.
    2. • Jose Cruz, 17, is admitted to hospital following a car accident. He has some pain and is also anxious and fearful. A nurse gives him medication to control his pain. She explains what to expect during surgery and afterward. He feels better knowing what will happen next.

    For clients who are unwell, moving from one environment to another can be very difficult. Many clients feel anxious, unsettled, and alone. Moving can even cause or increase signs of confusion or disorientation in some clients, especially older persons. You can promote clients’ emotional well-being during transitions when you help them with the admission, transfer, and discharge processes.

    The Hospital Unit

    Hospital units have similarities to and differences from long-term care facilities. Each unit consists of client rooms and a nursing station (or central station). The nursing station is where change-of-shift reports may take place and where the unit receptionist sits to answer phone calls. In many hospitals, it is also where client records are stored and the medication room is located. Each hospital unit has many other rooms within it as well: a clean utility room, where clean supplies are stored; a soiled (or dirty) utility room, where garbage and contaminated objects are kept; and a pantry (or kitchenette), with a refrigerator for storing client snacks, a microwave oven to heat (or reheat) client food, and storage for disposable eating utensils.

    A hospital volunteer or staff member usually escorts newly admitted clients who are ambulatory to their assigned unit and notifies the nursing station of the new arrival. The nurse assigned to the client usually greets the client and begins the admission process. If the client is not in pain or distress, a support worker may be asked to greet the client and assist with the initial admission procedures. If you have been instructed to begin admission for a client, you need to introduce yourself by name and title to the client and family (FIGURE 46.1). Call the client by name in the proper manner and lead the way to the client’s room. Remember that the client and family may be feeling anxious, so be friendly and gentle, and do not rush them. See Providing Compassionate Care: Welcoming a Newly Admitted Client, on page 1091.

    In a hospital that is decentralized, each client room will contain the client’s records and any medications that were ordered. The medications are kept in locked cupboards on the wall that is shared with the hallway, and they have doors on either side to allow for access from inside the client’s room and from the hallway for restocking. They are restocked by the appropriate person on a daily basis. Some decentralized hospitals also have small desks and computers, and some may have medication carts in the halls outside clients’ rooms instead of medication cupboards if the rooms are too small to hold these items within them.

    Most hospital rooms have medical apparatuses (such as blood pressure equipment) mounted on the wall near each client’s bed for easy access when needed. Other equipment may also be present in clients’ rooms according to their needs. For example, an intravenous (IV) pole (IV standard) is used to hang an IV infusion bag or feeding bag. Some hospital beds have an IV pole stored in the bed frame, or the IV pole may be a separate piece of equipment that is brought to the bedside when needed. (See Chapter 18 for instructions on how to dress and undress a client with an IV.)

    Hospital rooms also have wall outlets for oxygen and suction (FIGURE 46.2) that are ready for use when required. Oxygen tanks, oxygen concentrators, and portable suction equipment, all of which are also common in long-term care and home care settings, may also be present. Because medical equipment is expensive and easily broken, only medically necessary equipment (except that mounted on the wall) is kept in the client’s room. Equipment that is not required by the clients in the room should be properly cleaned and returned to the storage unit.

    As stated earlier, there will also be a nursing station (sometimes called the central station), at which information about the client is kept. The central station may include the following:

    2. • A main desk and chairs for staff to review client records. Some physicians may dictate their surgical notes there.

    Admitting a Client to an Acute-Care Facility

    Admission is the official entry of a client into any health care facility where the client will be staying for any period of time, from overnight to indefinitely. The focuses of admission to any acute-care setting are diagnosis of the client’s symptoms, treatment of or surgery for the client’s illness, and improvement in the client’s quality of life by providing pain and symptom relief, as well as rehabilitation and therapy if required.

    Clients are admitted to hospital by a physician or a health care team, under the physician’s admission privileges (rights). In many instances, clients are admitted to a specific hospital simply because it is the hospital where the client’s doctor or team of doctors have admission privileges. In most hospitals, clients are usually assigned to an available room on a unit according to the client’s main diagnosis. For example, clients who require heart monitoring will be admitted to a unit in which heart monitoring and treatment are the main focus.

    The admission process can cause uncertainty, anxiety, and fear. Clients often worry about medical tests, treatment, and surgery. They may fear pain and serious health conditions. Similarly, clients who face an uncertain length of time in the hospital often feel sad about leaving home. The facility is strange and unfamiliar—they do not know what to expect and where to go for things or who to approach if they have questions. They worry about getting meals, finding the bathroom, and getting help.

    Except in emergencies, the hospital admission process starts in the admissions office. A member of the admitting staff gives the new client an ID number and a bracelet. The client signs a consent-for-treatment form, and the nursing unit is notified that a client is being admitted. As a support worker, you may be asked to prepare the room for the client. A porter, hospital volunteer, support worker, or an admissions office staff member brings the client to the nursing unit, sometimes by wheelchair or stretcher.

    Admission procedures include the following:

    1. • Weighing and measuring the client (see Chapter 24)
    2. • Orienting the client to the room, nursing unit, and hospital
    3. • Obtaining a urine specimen if ordered (see Chapter 29)

    Transfers

    A transfer occurs when a client is moved from one room or nursing unit to another or even to another facility. Transfers usually occur when a client’s condition changes or when a room change is requested because roommates do not get along. Some clients are transferred for rehabilitation. A client may or may not welcome a transfer. The client’s physician, nurse, or social worker usually explains the reason for the transfer.

    You may assist with the transfer or carry out the entire procedure. Clients are usually transported by wheelchair or stretcher or on their beds. Be sensitive and compassionate as you carry out the transfer procedure, as clients need support and reassurance. The client does not know the staff on the new unit, so introduce the client to the staff and to the new roommate if there is one. Wish the client well as you leave.

    Discharges

    Discharge is the official departure of a client from a hospital or other health care facility. Discharged clients may have recovered enough to go home, and may need home care. Others are discharged to another hospital, to a long-term care facility, or to a hospice. The physician, nurse, dietitian, social worker, and other health care team members plan the client’s discharge. If the client is going home, they teach the client and family about diet, exercise, medications, treatments, and dressing changes. The case manager arranges for home care, rehabilitation, special therapy, and equipment, if required.

    As a support worker, you may help clients pack their belongings and get ready to leave and then transport them out of the facility. In a hospital, the physician must write a discharge order before a client is allowed to leave. In a long-term care facility, permission must be obtained from the nurse. In some facilities, the chart contains instructions as to when the client may leave and with whom.

    Always use good communication skills when assisting with a client’s discharge. Wish the client and family well as they leave the facility.

    Sometimes, a client may want to leave without permission. If clients tell you that they want or intend to leave, notify the nurse immediately.

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Welcoming a Newly Admitted Client

    1. • Dignity. Treat the new client and their belongings with respect. Introduce yourself by name and title to the client and family, and ask the client what name the staff should use. Do not rush into admission procedures. Treat the client and family as if they are guests in your home. Listen to their concerns or questions, and do not overload them with information. Explain details about routines and other matters only if the new client seems eager to hear them. Reassure clients that they do not need to remember everything. Remind them that they can ask questions and talk to the staff at any time.
    2. • Independence. Show clients to their assigned rooms, and show them where to store their clothing and where the bathroom is. Explain how to use the various equipment in the room. Make sure clients can reach the phone, TV remote, and light controls.
    3. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. Client care should be individualized to include dietary restrictions and medication allergies.
    4. • Preferences. Explain when visiting hours are and how to fill out the client’s menu. Take note and promptly report if the client has food allergies or menu preferences because of religious or lifestyle reasons. Some clients request to have a family member present when the doctor visits, especially if the client has cognitive, language, or communication challenges. Such issues should also be reported to the nurse assigned to the client.

In some provinces and territories, support workers in a facility may be asked to assist the physician or the nurse with a physical exam, and support workers in the community may assist physicians or nurses with some aspects of a physical exam.

    The Support Worker’s Responsibilities Regarding Physical Examinations

    As a support worker, your responsibilities depend on your employer’s policies and procedures, your scope of practice in your province or territory, the examiner’s preferences, the setting you are working in, and the type of examination. You may do some or all of the following:

    1. • Collect linens for draping the client for the procedure
    2. • Collect examination equipment
    3. • Prepare the room for the examination
    4. • Transport the client to and from the exam room if your facility has an examination room
    5. • Assist with lighting
    6. • Measure vital signs, height, and weight
    7. • Position and drape the client
    8. • Hand equipment and instruments to the examiner
    9. • Stay with the client before or during the exam to provide emotional support and to prevent falls
    10. • Label specimen containers
    11. • Dispose of soiled linen and discard used supplies according to the facility policy
    12. • Clean equipment

    Equipment

    Instruments needed for a physical examination may include, but are not limited to, the following (FIGURE 46.5):

    1. • Ophthalmoscope—a lighted instrument used to examine the internal structures of the eye.
    2. • Otoscope—a lighted instrument used to examine the external ear and the eardrum (tympanic membrane). Some scopes are combination ophthalmoscopes and otoscopes, with parts for examining both eyes and ears.
    3. • Percussion hammer—used to tap body parts to test reflexes. It is also called a reflex hammer.
    4. • Vaginal speculum—used to open the vagina so it and the cervix can be examined.

    Many facilities have examination trays in the supply department. If one is not available, collect the items listed in your facility’s or agency’s procedure manual or requested by the examiner , and arrange them on a tray or table.

    Preparing the Client for a Physical Exam

    Many clients worry about having a physical examination because of possible findings or because of confusion or fear about what the examiner will do. Feeling discomfort and embarrassment, fearing exposure, and not knowing what the procedure involves all cause anxiety. As a support worker, you need to be sensitive to the client’s feelings and concerns and prepare the client physically and psychologically for the examination.

    The client has the right to know who will do the exam, the reason for the exam, when it will be done, and what the procedure involves. This information is given to the client by the physician or nurse. If a client asks you questions about an examination, reply that your supervisor will provide the necessary answers.

    The exam is done only if the client gives consent. The client may want a different examiner or may want a family member to be present at the exam. Some clients want to have family members with them when exam results are explained to them. In the event that a client is not able to make an informed decision about an examination, the examiner must obtain permission from the appointed decision maker.

    The client’s right to privacy must be protected. In a facility, the examining area is screened and the room door closed. Depending on your employer’s policy, the client may have to remove all clothes for a complete examination and be asked to wear a hospital gown, which can reduce the feeling of nakedness and the fear of exposure. The client is also covered with a paper drape, bath blanket, or sheet. Explain to the client that only the body part being examined is exposed and that only minimum exposure will occur (see the Focus on Children: Promoting Privacy and Comfort During a Physical Exam box). In a community setting, as well, you should cover the client with a sheet to ensure that only the areas being examined are exposed.

    Focus on Children

    Promoting Privacy and Comfort During a Physical Exam

    The examination of an infant or a child is similar to an adult examination. However, a parent or other adult caregiver is required to be present. The parent or caregiver may need to hold the infant or child still for parts of the procedure if the child is uncooperative. Some parents or caregivers may be frightened by the child’s reaction or by the examination itself. The child or infant may also have separation anxiety or fear of physical harm during the examination. Your calm, comforting attitude will help not only the child but also the parent or caregiver, who may be feeling anxious, too.

    Most of the equipment is the same as for the adult examination; however, with children, toys are sometimes used to assess development, and vaginal speculums are never used. In addition, toddlers, preschool children, and school-aged children keep their underpants on for an examination. The underpants may be lowered or removed, as necessary, during the procedure.

    Clients are asked to void before the examination, as an empty bladder allows the examiner to feel the abdominal organs easily. A full bladder can change the normal position and shape of organs and can also cause discomfort, especially when the examiner is palpating (feeling) the abdominal organs. If a urine specimen is needed, ask the client to collect it when voiding before the exam. Explain how to collect the specimen (see Chapter 29), and when the client hands it to you, label the container.

    Maintaining the client’s warmth during the examination is important, so ensure that the client—especially one with an illness, an older client, or a child—is protected from getting chilled. Keep an extra blanket at hand, and take measures to prevent drafts.

    The examiner may want height, weight, and vital signs measured, so obtain these before the examination starts and record the information on the examination form. The client is then positioned and draped for the examination. Take care to protect the client from falls and injury. Do not leave the client unattended.

    Positioning and Draping the Client

    For some examinations, special positions may be needed, and some positions may be uncomfortable and embarrassing for the client. The examiner will tell you how to position the client. Before helping the client assume and maintain the position, explain the following:

    1. • Why the position is needed
    2. • How to assume the position
    3. • How the body is draped for warmth and privacy
    4. • How long the client can expect to stay in the position

    The supine position (dorsal recumbent position or horizontal position) is used to examine the abdomen, anterior chest, and breasts. The client lies flat on the back, keeping the legs together. If the perineal area is to be examined, the knees are flexed and the hips externally rotated (FIGURE 46.6, A). The drape is extended over the client’s body from the shoulders to the feet.

    The lithotomy position (see FIGURE 46.6, B) is used to examine the vagina. The client lies on her back, and her hips are brought to the edge of the examination table. The knees are flexed, the hips externally rotated, and the feet supported in stirrups. The client is draped as for perineal care (see FIGURE 17.26, on page 330). Some facilities provide socks to cover the client’s feet and calves. If a client finds it difficult to assume this position, the examiner will tell you how to position the client.

    The knee–chest position (see FIGURE 46.6, C) is used to examine the rectum and sometimes the vagina. The client kneels and rests their chest on their knees, with their head turned to one side. The arms are kept above the head or flexed at the elbows. The back is kept straight, and the body is flexed about 90 degrees at the hips. The client wears a gown and sometimes socks, and the drape is applied in a diamond shape to cover the back, buttocks, and thighs. This position is rarely used for older persons, who usually assume the side-lying position for rectal exams.

    FIGURE 46.6 Positioning and draping for the physical examination. A, Supine position. B, Lithotomy position. C, Knee–chest position. D, Sims’ position.

    Sims’ position (see FIGURE 46.6, D) is sometimes used to examine the rectum or the vagina. The client lies on their left side. The right leg is sharply flexed so that it is not on the left leg. The left arm is positioned along the client’s back. The drape is applied over the client in a diamond shape. The corner near the examiner is folded back to expose the rectum or the vagina.

    Preparing the Client for an Examination

    Advocate on behalf of the client’s quality of life by promoting:

    Dignity • Independence • Individualized Care • Preferences • Privacy • Safety (see BOX 1.4 , on page 17)

    Procedure

    • 1 Identify the client, according to employer policy.
      • This eliminates the possibility of mistaking one client for another.
    • 2 Explain the procedure to the client.
    • 3 Perform hand hygiene.
    • 4 Collect instruments and supplies, as listed in your facility’s procedure manual or as requested by the examiner.
      • The type of examination and the condition of your client will determine the instruments and supplies needed for the examination.
    • 5 Provide for privacy. In a facility, there will be curtains to provide privacy. In a community setting, you need to close the door to your client’s room for privacy.
    • 6 Ask the client to void. Offer the bedpan, commode, or urinal if necessary. Provide for privacy.
      • A full bladder can interfere with palpation of the abdomen and be very uncomfortable for the client.

    Depending on your province or territory and your facility’s policies, you may be asked to prepare, position, and drape the client and also to assist the physician or the nurse during the exam. As a support worker, you will provide valuable help to the examiner and provide comfort care to your client. When assisting with an examination, follow the guidelines in BOX 46.2 (also see the Focus on Children: Providing Privacy and Comfort During a Physical Exam box).

The client’s rights should always be respected.

    Supporting the Client who is Having Surgery

    In hospitals, as a support worker, you will come in contact with clients before and after surgery. In long-term care facilities, you may care for many clients who are recovering from surgery. In the community, you may provide postoperative care to clients in their homes. Surgery is performed on clients for many reasons, including removal of a diseased organ or body part, removal of a tumour, or repair of injured tissue. Surgery may also be done to diagnose a disease, improve appearance, or relieve symptoms. A specially trained physician, called a surgeon, performs the surgery. The client is prepared by nurses and physicians for what happens before, during, and after surgery. Clients need both physical and psychological preparation before surgery.

    A large number of surgeries—for example, repair of a hip fracture—require hospital stays; the client is admitted to the hospital before the surgery and stays for a few or several days after the surgery. However, outpatient surgery (also called ambulatory surgery, day surgery, or same-day surgery), which does not require an overnight hospital stay, is also done frequently. Clients undergoing these procedures are in the hospital for less than 24 hours.

    Many outpatient surgeries are done in clinics or surgical centres that are part of hospitals or physicians’ offices. For example, cataract surgery would be an outpatient procedure. As a support worker, you may be involved in caring for a client after the client returns home from surgery. Your employer will give you instructions regarding the care you need to provide to your client.

    Some outpatient procedures will require home preparation. For example, to prepare for a colonoscopy, the client must take medication, as prescribed by the doctor, the day before the procedure to clear the colon of all fecal matter.

    Surgeries may be elective, urgent, or emergency:

    1. • Elective surgery—surgery that is scheduled but nonurgent (e.g., hip replacement surgery). Delaying the surgery does not result in permanent damage, disability, or death. The surgery may be scheduled 1 day before surgery to months in advance. Often, clients are placed on waiting lists for elective surgery.
    2. • Urgent surgery—surgery that must be done soon to prevent further damage, disability, or disease (e.g., cancer surgery and coronary artery surgery).
    3. • Emergency surgery—surgery that must be done immediately to save a client’s life or prevent disability. Clients who have been in accidents often require emergency surgery.

    The reasons a client requires surgery will vary and may influence the client’s mood prior to surgery. For example, if a client is facing diagnostic surgery to see if he has cancer, he may be tense, agitated, or even irritable. It is important to be empathetic and nonjudgemental of the client’s actions at all times.

    Purposes of surgical procedures include the following:

    1. • Diagnostic —investigative surgery to determine the cause of a client’s symptoms or the extent to which an illness has affected the body. For example, a client who has been unable to conceive a child may require diagnostic surgery to explore why.
    2. • Reconstructive —surgery performed to restore the function or appearance of a body part or organ. For example, a client who has lost several fingers in a farming accident may require skin grafts and digit replacement to regain partial (or full) use of his hand.
    3. • Palliative —surgery performed to enhance the quality of the dying client’s life, not to cure the client. For example, a client with a large esophageal tumour may need to have the tumour removed or reduced to help her be able to eat.
    4. • Curative —surgery aimed at repairing (or removing) an injured or diseased body part in order to cure the disease. A client who has sustained abdominal injuries in a car accident would undergo surgery to repair any damaged areas.

    Psychological Care

    Imagine you were in an accident, and when you woke up hours later, you were told that your right leg had been surgically amputated. How would you feel? If you were told you needed surgery the very next day, would you have fear of pain or death? Would you worry about who would care for your children and your home and who would earn money for your family while you were in the hospital?

    Psychological preparation before surgery is very important. Illness or injury causes many fears and concerns (BOX 46.3), and if surgery is needed, these concerns can increase. For example, clients may be afraid of the pain that they may experience after surgery. The entire health care team must communicate warmth, sensitivity, and caring to the client. Clients may express their fears to you, and as a support worker, you must respect these fears and concerns and the client’s need to talk about them.

    Feelings are influenced by past experiences. Some clients may have had surgery before, and some may not have had that experience at all. Family and friends may share experiences of their own surgeries with the client, which also can affect the client. Clients may have heard tragic stories related to surgeries—surgery on the wrong client, surgery on the wrong body part, instruments left in the body, death during surgery, and so on. Before surgery, some clients may not talk about their fears and concerns but just cry or be quiet and withdrawn; some may constantly talk about other things; some may pace restlessly; and some may act overly cheerful.

    Client Information

    The physician explains to the client and family the need for surgery and the surgical procedure, risks, and possible complications. Options other than surgery as well as the risks of not having surgery are also explained. The physician or nurse gives information about when the surgery is scheduled, who will perform it, and how long it will take. The physician also answers all the questions and addresses any doubts the client and family may have about the surgery and what to expect and gives instructions about pre- and postoperative care. Consent for surgery must be given by the client or a substitute decision maker. In most provinces and territories, support workers cannot witness consent for surgery, as doing so is not within their scope of practice. If your client and the family request more information about the surgery, refer them to your supervisor.

    Often, the health care team knows the results of the surgery before the client does. The physician informs the client and the family about the outcome of the surgery, deciding what and when to tell them. Clients and families are usually anxious to know the results and may ask you, nurses, and other health care workers for information. They may ask, for example, if reports are back from the laboratory or what the reports say. It is not your responsibility to reveal any information about the surgery or test results. If the client asks you for information, explain that you cannot give it and that their physician will provide that information. Ensure that you report to your supervisor your client’s concerns and questions so that the supervisor can answer the questions or direct the client to the doctor, who can address these issues. Your supervisor will tell you what the client and the family were told and when it was told. This information is confidential, so do not repeat it to anyone.

    The Support Worker’s Role

    As a support worker, you can assist in the psychological care of the surgical client, whether the client is an inpatient or is in for day surgery. Do the following if you are involved in preoperative and postoperative care:

    1. • Listen to the client when the client voices fears or concerns about surgery.
    2. • Refer any questions about the surgery or its results to the nurse.
    3. • Explain to the client the procedures that you will perform and the reasons for them.
    4. • Communicate effectively. Use verbal and nonverbal communication (see Chapter 9).
    5. • Report to your supervisor basic assessment findings that you have observed in the client, such as verbal and nonverbal signs of pain, fear, or anxiety.
    6. • Report to your supervisor a client’s request to see a spiritual advisor.

    The Preoperative Period

    The preoperative (before surgery) period may be many days or just a few minutes (BOX 46.4). If time permits, the client is prepared psychologically and physically for the effects of anaesthesia and surgery (see the Focus on Children: Preparing for Surgery box). Good preoperative preparation prevents complications after surgery.

    Different kinds of medication are prescribed for preoperative preparation, and your supervisor will tell you the procedure to follow when assisting a client with these medications. Some medications may cause diarrhea and, therefore, dehydration in the client. You need to ensure that the client drinks lots of fluids. Instructions from the doctor will include what types of fluids are allowed. The client will need to stay close to a bathroom and may experience some cramping. The area around the anus can become quite sore with frequent defecation. You can assist the client with the use of adult unscented wet wipes, a water spray, or a barrier cream.

    Focus on Children

    Preparing for Surgery

    The child who will be operated on, as well as the parents or caregivers, is psychologically prepared before the surgery if it is a scheduled surgery. Often, play is used to help the child understand what will happen. For example, dolls are used to show the anatomical site of surgery. The child and parents or caregivers are introduced to the members of the health care team who will be caring for the child. A tour of the operating room and the recovery room also is common.

    Special Tests

    Before surgery, the physician orders tests to evaluate the client’s circulatory, respiratory, and urinary systems. These tests include a chest X-ray, a complete blood count (CBC), and urinalysis. An electrocardiogram (ECG) is used to detect any cardiac (heart) conditions. In preparation for any needed transfusions to correct anticipated blood loss, the patient’s blood is tested to determine blood type (type and cross-match). Other tests are also done, depending on the client’s condition and the requirements of the surgery. The client is prepared for the tests, as needed, and results are on the client’s chart by the time of surgery.

    Nutrition and Fluids

    The client is usually allowed to have a light meal the day before the surgery and then is NPO for 6 to 8 hours before the surgery. These measures reduce the risk of vomiting and aspiration during general anaesthesia and after surgery. An NPO sign is placed in the client’s room. Some clients may be required to take medications on the morning of surgery—for example, insulin or blood pressure medication. It is important that any such medication need is a doctor’s order and that it is clearly written. Remember to remove the water pitcher and drinking glasses from the room when a patient is ordered NPO. If the time of surgery is delayed or cancelled, your client will need nutrition during the waiting period. Check with your supervisor about the type of diet and fluids allowed.

    Elimination

    Abdominal surgeries usually require a preoperative enema. Cleansing enemas are common before intestinal surgeries and are ordered to clear the colon of feces so that feces do not spill into the abdominal cavity when the intestine is opened.

    Enemas are also given when straining or when a bowel movement could cause postoperative issues such as pain, severe bleeding (hemorrhage), or stress on the operated area. The physician specifies what enema to give and when, and usually a nurse administers the enema.

    Some surgeries will necessitate urinary catheterization. For pelvic and abdominal surgeries, the bladder must be empty, as a full bladder is easily injured during surgery. Catheters also allow accurate urinary output measurements during and after surgery.

    Personal Care

    Personal care before surgery usually involves the following:

    1. • Giving a complete bed bath, shower, or tub bath. A special soap or cleanser may be ordered. A bath and a shampoo help reduce the number of microbes on the body at the time of surgery.
    2. • Removing makeup and nail polish. The skin, lips, and nail beds need to be observed for colour and circulation during and after surgery.
    3. • Caring for hair. All hairpins, clips, combs, and similar items as well as wigs and hairpieces are removed. Some hospitals have all clients wear surgical caps to keep hair out of the face and the operative area.
    4. • Giving oral hygiene to promote comfort. Being NPO causes thirst and a dry mouth. Ensure that the client does not swallow any water during oral hygiene (see the Focus on Children: Preoperative Oral Hygiene box).
    5. • Removing dentures. Before preoperative medications are given, dentures are removed, cleaned, and kept in a denture cup in a safe place. Some clients do not like being seen without their dentures, in which case allow them to wear their dentures as long as possible. This measure promotes the client’s sense of dignity and self-esteem.
    6. • Removing all jewellery. If body-piercing jewellery cannot be removed, it should be taped down.

    Focus on Children

    Preoperative Oral Hygiene

    In a child, a loose tooth can fall out during anaesthesia, and the child can aspirate the tooth. Therefore, during preoperative care, check for loose teeth when giving oral hygiene, and report any that you observe to the nurse. The nurse will note your observations on the preoperative checklist and inform the operating room (OR) staff.

    Valuables

    Valuables such as dentures, eyeglasses, contact lenses, hearing aids, and jewellery are removed from clients for safekeeping. Artificial eyes and limbs also are removed, as these items are easily lost or broken during surgery. A note is made on the client’s chart about the valuables removed and where they are kept. The client may not want to remove a wedding band or religious medal. In this case, the item is secured in place with gauze or tape, according to hospital policy.

    Skin Preparation

    The skin and hair shafts contain microbes that could enter the body through the surgical incision, and a serious infection could result. The skin cannot be sterilized, but skin prep can help reduce the number of microbes.

    Hospital policy and the surgeon’s preferences determine the area to be prepared for a specific surgery and the type of preparation to be done.

    The Preoperative Checklist

    A preoperative checklist (FIGURE 46.8) is placed at the top of the client’s chart. The nurse makes sure that all items on the checklist have been completed because only when the list has been completed is the client considered to be ready for surgery. The nurse may ask you to do some things on the list, and when you complete each task, promptly report it to the nurse along with any observations. Except for positioning of bed rails, the entire checklist should be completed before preoperative medications are given.

    Preoperative Medication

    About 45 minutes to 1 hour before surgery, the preoperative medications are given by a physician or a nurse. One medication helps the patient relax and feel drowsy, and another dries up respiratory secretions to prevent aspiration. Drowsiness, light-headedness, thirst, and dry mouth are normal and expected reactions (see the Focus on Children: Preoperative Medications box).

    After the medications are given, safety measures are taken to prevent falls. Bed rails are raised, and the patient is not allowed out of bed from this point forward, so the client must void before the medications are given. If the client needs to void after the medications are given, a bedpan or urinal is used.

    The bed is kept in the lowest position or raised to the highest position, according to hospital policy. Move furniture out of the way to make room for the stretcher. Also, clear the overbed table and the bedside stand to prevent damage to equipment and valuables. Raise the bed to the highest position for transferring the client from the bed to the stretcher.

    Preoperative Medications

    Anaesthesia

    Immediately prior to most surgical procedures, the client will be given anaesthesia medication intended to result in a loss of feeling or sensation. Anaesthetics are given by specially trained physicians called anaesthetists. There are three types of anaesthesia:

      1. General anaesthesia—unconsciousness and the loss of feeling or sensation, produced by a medication that is given intravenously or that is inhaled (i.e., a gas).
      1. Regional anaesthesia—loss of sensation or feeling in a large area of the body, produced by the injection of a medication. The client does not lose consciousness.
      1. Local anaesthesia—loss of sensation in a small area, produced by a medication that is injected at the specific site.

    The Postoperative Period

    During the postoperative (after surgery) period, the client is taken to the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU) or, as it is called in some regions, the recovery room. Some clients may suffer from nausea and vomiting. Vital signs are taken and observations are made frequently. The client leaves the recovery room when the following states are reached:

    1. • Vital signs are stable.
    2. • The respiratory function is good.

If unsure of what to observe for, you should ask the nurse what to look for, how often to check on the client, and what you need to report. You must always be alert for the signs and symptoms listed in  BOX 46.5, and report them to the nurse immediately.

    Positioning

    Proper positioning after surgery promotes comfort and prevents complications. The type of surgery determines positioning and, possibly, position restrictions. The client is usually positioned for easy and comfortable breathing and to prevent stress on the incision. When the client is in the supine position, the head of the bed is usually raised slightly, and the client’s head may be turned to the side, which will help prevent aspiration if vomiting occurs.

    Repositioning every 1 to 2 hours helps prevent respiratory and circulatory complications. Turning may be painful for the client, so provide support, and turn the client with smooth, gentle motions. Pillows and other positioning devices are often used (see Chapters 14 and 15).

    The nurse will tell you when to reposition the client and the positions allowed. Usually, you will assist the nurse, but sometimes, you will turn and reposition the client yourself once the client’s condition is stable and the client is in a community setting or at home (see the Focus on Older Persons: Postoperative Positioning box on page 1105).

    Coughing and Deep Breathing

    Respiratory complications in the postsurgical period must be prevented. There are two major complications: (1) pneumonia, an inflammation and infection in the lung; and (2) atelectasis, the collapse of a portion of the lung. Coughing and deep-breathing exercises and incentive spirometry help prevent these complications (see Chapter 39) and are done every 1 to 2 hours when the client is awake. For comfort, the client should be instructed to splint the incision (hold their hands tightly over the incision) if possible (see the Focus on Older Persons: Respiratory Complications After Surgery box on page 1105).

    Stimulating Circulation

    After surgery, circulation must be stimulated, especially for blood flow in the legs. If blood flow is sluggish, a blood clot (thrombus) can form in the deep leg veins (FIGURE 46.9, A).     Early Ambulation

    Early ambulation prevents postoperative circulatory complications such as thrombus formation, pneumonia, atelectasis, constipation, and urinary tract infections. The client is usually encouraged to ambulate even on the day of surgery. The client first sits up with their legs over the edge of the bed (see Helping the Client to Sit on the Side of the Bed (Dangle the Legs) in Chapter 14). If blood pressure and pulse are stable, the client is assisted out of bed. Usually the client does not walk very far, just a few feet in the room. Walking distance is increased as the client gains strength.

    The nurse will tell you when to help the client ambulate. Usually, you will assist the nurse the first time the client ambulates.

    Wound Healing

    The surgical incision needs protection, so measures are taken to promote healing and prevent infection. Sterile dressing changes are done by a physician or a nurse. Some hospitals allow support workers to perform simple dressing care if the client is stable (see Chapter 22 for a discussion on wound healing).

    Nutrition and Fluids

    The client will have an IV infusion upon return from the OR. Continuation of the IV therapy depends on the type of surgery and the client’s condition—for example, whether nausea and vomiting have resulted from the general anaesthesia. The client’s diet progresses from NPO to clear liquids to full liquids to a light diet to a regular diet that is ordered by the physician. Frequent oral hygiene is important when the patient is NPO.

    Some clients will have nasogastric (NG) tubes (see Chapter 28) after surgery. The NG tube is attached to suction equipment to keep the stomach empty. The patient remains NPO and receives IV therapy. It is important to remember to perform oral care on clients who have an NG tube or have been NPO.

    Elimination

    Anaesthesia, the surgery, and being NPO can all affect normal fecal and urinary elimination, and medications for pain relief can cause constipation. You will be asked to take measures to promote elimination, as directed by the nurse (see Chapters 29 and 30).

    Fluid intake and output (I&O) are measured during the postoperative period. It is important that the client urinates within 8 hours after surgery. Report the time and amount of the client’s first voiding. If the client does not void within 8 hours, a catheterization is usually ordered. Some clients have catheters inserted routinely after surgery—for example, after bladder surgery (see Chapter 29 for care of the client with a catheter).

    Adequate fluid intake and return to a regular diet are needed for normal bowel movement. Suppositories or enemas may be ordered if the client is constipated, and rectal tubes may be ordered for relief of flatulence (see Chapter 30).

    Comfort and Rest

    Pain is common after surgery. The degree of pain depends on the extent of surgery, the size and location of the incision site, and the presence of drainage tubes, casts, or other devices. In addition, as mentioned above, because the client was placed in one position during surgery, this often results in severe muscle strains and discomfort, usually in the chest, back, and arms, postoperatively. The physician orders medications for pain relief, and the nurse will tell you how to promote comfort and rest (see Chapter 23 for guidelines) listed as part of the client’s care plan.

    Some surgeries (such as scopes) involve inserting gas to inflate the dermis (skin) to allow the surgeon to have better visualization of the desired site. This gas is harmless to the body, but it can cause discomfort that resembles severe muscle cramping. Many clients also experience a sore throat from being intubated (having a tube inserted into the throat for introduction of gas anaesthesia and for assistance with breathing).

    Remember that people experience pain differently, and many factors, such as anxiety or fear, may enhance the pain the client is experiencing. Pain can also be a sign of an impending complication that is developing , so it is important to always be empathetic to the client and to report any complaints of pain to the nurse.

    Personal Hygiene

    Personal hygiene is important for the client’s physical and mental well-being. Wound drainage and skin prep solutions can irritate the skin and cause discomfort, and being NPO can cause dry mouth and breath odours. Moist, clammy skin from blood pressure changes or elevated body temperatures also cause discomfort. Frequent oral hygiene, hair care, and a complete bed bath the day after the surgery help refresh and renew the client’s physical and psychological well-being. The gown should be changed whenever it becomes wet or soiled (see Chapter 17).

    Think About Safety

    Safety Precautions for Clients Who Are Having Surgery

    1. • If your client is having a day surgery (e.g., colonoscopy), medications may be prescribed to be taken before the procedure, and these drugs can cause diarrhea. You must ensure that your client has access to the bathroom and that you can provide fluid replacement as prescribed by the physician.
    2. • If you are employed in an acute-care facility and your client is having surgery, ensure that the side bed rails are raised once any preoperative medications have been administered so that your client does not roll out of bed. Your supervisor will tell you when you can lower the bed rails after the operation.
    3. • Your supervisor will give you instructions regarding when your client will be NPO before surgery and after surgery. Follow the instructions, as giving a client something to drink before surgery could result in vomiting or aspiration. The physician will order fluid and food intake for your client after surgery.
    4. • Depending on your agency’s policy, you may be delegated by a nurse or physiotherapist to provide deep-breathing, coughing, and leg exercises.
    5. • Ensure that you report your client’s I&O to your supervisor, as required.
    6. • If your client has received pain medications, you must be aware of the possibility of falls, so ensure that you are with your client at any time when they are out of bed.

    How to Assist in an Emergency Situation

    As stated at the beginning of this chapter, because acute-care clients (such as those who are undergoing investigative procedures or having surgery) can develop life-threatening health challenges very quickly, it is important to observe for and immediately report changes in the client’s condition to the nursing staff. It is also important to stay within your scope of practice and not attempt to do anything that you have not been delegated or taught to do.

    Life-Threatening Conditions

    Many life-threatening conditions have no cure, and some injuries are so serious that the body cannot function. In these cases, recovery is not expected, and the health condition or injury inevitably ends in death.

    Health care providers cannot predict the exact time of death. A client may have days, months, weeks, or years to live. Some clients who are expected to live for a short time live for many years, whereas others who are expected to live longer die much sooner than predicted. Some clients die sooner than expected because they lose the will to live. No one can predict the length of time a client may have, as each client is unique.

    The time spent knowing that death is approaching is often difficult for the client as well as for the client’s family, friends, and loved ones. The approach of death may be when people who are dying resolve existing interpersonal issues, put financial affairs in order, and decide on the extent of medical care they would like as they near their life’s end. The loved ones of the dying client must begin to realize that they will not always have this person in their lives, and they may start to grieve the loss of this partner, friend, or family member.

    Attitudes Toward Death and Dying

    Personal beliefs, culture, religion, age, and experience influence attitudes toward death. Many people fear death; others believe they will regain health; and some accept and even look forward to death. A person’s attitude toward death often changes with age and experience.

    Until the beginning of the twentieth century, most Canadians died in their own homes, and families cared for loved ones who were dying. Death was considered a natural part of life. Today, however, most Canadians die in facilities. Many people have never seen a dying person or a dead body, so death can be a frightening experience for them.

    Personal Beliefs, Culture, and Religion

    Practices and attitudes about death and dying differ among individuals, cultures, and religions (see the Respecting Diversity: Death Rituals box on page 1124). Some people believe that there is no suffering and hardship after death, that they will be reunited with loved ones in the afterlife, and that sins and misdeeds will be punished then, too. Other people believe there is no afterlife. Still others believe in rebirth. Many clients experience a strengthening of their religious beliefs when they face death, and such faith often provides comfort to the dying person and the family. Alternatively, clients may instead experience a loss or diminishment of religious faith.

    A client’s personal, cultural, and religious beliefs also influence choices about end-of-life care and treatment of the body after death.

    How Age and Experience Affect Responses to Death and Dying

    People’s attitudes and responses to dying are highly individual. Age and stage of life are some of the major influences on people’s feelings and reactions in regard to death.

    Infants and toddlers have no concept of death. Between the ages of 3 and 5 years, children are curious and have some ideas about death because they know when family members or pets die, and they notice dead birds and insects. However, they may think that death is only a temporary state. Not answering their questions about death accurately and properly can cause fear and confusion. For example, children who are told “He is sleeping” may be afraid to go to sleep. Children between ages 5 and 7 know that death is final, though they may not think it will happen to them.

    Adults often have more fears about death than children do. Adults may fear pain and suffering, dying alone, separation from loved ones, and loss of dignity. They may worry about the care and support of those left behind. Some people have regrets about missed opportunities, failed relationships, or unfulfilled hopes and dreams. People may feel that their lives did not have any meaning or, alternatively, that their lives did make a difference.

    Older people often have fewer fears about death, as they expect to die, and many have experienced the deaths of friends and loved ones. Some older persons welcome death as freedom from pain, suffering, and disability. As a support worker, you will have clients who may want to discuss their wishes about what should happen in the event of their death. Report their wishes to your supervisor, who will forward them to the family. Like younger adults, older persons often fear dying alone.

    Although age and stage of life influence a person’s response to dying, you cannot predict what their response will be.

    Caring for a Client Who is Dying

    The health care team helps meet the dying client’s emotional, social, spiritual, intellectual, and physical needs, and every effort is made to promote physical and emotional comfort, whether the client is at home, in a hospice setting, or in a facility of any kind. The client’s right to die in peace and with dignity is always kept in the forefront of these efforts (see BOX 47.1 and the Providing Compassionate Care: The Dying Client box on page 1114).

    Emotional, Social, Intellectual, and Spiritual Needs

    Clients who are dying need accurate information to make informed decisions. They may want to talk about their fears, worries, and anxieties. Some may want family and friends present, and some may want to be alone. Others may want someone from the health care team to stay with them. Often, clients prefer to talk at night, either because things are quiet and there are few distractions or because their fears and anxieties are worse at night. The following are ways you can meet a dying client’s needs:

    1. • Listening. If the client needs to share worries and concerns with you, let the client talk. You do not need to talk or worry about saying the wrong thing or finding comforting words. Sometimes, the client does not want to talk but just needs you around. Just being there and listening can be very helpful. If the client requests medical information, spiritual guidance, or counselling, immediately tell your supervisor. Remember, it is not your role to give clients information about their medical conditions.

    Physical Needs

    1. • Pain relief and comfort. Some clients experience severe pain. Health care providers will administer medications to relieve pain; as well, the care plan may call for other pain-relieving measures such as back massages, imagery, soft music, and relaxation techniques. Skin care, personal hygiene, and good alignment also promote comfort. In caring for these clients, follow the client’s care plan.
    2. • Comfort and positioning. Frequent position changes and supportive devices also promote comfort. You may need the help of another worker to turn the client slowly and gently. Semi-Fowler’s position (Fowler’s position with the knees slightly bent) is usually best for easing difficulty with breathing. Ask the client what position is most comfortable.
    3. • Vision and eye care. At the end of life, vision blurs and gradually fails, so the client may find a darkened room frightening. The room should, therefore, be well lit (if the client wishes), but bright lights and glare should be avoided. Since the client’s vision may be failing, explain what you are doing. Secretions may collect in the corners of the client’s eyes. Ease the client’s discomfort by providing eye care. Follow the client’s care plan. It may call for applying protective ointment and moist pads to the client’s eyes.
    4. • Hearing. Many clients, even unconscious ones, are able to hear until the moment of death. Hearing is believed to be one of the last functions that a client loses. Always assume that the client can hear, and continue to introduce yourself and give explanations and reassurance about the care you are giving, even when the client is unconscious. Speak in a normal voice, offer words of comfort, and avoid topics that could upset the client.
    5. • Speech. The client may have difficulty speaking, so it may be hard to understand what they say. Some clients cannot speak at all. Anticipate clients’ needs. Ask “yes” or “no” questions, and avoid asking questions that require long answers. Do not tire the client with questions, but do talk to the client.
    6. • Mouth care. The client’s mouth may feel dry, uncomfortable, or sore, and swallowing may be difficult and uncomfortable. Oral hygiene promotes comfort. Routine mouth care is given if the client can eat and drink. Frequent oral care is given as death nears and when the client has difficulty taking oral fluids. It is important to provide good mouth care, as it is comforting to the client (see Chapters 17 and 23). Mucous membranes can become very dry, and sores can develop in the mouth. Oral hygiene is needed if mucus collects in the mouth and the client cannot swallow. Offer ice chips or small sips of fluids if the client has a dry mouth. Apply moisturizer to relieve dry, chapped lips. Follow the client’s care plan.
    7. • Nostril care. The client’s nostrils may become crusted and irritated, usually by nasal secretions, oxygen cannulas, or nasogastric tubes. Clean the nose carefully. Apply lubricant, as directed by the care plan.
    8. • Skin care. Circulation to the arms and legs slows as death approaches. Hands and feet may feel especially cold to the touch. Skin colour may change, and skin may appear pale or mottled (blotchy) and also feel cold to the touch, but the client is usually not aware of feeling cold. The upper parts of the body may sweat as circulation to the peripheral parts fails. The body temperature may rise, and blankets may make the client feel too warm and cause restlessness, so only light bed coverings should be applied. Skin care, bathing, frequent position changes, and fresh garments and linens help keep the client comfortable. Measures must be taken to prevent pressure injuries.
    9. • Elimination. The dying client may have urinary or fecal incontinence. Follow the care plan for the use of incontinence products or bed protectors. Keep the client dry and clean. Give perineal care, as needed. Constipation and urinary retention are common, and the care plan may call for the use of enemas and catheter care, which may be delegated to you. Remember to follow Standard Practices.
    10. • Nutrition. Nutritional needs decrease as the body can no longer deal with digesting food, so amounts of food should be reduced, and easily digestible types of food offered often.

    Providing Compassionate Care

    The Dying Client

    • • Dignity. Treat the dying client with respect, dignity, warmth, and empathy. Be sensitive to the client’s wishes. Listen when the client wants to talk. Respect the client’s need for quiet and silence. Do not avoid the client if you feel uncomfortable or sad. Try to understand how your client is feeling.
    • Whether a client is dying at home or in a facility, the environment should be comfortable and pleasant, quiet, well lit, and well ventilated. Try to keep equipment and supplies out of view, as some clients may find equipment such as suction machines and drainage containers upsetting to look at. Display expressions of care (such as flowers and cards) in clear view, as some clients may find these comforting.
    • Be sensitive to the client’s needs. Provide comfort through care and touch if culturally appropriate for the client.
    • Respect the client’s religious views and practices. Do not impose your views on the client. Treat religious items and other personal possessions with care and respect.
    • • Independence. Encourage the client to do things without help, if possible. Offer to assist before actually doing something for the client. The client has the right to autonomy.
    • • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the dying client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. Encourage the client to share any wishes the client may have. Provide comfort care whenever possible.
    • • Preferences. The client has the right to make choices about their care, treatment, and environment, so respect these choices. Ask before providing care. Respect a client’s decision to refuse treatment and not prolong life. In all health care settings, the client and family should be free to arrange the room as they wish, to reflect the client’s choices. Allowing such changes shows respect and helps the client feel cared for.
    • • Privacy. The dying client has the right to privacy. Remember to not expose the client unnecessarily during care. The client has the right not to have their body seen by others, so follow proper screening and privacy procedures. Provide privacy during prayer and spiritual moments. The client and family have the right to have visits in private. In facilities, privacy may need to be arranged; for example, a roommate may have to leave while the family visits. Some clients may ask to see a spiritual advisor or chaplain. The client also has the right to confidentiality; protect this right before and after death. Share information about diagnosis, health, and treatment only with those involved in the client’s care. Maintain confidentiality about the client’s final moments and cause of death as well as statements and reactions of the family.
    • • Safety. Clients who are dying are at high risk for falls, choking, and other accidents. Know the safety measures that must be taken for each client. The dying client has the right to be free from abuse, mistreatment, and neglect. It must be ensured that neither family members nor health care team members abuse or mistreat the client. Follow the safety measures described in Chapter 20.

    Comforting the Family

    The approaching death of a loved one is a stressful time for family members, who may be very tired, sad, and tearful. Watching a loved one die and dealing with the eventual loss of that person can be very painful. If the family wants a spiritual advisor or chaplain to be present with them, let your supervisor know immediately.

    Finding comforting words may be very hard. Show your feelings by being respectful, empathetic, and supportive. You may need to listen to a family member who wants to talk. However, do not feel that you have to talk. Use touch, if appropriate, to show your concern. In a home care setting, you can show your support in many ways. You might help by taking on household tasks while the family cares for the dying client, or you might provide respite care to give the caregivers a break. Follow the client’s care plan.

    In facilities, family members are allowed to stay 24 hours a day if death is imminent. Palliative care rooms are available so that family members and the client can be together. The health care team helps make them as comfortable as possible. Unfortunately, if a client has been diagnosed with a contagious illness such as COVID-19, the client may not be allowed to have loved ones at their side, even if dying (see the Think About Safety: Caring for a Dying Client Who Was Diagnosed With COVID-19 box).

    Think About Safety

    Caring for a Dying Client Who Was Diagnosed With COVID-19

    Special isolation precautions, use of PPE, and disinfection of equipment is necessary when a client is diagnosed with highly contagious and dangerous illnesses such as COVID-19. These precautions are meant to ensure the safety of the other clients, families, and staff (see Chapter 19).

    In these situations, the client (or agency) may not be allowed visitors, even if they are dying. Families will only be able to communicate with their loved one by way of cellphone or a social media platform, Staff will be responsible for providing safe, compassionate care; making necessary electronic connections; and holding the phone or tablet for the client and family to see each other and say farewell if the client's health deteriorates. The staff will often have the important responsibility of being the only ones present when the client dies.

    No matter what the setting, you need to respect the family’s right to privacy while taking care not to neglect care because the family is present. Most facilities allow family members to give care to their loved one. When they do, you may help them take beverage or meal breaks. Family members often hesitate to leave because they do not want their loved one to be alone. Offering to remain with the client can be a great relief for the family and make them feel better about taking a much-needed break.

    Palliative Care

    The World Health Organization (WHO) defines palliative care as “an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing the problem associated with life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual.”1 The focus of palliative care is on controlling symptoms and providing support to the client and family when there is no response to treatment.

    The idea of hospice care began with giving a place of comfort to weary travellers in Europe during the Middle Ages. Centuries later, in 1967, Dr. Cicely Saunders opened the first free-standing research and teaching hospice in London, England. In 1976, pilot projects for palliative care were started at St. Boniface Hospital in Winnipeg and the Royal Victoria Hospital in Montreal. Soon after, hospice services in Canada proliferated both in the community and in hospital settings.

    The practice of hospice palliative care is relatively young. In Canada, it began in the 1970s and has evolved rapidly. The term hospice palliative care was created to recognize the convergence of hospice and palliative care into one movement that has the same principles and ethics of practice.2 However, the terms palliative care and hospice care are sometimes used interchangeably to mean the same thing—the compassionate care of dying persons that is directed at comfort and pain management, whether in a facility or the client’s home.

    Depending on your location, the term palliative can refer to the care given, whereas the term hospice can refer to the place where the care is given. However, in some provinces and territories, palliative care is still used to describe this care provided by health care teams within a specialized unit in a health care facility.

    The most common facility setting is the palliative care unit within a hospital. Long-term care facilities also provide hospice palliative care. Across Canada, there is increased awareness that many people choose to die at home, surrounded by familiar surroundings and loved ones. The support worker has an important role in assisting the dying person and their family by providing compassionate care. Some cities and towns have community hospice organizations, which are usually nonprofit organizations run by volunteers and which sometimes work closely with home care programs.

    Not all people who are dying wish to die in their own homes, and yet many do not want to be admitted to a hospital. Separate facilities called hospices also provide palliative care. Many hospices provide care in houses that once were private homes and that are often located in quiet neighbourhoods in parklike settings. The goal is to provide a comfortable, peaceful setting and to ensure that comfort measures are provided to the client. Some hospices provide palliative care to specific client groups, such as those dying of acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) or cancer.

    Hospice or palliative care is not intended to prolong life but to provide the best quality of life during the final days before death. The three main goals of hospice or palliative care are as follows:

    • 1. To assist in controlling the pain and symptoms of illness. Most terminally ill clients fear pain more than death itself. Some of a dying client’s fears and anxieties can be alleviated by providing good pain control.
    • 2. To ensure that death is a natural process. The goal of care is not to prolong life but to provide the best quality of life during the final days before death. As a result, medical interventions such as respirators and tube feedings are not used to keep clients alive.
    • 3. To provide compassionate care. A team of professionals, nonprofessionals, and volunteers with a special interest in the field of palliative care work with sensitivity to meet the needs of the dying client and the family. The right to die with dignity is strongly upheld.

    Because palliative care is demanding and complex, it requires the expertise of more than one health care provider and, therefore, a team approach. The common goal or purpose behind all activities is to assist the dying client and their family in the best way possible. Team members vary, depending on the situation (TABLE 47.1). Some clients may choose to include in the team a close friend as well as family members, all of whom can provide much emotional and physical care. Even in facility settings, family members and close friends are encouraged to help with the care of clients, and clients themselves can be active participants in their own care.

    Legal Issues Surrounding Death and Dying

    A person’s right to choose the time and the way they die is a much-debated issue at present. Some clients do not want machines or other measures keeping them alive, so the client’s informed consent is legally required by all provinces and territories for any such lack of treatment (see Chapter 5). Clients may give or withhold consent and make their own decisions about care. Some clients, however, may be unable to do so because of confusion, dementia, or other impairments, so a substitute decision maker often makes decisions for these clients.

    Advance Directive

    An advance directive (or living will) is a legal document in which a person states specific wishes about future health care, treatment, and personal care. Health care includes all medical treatment: diagnostic, therapeutic, preventive, and palliative. Personal care includes shelter, hygiene, nutrition, clothing, and safety.

    An advance directive is used when the person can no longer make decisions or express personal wishes. Advance directives allow people to control their future health care. Without an advance directive, family members sometimes have to make difficult decisions on behalf of a loved one, and such decisions can result in conflict in the family.

    Every province and territory has legislation about advance directives. Most advance directives have a dual function:

      1. They allow a person to appoint a representative (proxy) to make medical-care, treatment, and personal-care decisions.
      1. They give written instructions about medical care and treatment and personal care.

    People often use advance directives to forbid certain types of treatment and care when there is no hope of recovery. For example, some people do not want life-sustaining measures that support or maintain life (e.g., tube feedings, ventilators, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation [CPR]). These measures and other machines keep a person alive when death is likely. An advance directive may instruct physicians not to start measures that prolong dying or to remove measures that prolong dying.

    Advance directives are known by different names and have different powers, depending on provincial or territorial legislation—in Alberta, advance directives are called personal directives; in Manitoba, they are called health care directives; and in Ontario, these documents are called powers of attorney for personal care (in which a substitute decision maker is chosen). It is important to note that the word attorney in this use has a different meaning in Ontario or in your province or territory from what it means in the United States. A person who is appointed an attorney for personal care does not have to be a lawyer.

    Most people appoint a family member or friend to be their representative or proxy. In some cases, a lawyer may be named. The advance directive comes into effect only when a client can no longer make decisions about health care and personal care. At this point, the proxy, who has been given the legal right to make decisions for the client, steps in to make decisions on the client’s behalf.

    In most provinces and territories, residents name a proxy to make decisions about personal care as well as medical care and treatment. However, in some parts of the country, a proxy can be used only for decisions about medical care and treatment.

    Not all clients name a proxy. When no proxy is named, family members, in consultation with the physician, decide for the client. Some provinces, however, require a court application before any decision can be made on behalf of an incapable person.

    “Do Not Resuscitate” Orders

    When sudden and unexpected death is possible, every effort is made to save the client’s life. An emergency response system is activated, and CPR is started. In facilities, nurses, physicians, and emergency staff immediately get to the client’s bedside, and in the community, ambulances and paramedics rush to the scene, bringing emergency and life-saving equipment with them. CPR and other life-support measures are continued until the client is resuscitated or until a physician declares the client dead. Clients and new staff need to clearly understand the policies and procedures of these facilities regarding resuscitation protocols.

    Physicians often write “do not resuscitate” (DNR) or “no code” orders for clients who are not expected to recover. Such orders mean that the client will not be resuscitated and is allowed to die in peace and with dignity. The orders are written after consulting with the client and the family. The family or the proxy, in consultation with the physician, makes the decision about the DNR order if the client is not mentally able. Some people prepare an advance directive that contains DNR orders.

    As a support worker, you may not agree with care and resuscitation decisions. However, you must follow the client’s (or proxy’s) wishes and the physician’s orders, even if they are against your personal, religious, or cultural beliefs. If you are not comfortable with care and resuscitation decisions, discuss the matter with your supervisor.

    Medical Assistance in Dying (MAID) Legislation

    Many issues must be dealt with and discussed when a client is faced with a long (and sometime difficult) battle with a terminal illness. Most people fear pain and suffering. The goal of all palliative care is to reduce pain and suffering as much as possible for that person using pain medications and soothing comfort measures.

    However, some people believe that “dying with dignity” should include removing breathing machines, feeding tubes, and other medical devices that only prolong life but do not cure the client. There are other people who believe that a terminal illness that brings pain and suffering is not life at all, and that the client should have the right to choose when they will die. These people support assisted suicide (suicide that is aided by providing the client with the means to do it or the information on how to end their own life) or euthanasia (the act of putting another person painlessly to death).

    The assisted suicide debate has been argued at every level in the Canadian legal system. In 2015, after many legal challenges of the federal law that criminalized assisted suicide, the Supreme Court of Canada legally reversed the position of Canada’s Criminal Code and on June 6, 2016, Medical Assistance in Dying (MAID) legislation came into force. This means that assisted suicide became legal for any adult who is legally capable of making such a choice rationally as long as (1) the person clearly consents to the termination of their life, and (2) the person has an irreversible illness, disease, or disability that is causing intolerable pain or suffering and that cannot be reversed or acceptably reduced. This hotly debated law will have a wide-reaching impact on many Canadian lives and business industries, including medical tourism, health care, and law. At the time of this writing, physician, law, and health care worker groups are examining its legal implications (BOX 47.2).

    Grief

    Loss is one of life’s most stressful events (see Chapter 8). After the loss of a client or loved one (whatever the circumstance), it may take time for the person to heal emotionally. As each circumstance is different, each person responds to grief in their own way. Some people may need the support of family or friends to help them cope with grief, while others seek the assistance of trained professionals when struggling to cope with the changes in their lives.

    What Is Grief?

    Grief (also called bereavement) is the emotional response to a loss or a death.3 Every client and family member will grieve differently, based on past experiences, coping strategies, cultural beliefs, and spiritual beliefs. Coping with grief involves the processes of mourning and of adapting to the loss. A person will move back and forth through a series of stages and tasks many times over several years before the grieving process is complete.

    Although no one really “gets over” a loss, the individual can heal and adapt to the loss. Many people associate grief with the death of an important person or pet. However, people also experience grief after any important loss that affects their life, such as the loss of a job or relationship. Grief after diagnosis of an illness or other health challenge is also common.

    Grief is complicated and is different for everybody (BOX 47.3). Feelings, thoughts, reactions, and challenges related to grief are very personal. Some people have thoughts or feelings that seem to be at odds with each other. For example, someone may feel very depressed about the loss but accept the loss at the same time.

    In most cases, people navigate through grief with help from loved ones and other supporters and, in time, go back to their daily life. People may feel shocked, sad, angry, scared, anxious, or even numb, having a hard time feeling any emotions at all. Many people even feel relief or peace after a loss. The intensity of grief changes a lot over time. Holidays can often bring up strong feelings, for example. People work through grief in their own time and on their own path.

    Theories of Grief

    Grief can be both an overwhelming and debilitating state. A number of theories about the stages of the grieving process have been created to better understand its effect on people. Some are discussed here and in TABLE 47.2.

    Kübler-Ross’s Stages of Grief

    Kübler-Ross’s theory identifies five stages that the client and family may undergo while grieving. These stages can be relevant when experiencing other types of grief, such as the loss felt in a marital breakup. Not everyone who is grieving will go through every stage or experience them in any specific order. Examples of behaviours during these stages are as follows:

    1. • During denial, people grieving refuse to believe that anything is wrong.
    2. • In the anger stage, they may strike at anything or anybody.
    3. • During the bargaining stage, they try to postpone the loss and hope the loss can be prevented.
    4. • In depression, they may feel overwhelmed and lonely and may withdraw from others.
    5. • With acceptance, they move forward with their lives and appreciate their memories of the person.

    Bowlby’s Phases of Mourning

    Bowlby’s attachment theory is the foundation for his theory on mourning. He describes four phases of mourning:

    1. • The numbing phase may last for a few hours or longer. Mourners may describe it as feeling stunned.
    2. • The yearning and searching phase arouses emotional outbursts and acute distress in most people. Trying to suppress these emotions will increase the amount of time needed to move through this phase.
    3. • During the disorganization and despair phase, they try to examine how and why the loss occurred. They may try to blame someone for the loss.
    4. • The final phase is reorganization. During this stage, people take on new roles, form new relationships, and acquire new skills.

    Worden’s Four Tasks of Mourning

    Worden’s four tasks of mourning suggest that people who mourn can help themselves and be helped by people around them. Working through these tasks usually takes at least 1 year.

    1. • Task 1 —to accept the reality of the loss. Even when death is expected, there is a period of disbelief.
    2. • Task 2 —to work through the period of grief. There is emotional pain. For those who try to suppress the pain, this phase will last longer.
    3. • Task 3 —to adjust to the environment in which the deceased is missing. As time passes, friends and family make less frequent contact, and the person starts to feel the full impact of the loss.
    4. • Task 4 —to emotionally relocate the deceased and move on with life. The deceased will not be forgotten but will take a less prominent place in the person’s life.

    Signs of Death

    Signs that death is near may occur rapidly or slowly. These include the following:

    1. • Loss of movement, muscle tone, and sensation —usually begins in the feet and legs and eventually spreads to other parts. When a client’s mouth muscles relax, the jaw drops, and the mouth may stay open. The client’s face often looks peaceful.
    2. • Slowing of peristalsis and other digestive functions —abdominal distension; fecal incontinence, urinary incontinence, or both; fecal impaction; nausea; and vomiting are common. The client usually refuses to eat or drink.
    3. • Failure of circulation —the pulse is fast, weak, and irregular. Blood pressure begins to fall. Skin may have a mottled appearance.
    4. • Failure of the respiratory system —presence of Cheyne–Stokes respirations. These respirations gradually increase in rate and depth, and then become shallow and slow, and then they stop for up to 30 seconds. Mucus collects in the airway and causes a wet, gurgling sound as the client breathes, known as the death rattle.
    5. • Excessive fatigue and sleep —as the client’s metabolism slows down, the client may begin to sleep for prolonged periods throughout the day and night. The client may even become difficult to awaken or may lose consciousness. However, some clients do remain conscious until the moment of death.
    6. • Changes in eating —the client may not be interested in eating food once loved or may have cravings for odd food.
    7. • Social withdrawal —as the client’s circulation decreases and organs begin to fail, the client’s body is beginning to shut down. The client may gradually seem to lose interest in their surroundings; they may stop talking, may seem disinterested in activities (or people) and turn away visitors.
    8. • Coolness in the tips of the fingers and toes —the client’s blood circulation slows and shifts away from the extremities in order to help the client’s major organs. As a result, a noticeable cooling, development of a bluish tint to the client’s skin, and cyanosis of the nail beds occur.

    The physiological signs of death include absence of pulse, respirations, or blood pressure and fixed, dilated pupils. A physician determines that death has occurred and pronounces the person dead. In some parts of Canada, nurses are allowed to pronounce death when death is expected (see the Focus on Home Care: When Death Occurs box).

    Focus on Home Care

    When Death Occurs

    Some clients die at home, and you may be present at the time of death. Know your employer’s policies and procedures. Call your supervisor, and follow the directions. Be supportive of the family. Respect the client’s and the family’s cultural values.

    Care of the Body After Death

    Care of the body after (post) death (mortem) is called postmortem care, which begins after the client is pronounced dead and when no autopsy (an examination of the body to determine the cause of death) is required. You may be asked to assist with postmortem care, and during this process, you may have contact with blood or body fluids. Follow Standard Practices.

    Postmortem care is done to maintain the body’s appearance by helping prevent discoloration and skin damage. Remember, the right to privacy and the right to be treated with dignity and respect should be preserved even after a client’s death. Remember to respect cultural diversity for this care as well.

    Within 2 to 3 hours after death, rigor mortis develops. Rigor mortis is the stiffness or rigidity (rigor) of skeletal muscles that occurs after death (mortis).

    Postmortem care involves positioning the body in normal alignment before rigor mortis sets in. The family may want to see the body before it is taken to the morgue or funeral home. The body should be placed in a natural position for this viewing.

    In some cases, the body is prepared initially only for viewing. Postmortem care is completed later at the funeral home. If the client dies at home, the funeral home usually provides postmortem care.

    Postmortem care may involve repositioning the body to clean soiled areas and to put the body in good alignment. Movement of the body can cause sounds to come from the body as air is expelled. Do not be frightened by these sounds; they are normal and expected.

    As a support worker, you will not have full responsibility for postmortem care, but you may assist in the procedure.

    Beliefs About the Body After Death

    People have various beliefs about the body’s form after death. Some believe that the body keeps its physical form, whereas others believe that only the spirit, or soul, is present in the afterlife. Some believe in the concept of reincarnation, which is the belief that the spirit or soul is reborn in another human body or in another life form. These beliefs can influence the following:

    1. • The client’s (or family’s) choice to be buried or cremated
    2. • Whether an autopsy should be performed
    3. • Whether or not organs should be donated for transplant
    4. • Whether the body may be donated to science

    Funeral Planning

    It can be difficult for clients and the family to discuss funeral arrangements. Some clients or families may have already made arrangements for the funeral or have discussed their wishes with an executor or outlined this in their will (see Chapter 5). If prior arrangements have not been made, funeral directors will guide the family through the process and provide counselling sessions after the funeral to provide the necessary paperwork and information to settle the estate.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • As a support worker, you will be required to help meet the physical, social, emotional, intellectual, and spiritual needs of clients who are dying and to support their families through the process.

    • Personality, culture, religion, age, and experience influence people’s attitudes toward death.

    • As a support worker, you need to be aware of the cultural beliefs of your client and respect the death and dying rituals of the client and the family.

    • Palliative and hospice care may be available in your community. The goal of care is to provide comfort and support to a dying client in the client’s home or in a facility.

    • As a support worker, you need to know your employer’s policies and your legal obligations regarding advance directives and “do not resuscitate” orders.

    • The stages or phases of grief the client, the family, and you experience will vary in each case.

    • Care of the body after death will depend on the cultural beliefs of your client. Follow your employer’s policy.

    • Funeral directors are trained to assist families through the necessary processes and paperwork. They can also refer families to other support services.
