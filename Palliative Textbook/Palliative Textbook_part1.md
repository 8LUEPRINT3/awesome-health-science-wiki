Integrating a Palliative Approach: Essentials for Personal Support Workers
This second edition of the text will equip frontline caregivers to respond to the changing needs of any per-
son living with life-limiting illness and their family. You will learn best practice skills for participating fully as a
vital member of the health care team. Integrating a Palliative Approach will increase your confidence and com-
petence in providing compassionate palliative care for the dying person and their family by developing your
capacity to: 1) integrate a palliative approach and support the person and family through decline and during last
days and hours; 2) provide culturally safe palliative care that is free of systemic bias and racism; 3) understand
and provide psychosocial care for the person and family; and 4) develop self-care practices to sustain you. This
is a rare text that engages you with its warmth and heart—it is an essential resource for all frontline caregivers.

DEDICATION
I dedicate this book to personal support workers. I will refer to you in this text as personal support
workers, PSWs, because you support people in such a personal way. I acknowledge that you may
work under a variety of other titles such as: resident care assistant, nurse’s assistant, home support
worker, caregiver, community health worker. Whatever your title, I honour you. I thank you.
For years you have been referred to as the “heart and hands” of the health care team. Now, during
the COVID-19 pandemic, you are being called heroes. Yes, you are, the hearts, hands and heroes of
the health care team. I salute you.
I also dedicate this edition to Ann-Marie Gilbert who led the completion of this text. I thank you.
This resource is dedicated to all the fabulous personal support

About the Cover
The arbutus tree shown on the cover of this book is native to southeastern Vancouver Island where
I live, the nearby Gulf Islands, the adjacent coast of mainland British Columbia, and some areas
along the west coast of the United States. Often rooted in the crevices of rock faces along the
shore, this tree, with its gnarled and twisted branches, hangs over the edge and is blown about by
the wind. The smooth, papery bark peels off as new bark grows. Each arbutus is unique.
The arbutus inspires me with its ability to grow, survive, and even thrive in such rough and rocky
areas. This tree graces the cover of my books because to me the arbutus symbolizes the strength of
the human spirit, the amazing ability of human beings to survive, grow, and even thrive in difficult,
harsh, and even traumatic situations. Like the tree, we too get gnarled as we age. And like the tree,
we are all unique.
The arbutus reminds me of those I care for, those who grow in the midst of dying, who grow as
they care for family and friends, and those who grow during their grief and bereavement.
The arbutus reminds me of you, the many personal support workers that I have met over the years.
You also struggle with personal and work challenges, you provide excellent care, you grow in the
midst of challenges, and your stories and lives inspire me.
I wish you well. I hope that you will find great satisfaction and growth in doing this work.

PREFACE
I came to hospice nursing as a child.
A few of my earliest recollections of death include a dead rat and a lovely transparent dead leaf.
I tried to nurse both back to life, with no success. When my siblings and I found a dead bird, we
decided we were not doing well in the healing department and opted for burial.
During my teenage years, I lived with my aunt Frankie. Frankie, a nurse, was the master family care-
giver. During my teens she cared for many family members and friends through aging, illness, and
dying. I learned that death is a part of life.
As a teen and young adult, I had friends who died. I learned that even the young die. I learned
that people die from cancer, accidents, and suicide. I learned that when people are in need, you
respond.
When I was in my 20s, my uncle and my father died. My uncle died after months of excruciating
pain, not able to access morphine because, he was told, it needed to be saved for when the pain
was “really bad,” and because “he might become addicted.” My father died with congestion in his
lungs, gasping for air. No medications were available to relieve his breathlessness or decrease his
congestion. I learned that dying can be painful.
In the late 1980s, as the hospice palliative care movement spread across North America, I began
nursing at Victoria Hospice. I learned about symptoms and symptom management. I learned
about loss and grief, last days and hours, rituals, transitions, and traditions. I learned about people,
about families, about communication. I became more comfortable talking about dying, grief, loss,
and death. I learned that symptoms can often be managed, and that death can be less painful
when palliative care principles and practices are integrated in care. I also learned that care is best
provided by team.
In this second edition of the text, I have aimed to ensure that content is current and addresses
the palliative care competencies identified by provincial leaders in Nova Scotia, British Columbia,
Ontario and Alberta and the Ontario, in particular, the competencies for caring for the First Nation,
Inuit, Metis and urban Indigenous people. Given the statistics on those who have experienced or
are experiencing trauma, I also integrated a trauma informed approach to care throughout the
text.
If you are a Personal Support Worker or a caregiver, it is my sincere hope that this resource and
the companion workbook will help you be more competent and feel more confident in providing
excellent compassionate care for the dying person1 and their family. I hope that you will be able to
serve as a leader in your community in strengthening the integration of a palliative approach in all
care settings.

Starting the Journey—How to Use This Book
Welcome to the second edition of Integrating a Palliative Approach: Essentials for Personal Support
Workers. We’re happy to be here with you, supporting you as you learn the skills, attitudes and
knowledge for providing compassionate palliative and end-of-life care.
Whew! That’s a mouthful isn’t it? However, you have nothing to fear! This set of resources is de-
signed with you, the learner, at the forefront. Designed and written to be interesting and easy-to-
understand. Knowing your course load when in school, and the workload when providing care,
we knew we needed resources that captivated our learners. We hope that you enjoy your learning
experience.
Let me tell you what is new in this text.
Touchstones: Through the text are “touchstones” which identify competencies. Use the
touchstones to help you to understand the key concepts for providing palliative and end-of-
life care.
Best practices: Develop best practices to ensure your success as a caregiver, in any setting, to
any age group. You can apply the best practices in your practice every day.
Cultural Safety: Embedded in the text are key strategies to help you communicate
with and provide care respectfully for any person from any culture. Based on Canada’s
Truth and Reconciliation Commission Calls to Action, learning to provide culturally safe care
with a trauma-informed approach will help you provide excellent care for any person and their
family. The feather icon will draw your attention to cultural considerations. The feather was
chosen because birds often hold a role cross-culturally as important intermediaries, messen-
gers, and guides for knowledge.
So take a deep breath in—and breathe out slowly. We’ll get through this together.
If you are a PSW student, use the companion workbook as a guide for studying the textbook.
The workbook builds on the basics. Do not feel you need to learn everything right now. Follow
your instructor’s directions about what content you need to know at this point, and what you can
put aside to learn in the coming months or years.
If you are a PSW already working in the field, you may want to read this book cover to cover,
use it as a reference as you provide care for people and as questions arise, or share it with people
whose loved ones are dying.
I believe that teaching is an invitation to learn and learning is an invitation to teach. As you learn,
share your learning with your colleagues. As you share your learning, you will not only help them
learn the importance of integrating a palliative approach into caregiving, but also deepen your
own knowledge..

The stuttering decline (Figure 3) occurs over multiple years
and describes the decline of people with chronic, life-l imiting
illnesses leading to organ failure such as congestive heart fail-
ure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, end-stage kidney
disease, autoimmune dieases such as HIV, and comorbidities
such as Parkinson’s disease or dementia-related illnesses. This
pattern alternates between periods of substantial decline
and periods of partial recovery. A period of decline may result
from a worsening of a chronic or life-limiting illness, or from
a fall, a fracture, the flu, pneumonia, and so on. On occasion,
the person may require hospitalization and treatment, or they
may require an adjustment of medications to help stabilize
their condition. Following treatment and stabilization, the
person may recover most or some of their previous level of
functioning and may again enjoy some of the activities they
used to do. The person may be stable for weeks or months
and then experience another substantial period of decline
when their life-limiting illness changes or they experience
another infection, fall, or flu. Each period of decline contrib-
utes to an overall decline. The last three months often show
the highest rate of decline. Eventually, the person is not able
to recover from a period of decline and dies.
The stuttering decline can feel ambiguous for the family
because the person may decline and recover repeatedly.
