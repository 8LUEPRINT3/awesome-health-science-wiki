#### Elements of a Resumé

All resumés include information on the following:

1. •
   Experience.
    Employers want to know what you have done that qualifies you for the position. Experience does not have to be paid work. It can come from volunteer work, field placements, special projects, and recreational activities. Stress your skills and accomplishments rather than your duties and responsibilities.
2. •
   Education.
    Employers want to know if you have the educational qualifications required for the position. Your resumé should include the exact name of your certificate, the name of your educational institution, and the year you graduated. Also include additional training and any awards or honours you received.

The following elements are optional:

1. •
   Objective.
    An objective on a resumé states your immediate career goal and briefly lists relevant skills, abilities, and personal qualities. It appears at the top of the first page of your resumé, right after your name and address.
2. •
   Profile.
    A career profile summarizes your qualifications, highlighting particular skills and experience in one brief paragraph. It usually appears on the first page of your resumé, right after your name and address. Most people include either an objective or a profile but rarely both.
3. •
   Interests.
    Most career consultants advise against including a separate section on interests because it takes up valuable space. Instead, they suggest incorporating accomplishments in sports or hobbies into other sections of the resumé and doing so only if it is relevant to the job. For example, you may want to mention your skills in curling to show you are an experienced team player.

### Getting Started on Your Resumé

Think of the resumé as an advertisement for those of your experiences, skills, and qualities that an employer might be seeking. Start by making a list of the skills and qualities an employer would want in a support worker; then make a list of your own skills and qualities and the experiences that helped you to develop those qualities.

If you are not able to identify your skills and qualities, start by thinking about your experiences. Ask yourself what you learned from each experience and how your experiences have influenced you.

### Organizing Your Resumé

There are two basic resumé styles: (1) the chronological resumé and (2) the functional resumé.

#### Chronological Resumé

A chronological resumé highlights employment history, starting with your most current employment and working in reverse chronological order (backward) through earlier jobs ([FIGURE 13.1](#f0010)). Because of its emphasis on work history, this style of resumé is best for those individuals with the following:

1. • A steady history of employment in their chosen field
2. • At least 2 years’ employment with one employer
3. • Few gaps between jobs

#### Functional Resumé

A functional resumé highlights skills or functions and briefly lists positions held ([FIGURE 13.2](#f0015)). Because it emphasizes skills and abilities, rather than specific jobs, a functional resumé is best for those individuals with the following:

1. • Little work experience in their chosen field
2. •
   Long periods during which they were not in the workforce
3. • Frequent job changes

A functional resumé should include your employment history with dates. A resumé that excludes this information might suggest that you have something to hide.

### Creating Your Resumé

You will probably need to prepare several drafts of your resumé before you are satisfied. As you prepare your drafts, ask yourself the following questions:

1. •
   Does my resumé highlight the qualifications that are outlined in the job posting?
    It is important to mention any of your qualities and qualifications that match what the employer is seeking.
2. •
   Is the information relevant?
    You have limited space to make an impression. Mention only those experiences that say something about how well you would perform on the job.
3. •
   Have I been completely honest?
    Emphasize your strengths, but do not misrepresent yourself.
   It is unethical to lie.
    Do not change your qualifications, job titles, education, or dates of employment. Do not exaggerate your limited accomplishments if you lack experience. Remember that some employers prefer to hire inexperienced workers because they may be more motivated, more flexible, and easier to train than those with experience.
4. •
   Have I expressed myself clearly?
    Be concise and avoid using long words. Revise your draft to make sure it is clear. Avoid giving information in paragraphs; use bullet points instead. Sentences should be short and direct. Start them with action words and avoid use of the word “I.” Remember, your resumé should be only one or two pages in length.
5. •
   Is my resumé consistent?
    Set up the page, headings, and sentences in a consistent format. Use bullets, bold type, italic type, or underlining in the same style throughout the document. Do not spell the same word two different ways (e.g., honours and honors). Use the same grammatical form for each point.
6. •
   Have I accounted for all my time?
    Do not leave blocks of time that are unaccounted for.
7. •
   Is my resumé correct?
    Read over the final document several times slowly and carefully. Do not trust your word processor’s spell-check feature to catch all spelling errors (e.g., their, they’re, and there are not always caught if misused). Some people have problems proofreading their own work because they read what they think they have written rather than what is actually on the page.

Begin by reading every word out loud. Make sure that what you have written makes sense as you hear it. If you find that you cannot judge your own work impartially and objectively, ask someone to read it aloud for you. Many employers use your resumé as a predictor for the type of support worker you will be. If there are errors, they may assume that you do not pay attention to your work, and you will not be called for an interview.

Follow this step by reading your document backward, starting at the end of the resumé and reading each sentence backward. You are more likely to catch typos and spelling mistakes when you read it this way. When you are satisfied with your resumé, ask at least two reliable people to proofread it.

### Formatting and Printing Your Resumé

To make a good impression, your resumé must look neat and professional:

1. • Choose a simple, professional-looking font (typeface) such as Times New Roman or Arial in an 11 or 12 font size. Do not choose fancy or script-type fonts.
2. • Do not use more than one font.
3. • Keep the use of graphic devices such as bold or italicized type to a minimum.
4. • Do not crowd too much material on a page.
5. • It should be printed single-sided and stapled in the top left-hand corner.

Most computer programs have preset resumé designs that you can use to format your resumé. Many books and websites also present a variety of designs you can use. Some websites offer services such as formatting your resumé and posting it on the Internet. Because resumé formats change constantly, it may be worth securing the services of a professional resumé writer. Check the Internet for names in your area. Your school or college may also provide this service through the career centre.

If you are submitting your resumé in person or by mail, print it on good-quality white or ivory bond 8½˝ x 11˝ paper.
Never
 use designed or patterned paper. If your resumé runs to two pages, staple the pages together.
Never
 fold the corners down together. Paper clips can be used to hold your pages together, but many employers prefer staples.

### The Professional Portfolio

Many interviewers or clients appreciate or even expect to see a portfolio when interviewing an applicant for the first time. A portfolio is a binder or electronic file containing the applicant’s certificates, letters of recommendation, awards, proof of volunteering, or other pertinent information. Items should be included in the order they were obtained, with the most recent item appearing first, the second most recent after that, and so on. This way, the interviewer does not have to sift through the contents of the portfolio. Its purpose is to easily reveal information about the applicant to the interviewer. Your completed portfolio should contain the following:

1. • Copy of your resumé and cover letter, individualized for each specific employer
2. • List of references (with complete contact information) that you are prepared to leave with the interviewer. Include their title (e.g., RN, Director of Care), as well as how they know you.
3. • Certificates that you have achieved
4. • Letters of recommendation and awards
5. • Workshops that you attended, along with the dates that you attended them
6. • Proof of volunteering
7. • Immunization record
8. • Police check (or vulnerable sector screen)
9. • Letters of reference from previous employers or clients
10. • Any other pertinent information

#### Hardcopy Portfolio

Some employers ask for applicants to bring in copies of these items so they can browse through it during the interview. Each item (and a photocopy) within the portfolio should be placed in a plastic page protector and inserted into the binder in an organized manner. It is easiest when the items are sorted according to categories and labelled page dividers separate each section.

The interviewer or potential client may wish to keep photocopies of some of the pages contained in your portfolio.

#### Electronic Portfolio

Some employers may request a copy of your portfolio to be sent electronically prior to an interview, so it is also advisable that you prepare an electronic portfolio. Each of the above items should be carefully scanned and placed in order, and the electronic portfolio should be made available as either an electronic file or a link that can be sent to the employer.

### Selecting References

A reference is a person who can speak to a potential employer about your skills, abilities, and personal qualities. Determine who you want as references early in the job search. Do not use family members or friends as references. Previous employers are usually the best people to use as references. Other possibilities are people in positions of authority, such as teachers and camp directors. Here are some basic tips about references:

1. •
   Ask for the reference.
    Do not use someone as a reference without asking the person. Explain the type of work you are looking for. Do not assume the person will speak positively about you. Ask the person what they would say are your strengths and weaknesses. Be reasonably certain the person will speak positively about you before listing that person as a reference. If you are applying for more than one position, be sure to ask if the person would give you a reference for each position.
2. •
   Make sure contact information is correct.
    Make sure you have accurate phone numbers or email addresses for your references to give prospective employers.
3. •
   Keep your references informed.
    Let your references know when you are going for a job interview so that they can be prepared for a phone call from your prospective employer. A reference who is prepared is more likely to give you a good reference.
4. •
   Follow up.
    Keep your references informed about your job search. If you got the job, thank them for their help. If you did not get the job, let them know.

## Preparing a Letter of Application

A letter of application, also called a cover letter, is included with a résumé. There are two kinds of letters of application:

* 1. A solicited letter of application responds to an advertised position ([FIGURE 13.3](#f0020)).
* 2. An unsolicited letter of application inquires about potential job openings ([FIGURE 13.4](#f0025)).

A letter of application should not simply summarize the information in your resumé. It should be typed in the same font as your resumé and focus on what you can offer by summarizing how your skills and experiences are relevant to the job you are seeking. A good letter of application is persuasive, professional, and personalized. Try to find out the specific person who is responsible for hiring for the position, and direct your correspondence to that person. Most administrators do not like to receive applications or resumés from job seekers when there is a designated individual and department to deal with recruitment and hiring.

### Determining the Employer’s Needs

If a letter of application is solicited, study the advertisement carefully to determine what exactly the employer is looking for. Describe your experiences and skills that match what the employer wants. Consider if you have skills and qualities that are not listed in the advertisement but that could contribute to the position advertised. Even if you are new to support work, your course should have provided you with information about those qualities.

If you write an unsolicited letter, find out as much as you can about the employer first. As mentioned earlier in this chapter, many agencies and facilities have websites that contain information about their health care goals and practices. For example, a facility’s website might emphasize respect, compassion, and innovation as important goals. In your letter, you could indicate experiences in which you have demonstrated these qualities.

#### Front Matter

1. •
   Contact information.
    Put your mailing address at the top left-hand corner of the page (Note: Many people choose to only include their email and phone number, instead of their home address).
2. •
   Today’s date.
    Include today’s date on the left-hand side of the letter beneath your return address.
3. •
   Name, title, and address of prospective employer.
    Include the contact person’s name, title, and address aligned with the left-hand margin. Ensure that this information is correct. You may have to call the facility or agency to confirm it. If you are unable to find out the person’s name, you may have to exclude this information.
4. •
   Greeting.
    Greet the person you are writing to in a professional manner. If you do not know the name, write “Dear (company).”

#### Body

1. •
   Paragraph one.
    Identify the job, indicate how you learned about it, and explain your purpose for writing. In unsolicited letters, use this opening paragraph to ask about potential opportunities.
2. •
   Paragraph two.
    Explain how your qualifications, experience, and skills relate to the position you are seeking.
3. •
   Paragraph three.
    Describe personal qualities or additional skills that make you a suitable candidate.

#### Closing

1. •
   Paragraph four.
    Use the concluding paragraph to ask for an interview. If the letter is unsolicited, say that you will contact the reader at a future date. Refer the reader to the enclosed resumé.
2. •
   Signature block.
    End the letter in a professional manner by typing “Sincerely” or “Yours truly.” Leave some space, and either type your name or insert your electronic signature at the end of the letter. Sign in ink above the typed name. Add your phone number and email address after your name. Type “Enclosure” at the bottom of the page to indicate that your resumé is enclosed.

### Writing Your Letter of Application

You will probably have to write several drafts to create a convincing letter. First, concentrate on putting down your main points on an outline. As you revise the content of the letter, ask yourself the following questions:

1. •
   Have I emphasized relevant skills and qualities?
    Mention experiences that demonstrate the skills, qualities, and attitudes necessary to support work. For example, if you have worked as a sales clerk, emphasize your positive interactions with clients.
2. •
   Have I emphasized accomplishments and skills?
    Rather than simply listing the tasks performed, explain what your experience has taught you. You might say “Working as a sales clerk taught me how to interact with the public in a warm, courteous, and respectful manner.”
3. •
   Have I used an appropriate tone?
    Your letter should be written in a respectful, capable, and professional tone. Be persuasive without being boastful. Minimize use of the word “I” to sound more modest. If you lack experience, avoid an apologetic tone.
4. •
   Is my letter concise?
    Your letter should not be more than one page long. If it is longer, edit out some of the details.
5. •
   Does my letter sound and look professional?
    Proofread your letter carefully and have at least two reliable people also read it. Sometimes, it helps to read your letter out loud, trying to listen for grammar and sentence structure errors. Create it on a computer. Use a standard business letter format, and print out the letter on high-quality paper (if submitting a paper copy). Double-check that your contact information is correct. You want to be completely sure that a prospective employer can reach you.

### Delivering a Hard Copy of Your Letter of Application and Resumé

Some employers request that the applicant deliver the resumé by ground mail (e.g., Canada Post) or in person. Hand-deliver your letter of application and resumé whenever possible to make sure it arrives on time. Another method of delivery is courier. Make sure that it is directed to the correct department or room, especially if you are applying to a large employer such as a hospital. Follow up with a telephone call to find out if the letter reached the appropriate person. If you respond to an advertisement, send out your letter and resumé as soon as possible. Sometimes the earliest applicants get more attention simply because their applications arrived first.

### Tips for Conveying Professionalism Electronically

Since many employers post job opportunities on the Internet, and many applicants apply electronically, here are a few tips to ensure that you get the best attention:

1. • Your email background should be electronically neutral. Do not have a patterned or designed background.
2. • Ensure that your email address is a professional one.
   Never create a childish or offensive email address.
    If your current email address is not a professional one, consider creating a new email address to use for potential employers.
3. • Never use abbreviations that are used on social media sites, known as “cyber-speak” (short forms for words or phrases, such as “LOL” or “CU later”).
   In any communication for professional purposes, whether on paper or online, you should use full sentences, appropriate punctuation, and correct and appropriate spelling and grammar.
4. • Ensure that your resumé is in the correct file format (e.g., Microsoft Word or Adobe Acrobat). If you are unsure, contact the agency to find out or send the resumé in both formats.
5. • Do not have attachments or postscripts that open automatically (that indicate that you listen to a particular radio station, for example).

## Completing a Job Application Form

For some positions, you are expected to complete a preset job application form when employers need to find out specific information about their applicants. Institutions may use forms as a way to test applicants’ reading, comprehension, and writing skills. It is a good idea to attach your resumé to the job application form, even when one is not required.

Most application forms ask for information about the following:

1. • Your legal eligibility to work in Canada
2. • Your education
3. • Your work-related skills
4. • Your employment history
5. • Names and phone numbers of employers
6. • Reasons for leaving previous jobs
7. • The names and phone numbers of three references

Remember that the completed application form says something about you. It should be professional looking and have no spelling or grammar mistakes or unfilled columns. Do a thorough job of filling out the form. If possible, take two copies of the form home with you. Use one as a rough copy and the other as your final copy. Ask someone reliable to read and check the final copy. If you make an error on the final copy, use correction fluid or tape to make the correction.

Application forms often require detailed information, such as the reason for leaving a job. You must answer every question truthfully. If you have been fired, or if you have an inconsistent work history, consider attaching a note of explanation.

Some employers require you to submit an online application form. Be just as careful with an online application as you would be with a hard copy. Ask someone reliable to read it over before you send it.

### Using Social Networking Sites

The use of Internet social networking sites to apply for jobs or to post work availability is now very common. However, there are some important points to remember when posting information electronically:

1. • Manage your identity. You wish employers to be able to contact you, but some identifying information such as a personal address may be misused by some people.
2. • Control your privacy settings. You can restrict the information that is distributed.
3. • Pay attention to the professionalism of your social media page or personal website since employers will read it. Pictures of you and your friends drinking at a party, for example, will convince some employers not to contact you.
4. • Avoid errors in spelling and grammar and the use of slang terms, which may be interpreted as unprofessional by some potential employers![1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur134)

## The Interview

You will not be hired as a support worker unless someone first interviews you and decides you are the right person for the job. During the interview, a prospective employer or client is trying to determine the following:

1. • Do you have the educational qualifications for the job?
2. • Do you have the skills necessary to do the job, or can you learn those skills?
3. • Will clients and co-workers respond well to you?
4. • Will you be a reliable, responsible, and motivated employee?
5. • Do you clearly understand confidentiality as well as professional boundaries in the workplace?

The interview is a key factor in any job search. From the interview, prospective employers decide if you have essential personal qualities such as compassion, respect, and enthusiasm. They can also judge if you are a good listener, if you speak clearly, and if you are at ease with others.

Interviewers may ask questions to find out the following:

1. • Can you handle the stress of working with seriously ill people?
2. • What do you bring to our organization that is unique and will enhance the care that you give?
3. • Can you deal with interpersonal conflict?
4. • Would you respond calmly and responsibly to an emergency?
5. • Are you capable of being firm and assertive with clients?
6. • Are you flexible enough to adapt to a variety of situations?
7. • Can you handle multiple demands and conflicts?
8. • Can you work under minimal supervision?
9. • Do you have initiative?
10. • Are you an effective team player?
11. • Can you handle routine problems on your own?
12. • Are you discreet?
13. • Do you respect individual differences?
14. • Are you a quick learner?
15. • Are you effective at managing your time?

Many interviewers ask situational questions, which are questions designed to find out what you would do in certain situations. You may be asked a question about an imaginary situation, or you may be asked to describe what you did in a particular situation you encountered in the past.

Always be well prepared for an interview. When you are nervous, it can be difficult to think and speak clearly. If you have thought about possible questions and answers, you are more likely to be at ease. To prepare for an interview, think about the following:

1. •
   The position.
    Make sure you understand the nature of the position. Learn about the employer’s services, clients, and philosophy. Read information about the employer that is available to the general public—for example, on its website and in brochures. Talk to friends, instructors, and acquaintances who know the agency or facility. Review the skills and qualities that are essential to support work.
2. •
   Your qualities, skills, and experience.
    Develop a clear understanding of yourself: your values, skills, qualities, interests, goals, strengths, and weaknesses. Write these down, along with specific experiences that illustrate each.
3. •
   Your portfolio.
    Review your personal portfolio and decide what you should highlight at each individual interview. What will impress each employer most? Bring your portfolio with you to your
   interview in case the interviewer wants photocopies of any of the pages.
4. •
   Your reference list.
    Most employers will require you to provide at least three references. Print off a list of references and a hard copy of your resumé, and attach them together. Your reference list should always be in the same font as the rest of your resumé.
5. •
   The interviewer.
    Put yourself in the interviewer’s place. Review your letter of application and resumé. Why do you think you were selected for an interview? What concerns might the interviewer have about you? For example, if you are young and inexperienced, the interviewer may be concerned about your maturity level. If you have never remained in a job for more than a year, the interviewer may be concerned about your commitment. Be prepared to address these issues.

### The Group Interview

#### More Than One Applicant

Occasionally, an agency may want to fill several positions, especially if it is short-staffed. In order to save time and trouble for the people who are doing the interviewing, the agency may group two or more applicants together in a room and ask each applicant specific questions. After one applicant has answered the question, the other applicants are invited to respond. Following this group interview, the applicants may then be interviewed individually.

The benefit for you of this interview type is that you might feel less nervous to know that “you are not alone.” However, it can be intimidating to think that your answers are being compared with the answers of the other applicants in the room. Remember that, in this respect, it is no different from an individual interview because responses of various applicants will always be compared.

It is important to watch your body language and your responses during this type of interview. Try to watch each of the applicants as that person answers questions. You want to appear friendly and polite. Do not ever cut someone off while that person is speaking. This type of interviewing is a good technique for interviewers to identify a “team player” who has effective communication skills (see [Chapter 9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)).

#### More Than One Interviewer

For some interviews, there is only one applicant but two or more interviewers, who will take turns asking the applicant questions. The interviewers usually read from a written list of prepared questions. Occasionally, the interviewers may ask the applicant an unscripted question for clarification of something the applicant has said.

This type of interview is usually very thorough, and the applicant may leave the room feeling overwhelmed. You should be aware that the interviewers are trying to determine if you can handle questions well and if you can answer them in an honest and sincere manner. They may ask you questions that test your ability to solve problems. These questions typically begin with “What would you do if . . .” The interviewers want to know how well you can apply what you have learned in your support worker education to the scenarios that they present to you. These responses can be easily practised with a friend or a classmate.

It is important to make eye contact with the interviewer who is speaking as a question is being asked (see [Chapter 9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)). Remember, too, to use words and body language that show that you are friendly and approachable.

#### Practising for Your Job Interview

Practice will give you confidence. When practising, focus on making eye contact with the interviewer, as well as on your listening skills, relaxation techniques, and responses to questions you think might be asked. Sit down only when invited to sit. Sit up comfortably straight without slouching. Try to remember your interviewer’s name.

#### Developing Listening Skills

The ability to listen is an important skill for interviews. If you fail to listen, you will not make a good impression. Since listening is essential in support work, an employer is unlikely to hire a poor listener as a support worker. Be a good listener during the interview by avoiding the following:

1. • Failing to concentrate on what the interviewer is saying
2. • Failing to ask for clarification if the question is unclear
4. • Talking too much
5. • Responding to questions too quickly without thinking first about the answer

#### Practising Relaxation Techniques

You will probably listen more attentively if you feel relaxed in the interview. Deep-breathing exercises and other relaxation techniques can help you relax (see [Chapter 8](../B9780323709392000080/B9780323709392000080.xhtml)).

#### Practising Your Responses

Most interviewers ask similar questions. See [BOX 13.3](#b0020) for a list of commonly asked questions. Practise your responses to these questions in front of a mirror or with a friend.

### Planning

Plan ahead so that things go smoothly on the interview day.

1. • Decide what you are going to wear and make sure that the clothing is clean and pressed. Get a haircut if you need one. If beards or mustaches are worn, they must be neatly trimmed and be professional in appearance. Beards worn for religious reasons are permitted.
2. • If the route to the venue of the interview is unfamiliar, do a “practice run” so that you will not get lost on the interview day. Leave yourself at least 30 minutes to spare in case of traffic delays on the way to the interview.
3. • In addition to your portfolio, bring notepaper and a pen that works.
4. • Do some research about the facility or agency by checking available websites. Doing so shows initiative and interest in working there.
5. • Plan a few informed questions to ask during your interview (see page 211).

### Making a Good Impression

In an interview, first impressions are crucial. Poor impressions can be difficult to change, no matter how well you do later in the interview. From the moment you meet a prospective employer, you need to present a calm and professional image. This advice applies even when you stop by to pick up an application form. Pay attention to your grooming, your clothing, and your conduct, whether interviewing physically in person or via a teleconferencing platform such as Zoom.

#### Grooming

1. • Make sure your hair is clean, neat, and away from your face.
2. • Brush and floss your teeth. If you are a heavy coffee drinker or a smoker, bring breath mints with you (but do not have them in your mouth during the interview).
3. • Be careful not to wear too much makeup.
4. • Wear minimal jewellery. Avoid large bracelets, rings, and earrings.
5. • Remove body ornaments that are not religious in origin, such as nose rings and eyebrow studs. Tattoos that may be offensive in nature should be covered.
6. • Do not wear any perfume, cologne, or heavily scented lotions.
7. • Make sure your nails are trimmed. If you wear nail polish, use a neutral colour.
8. • Keep any facial hair trimmed and well groomed.
9. • If possible, wash and thoroughly dry your hands before you shake hands with the interviewer, especially if your palms perspire when you are nervous.

#### Clothing

1. • Men should wear clothing that is professional in appearance and businesslike, such as a shirt and dress pants, socks, and shoes. Women should wear a dress, blouse or sweater with either a skirt or tailored pants. Do not wear jeans, T-shirts, or shorts. Your outfit should fit properly, be in good repair, and be wrinkle-free.
2. • Wear clothing that fully covers undergarments such as panties, bras, or boxer shorts. Ensure these cannot be seen through the clothing.
3. • Avoid clothing that is tight fitting or revealing.
4. • Try clothing on at home and practise sitting and walking in the clothing in front of a mirror, if possible. Doing so is a good way to see if your clothing looks professional and feels comfortable when sitting. If you have access to one, inspect yourself in front of a full-length mirror.
5. • If wearing pantyhose or tights, make sure that there are no holes or runs. Carry an extra pair in case you need to change them.
6. • Shoes should be in good repair and well-polished. Avoid casual shoes, such as running shoes or sandals, and shoes with very high heels.

1. • Arrive in the reception area 5 to 10 minutes before the interview. If being interviewed online, be ready at least 5 minutes before the interview.
2. • Do not bring anyone with you. If someone has driven you to the place, ask the person to wait outside. If interviewing online, ensure that you are in a room where you will not be interrupted.
3. • Do not carry your cellphone in your hand. Place it in your purse, pocket, or on a holster. Make sure it is shut off for the interview.
4. • Do not chew gum, smoke, or eat as you wait for the interviewer. Ensure that your clothing does not smell of tobacco.
5. • Do not ask to use the phone, talk on a cellphone, or text-message anyone while waiting.
6. • Do not talk with the receptionist unless that person starts the conversation.
7. • If you feel nervous, take deep breaths to calm yourself.

### Interview Tips

Being adequately prepared should help you feel relaxed during the interview. If you feel calm, you are more able to focus on details that may help you get the job. Some of these are discussed below.

1. •
   Smile, make eye contact, and nod when introducing yourself to your interviewer.
    Maintain physical distance while there are enforced pandemic precautions (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)).
2. •
   Do not use the interviewer’s first name.
    The exception is if you are asked by the interviewer to do so. However, do address the interviewer by name.
3. •
   Project a confident image.
    Whether sitting or standing, practise good posture, breathe deeply to relax, and avoid nervous habits such as picking at your fingernails or fiddling with your hair. Be aware of gestures and body language that might be perceived negatively, such as crossed arms. Look the interviewer in the eye, and keep your hands folded in your lap.
4. •
   Make appropriate eye contact when speaking
    (see [Chapter 9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)).
5. •
   Listen carefully.
    If you listen carefully, you should be able to answer questions concisely and directly. Do not be afraid to ask the interviewer to repeat a question.
7. •
   Take your time.
    Some people talk quickly when they are nervous. Slow down, take a deep breath, and think about the question before you answer. An answer given too quickly may sound rehearsed.
8. •
   Answer questions honestly.
    You must be honest in your answers. If you do not understand a question or are unsure of an answer, say so. Do not try to hide aspects of your past that you think may disqualify you from the job. Instead, explain how you have learned from past mistakes. For example, if you were fired from your last position, explain the reason and give examples of how you have corrected the behaviour.
9. •
   Speak positively about your previous job.
    Do not demean, ridicule, appear upset with, or speak critically about your previous employer, regardless of the circumstances, as doing so may suggest a negative attitude or an aversion to authority.
10. •
    Use experiences to support opinions.
     When you are asked for your opinion, support your answer with examples from your experience. For instance, after saying, “I think it’s important for support workers to know their scope of practice,” describe a situation that illustrates this point. If you are inexperienced, use an example from your course of study.
11. •
    Ask the right questions.
     Most interviewers conclude by asking if you have any questions. The questions you prepared in advance may have been answered during the interview. Rather than trying to think up new questions, it is acceptable to say something like, “No, I don’t have any questions. You have addressed all the questions that I had during our discussion.” If you do have a question, make sure that it is appropriate. Do not ask questions about the salary, benefits, hours, and vacation times. It is suitable, though, to ask about these when you are offered the position.
12. •
    Concluding the interview.
     At the end of the interview, thank the interviewer for their time. Show confidence and enthusiasm by saying something like, “I am really interested in working for your facility, and I am sure I could be a productive member of your staff.” Whatever you say, it is important to be genuine.

### Follow-up

Send a brief thank-you note after an interview. Write the note as soon as possible, no later than the day following the interview. The thank-you note will indicate that you are enthusiastic and courteous, and it might set you apart from other candidates.

The note can be handwritten or prepared on a computer. If you write the note by hand, use a plain white note card. Write at least one draft before you write the final copy. Ask someone else to read the note before you send it. Mail the note rather than sending it by email. This approach shows the employer you are prepared to make an effort.

Express your thanks for the interview and reinforce your interest in the position. Sign your name on the card (see [FIGURE 13.5](#f0030) for a sample note).

## The Employment Offer

You may be offered the position at the end of the interview or within a few days following the interview. Or you may wait weeks before hearing anything. Instead of sitting by the phone and waiting, continue to explore other opportunities. Even if you are not offered a job, the interview experience will have been worthwhile if you learn something from it. Review the interview process by asking yourself the questions in [BOX 13.4](#b0025).

If you are not offered the job, contact the employer to find out why. Ask if you can talk to the person who interviewed you, either in person or on the phone. Most employers are willing to talk to people who are genuinely interested in improving their skills. Approach this conversation as a learning opportunity; do not sound angry, bitter, or accusatory.

terms of your employment. Find out if the offer is conditional and if the job begins with a trial period.

#### Terms of Employment

At the time of the job offer, make sure that you clarify your wages, your hours of work, requirements, and expectations. If, for example, a car is required for the position, find out the employer’s policy on car mileage and repairs. Many employers publish procedure manuals that should address your questions.

If the job is a contract position, find out the length of the contract, the terms of the contract, and what is and is not included in the contract prior to accepting the offer.

If you are hired directly by a client or a client’s family, you must specify the terms of your employment.
The client may be required to contribute to Employment Insurance and Canada Pension Plan. The client may also be required to submit your taxes directly to the government. Before you accept employment directly with a client, read Revenue Canada’s booklet Employee or Self-Employed (Catalogue Number RC4110), which is available online or at your local Canada Revenue Agency office.

#### Conditional Offer

In the health care field, an offer of employment is often conditional, meaning that you must meet certain conditions or requirements before you are hired. These conditions usually include a health report signed by your family physician that confirms that you are in good health, and a police record check that shows that you do not have a criminal record. You are responsible for providing all required documents. Note that there may be a fee to obtain these documents. Some employers may also require that you have certain equipment necessary to perform the job. For example, in community settings, a cellphone may be essential.

#### Probation

Most employers hire new staff for a probationary (trial) period that lasts 3 to 6 months. During this time, both you and the employer can decide if you are the right fit for the job. Within this period, the employer can end your employment at any time or you can leave without the usual 2 weeks’ notice, although giving notice will possibly get you a reference.

#### Benefits

Find out what you can about an employer’s benefits package. Many employers have booklets that explain their benefits plan. Benefits do not usually begin until the end of the probationary period. If you work part-time, you may not be eligible for benefits. Be especially careful to find out about benefits if you are working directly for a client in the client’s home.

#### How to Refuse or Leave a Job Without Leaving a Negative Impression

Occasionally, you might decide that you cannot work at an agency or facility (or you decide to leave the agency or facility). Quitting a job is a difficult thing to do, as it is important that the employer has a good impression of you. Many employers spend a lot of time and effort, both of which cost money, to train their employees and are understandably not happy when they leave, especially after a short period. Remember, you might want to return to that employer in the future! Ask to meet with your supervisor in person, and show up for this meeting dressed professionally. Remember, this meeting is the last chance you have to make a good impression!

It is important to give an employer adequate notice when you intend to leave. Two weeks’ notice is considered to be the minimal amount of notice to give; most employers would prefer more time than that, if at all possible. Employers are more likely to provide a good reference when sufficient notice is given and the employee continues to be reliable through the duration of employment.

Refusing a job or leaving a job because you are going back to school, have been hired at another agency on a full-time basis (assuming your current position is part-time), are moving to another city, or are pregnant and have decided not to work for some time is easy to convey to an employer. It is important—whatever the reason—that you be honest, tactful, and pleasant to the employer.

If, however, you are leaving because you do not like the atmosphere of the workplace or you are unhappy with the agency or facility in any way, you need to be able to state your feelings tactfully as well. Prepare what you are going to say ahead of time. If necessary, write down your thoughts on small index cards so that you can refer to them. Always begin by stating what you liked about the workplace. Be as fair as possible, remembering that you wanted to work there once!

When stating what you do not like about the agency or facility, be as specific as possible. Instead of stating, “Well, I don’t like the staff,” you can be more specific (and therefore more helpful) if you can say, “I do not agree with the afternoon staff who put residents in bed by 6:30 p.m. I think the residents should have a say as to when they are put in bed, and their families should be involved in the decision process.” Remember, you must always be honest. Do not lie or exaggerate a story to win favour with the supervisor.

Your statements inform the supervisor of the issues that exist in the agency or facility. If you are a valued employee, the supervisor might ask if you would reconsider your decision to leave. This is a choice only you can make. Regardless of whether your decision is to stay or leave, you should always be professional and pleasant when conveying it.

## Body Mechanics

The term body mechanics refers to the movement of the body. Proper body mechanics include moving in an efficient and careful way and having good posture and balance, as well as using the strongest and largest muscles for work. (Review the structure and function of the musculoskeletal system in [Chapter 32](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml).) Good body mechanics reduce the risk for injury. Fatigue, muscle strain, and injury can result from the improper use and positioning of the body during activity or rest, so you must be aware of both your client’s and your own body mechanics.

Body alignment (or posture) is the way the body parts (head, trunk, arms, and legs) are positioned in relation to one another. Good body alignment enables the body to move and function with strength and efficiency, reduces strain on the muscles and joints, and prevents injury. For people with limited mobility, correct body alignment helps prevent disabling complications such as muscle atrophy and contractures (see [Chapter 15](../B9780323709392000158/B9780323709392000158.xhtml)).

A client who is placed in poor body alignment may develop serious effects on all body systems. For example, a client who is poorly positioned in a stooped-over position will probably not be able to breathe deeply and bring oxygen into the lungs. Or if positioned flat on the back with no pillows under the knees, a client may experience severe pain in the lumbar spine region. As a support worker, you must constantly watch to see that clients are positioned (or repositioned) properly.

[FIGURE 14.2](#f0015) shows a person standing with good body alignment. Notice that both sides of the body are in line with each other—in the centre is the line of gravity. Body balance is achieved when a relatively low centre of gravity (the point in the body at which weight is evenly distributed on either side) is balanced over a wide, stable base. Imagine a vertical line falling from the centre of gravity and through the centre of the base of support.

Good posture in a standing position has the following traits:

1. • The head and neck are erect and straight.
2. • Shoulders and hips are parallel to each other.
3. • Shoulders are back.
4. • The chest is out.
5. • The spine maintains a natural lumbar curve.
6. ![A) shows gap between feet, labeled as base of support, with center of gravity marked on lower abdomen. B) Lateral view of a man standing on one leg, with leg on floor labeled as base of support and center of gravity is marked on thigh of raised leg.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000146/main.assets/f14-02-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 14.2 Proper body alignment when standing. Note how feet are placed apart for a wide base of support. Note where the line of gravity (L) and centre of gravity (C) are. In image B, the centre and line of gravity have moved to accommodate the person’s change of position.
7. • The abdomen is tucked in and the pelvis tilted in (also known as a pelvic tilt). This positioning helps improve the posture, strengthen the lower spine, and reduce the risk for back injury.
8. • Knees are slightly flexed.
9. • Arms are hanging comfortably at the sides.
10. • Feet are about shoulder-width apart.
11. • Toes are pointing forward; one foot is slightly forward.

Lying down and sitting also require good alignment.

The position of the feet is especially important for good body mechanics because feet provide the base of support. A base of support is the area on which an object rests. A wide base of support provides more stability and balance than a narrow base of support. You should stand with your feet and legs apart for a wide base of support, especially when moving, lifting, or transferring weight. A lunge (or stride) stance has one foot in front of the other, with the two about shoulder-width apart. This stance will enable you to keep your balance while performing the various tasks described in this chapter.

The strongest and largest muscles are in the shoulders, upper arms, hips, and thighs. These muscles should be used for lifting and moving. Using smaller and weaker muscles, such as those in the lower back, will strain your back and lead to back fatigue and injury. Follow the guidelines in the Think About Safety: Guidelines for Good Body Mechanics box to move clients and objects safely and efficiently.

Guidelines for Good Body Mechanics

1. • Assess the situation before you begin any attempt to move the client. Get help if you think the client’s weight is more than you can safely turn or transfer. Many employers have a no-lift policy, in which case workers are not allowed to lift or move clients without mechanical aid and qualified assistance. Always check the care plan and know the employer policy before moving a client.
2. • Face your work area to prevent unnecessary twisting. Remember to stand with a wide base of support.
3. • Bend at your knees and hips and squat when lifting or setting down objects below your waist. Do not bend forward from your waist, or you will strain your small back muscles ([FIGURE 14.3](#f0020)).

### Turning Pads and Sliders

A turning pad (also known as sliding pad, turning sheet, transfer sheet, lift pad, and, inaccurately, drawsheet) is a small pad or folded sheet placed on top of the bottom sheet and extends from the client’s head to above the knees; it helps keep the mattress and bottom linens clean and dry ([FIGURE 14.9](#f0050)). The turning pad can be made from a waterproof material or even a folded sheet (see [Chapter 21](../B9780323709392000213/B9780323709392000213.xhtml)). Some agencies use reusable waterproof incontinence pads as turning pads. However, at other agencies, it is unacceptable
to use an incontinence pad to move or reposition a client.
It is important to always use the equipment that your employer recommends.

### Rolling Versus Log-Rolling a Client

Clients who have suffered a severe injury to their spinal cord or hip or who are recovering from neck,
back, or spinal surgery often need to keep their spinal column in straight alignment to prevent further injuries. The client will need to be rolled as one unit, with care taken to prevent any twisting of the spinal column. This special type of turn is called log-rolling.

Clients who are not severely injured or recovering from neck, back, or spinal surgery—if stated in the client’s care plan—may be turned (or “rolled”) in bed (see Turning the Client, on page 224). For these clients, it can be easiest to bend the client’s knee to assist you with the turn. Bend the knee opposite to the direction of the turn. For example, to turn the client onto the right side, bend the client’s left knee and roll the client to the right, avoiding any twisting of the spine. For clients who cannot bend a knee without discomfort or injury, cross the legs at the ankles in the direction of the roll instead of bending the client’s knee.

### Turning the Client

Turning clients onto their side helps prevent complications from bed rest. Certain care procedures require the side-lying position so that clients are turned toward you or away from you. The direction depends on the client’s condition and on the situation. If you are going to turn clients over in bed, it is usually safer and easier to try to roll them. Rolling is the turning of a person over from the back or side to the other side. One of the client’s knees (the one opposite the side the client is being turned onto) may be bent, if necessary (and if stated in the client’s care plan),
but you must always make sure the client is in good body alignment. You must always turn the client’s neck and spine in a single step, making sure they are never twisted.

Many clients have conditions such as arthritis in the spine or knees that make turning painful for them. When turning these clients, it is best to roll them using a turning pad. Remember to check with your supervisor and the care plan to determine the correct procedure to use for each client.

Some clients will need a minimum of two people to roll them, and if a client is tall or heavy, even three people may be needed. Clients who will need three people to roll them include the following:

1. • Clients with arthritic spines or knees (common in older persons)
2. • Clients recovering from hip fractures
3. • Clients with spinal cord injuries (the spine must be kept straight at all times following spinal cord injury)
4. • Clients recovering from spinal surgery (the spine must be kept straight at all times following spinal cord surgery)

### Moving the Client From a Bed to a Stretcher

Stretchers are sometimes used to transport clients to other areas in a facility. In particular, they are used for the following:

1. • Clients who cannot sit up
2. • Clients who must stay in a lying position
3. • Clients who are seriously ill
4. • Clients who are waiting for or returning from surgery

The stretcher is covered with a folded flat sheet. A pillow and extra blankets are kept at hand. With your supervisor’s permission, raise the head of the stretcher to the semi-Fowler’s position (see Positioning the Client, on page 231). This position is more comfortable for most clients.

A slider board is used to assist in moving the client. Placed between the bed and the sheet the client is lying on, the slider board allows the staff to move the client with less friction. Its handles on either side allow for easy grasping. At least three staff members are needed to safely move a client from a bed to a stretcher. Remember, keep the client in good body alignment, and use good body mechanics.

After the client has been moved to the stretcher, fasten the safety straps across the client’s chest. The stretcher’s side rails are kept up during transport of the client. Clients should be moved feet first in the stretcher so that they can observe where they are going. As you push the stretcher, you should observe the client’s breathing and colour during the transport. Never leave a client unattended on a stretcher.

### Helping the Client to Sit on the Side of the Bed (Dangle the Legs)

Clients need to sit on the side of the bed (dangle the legs) for many reasons. Many older people become dizzy or faint if they get out of bed too fast. This is called postural hypotension. They need to sit on the side of the bed for 1 to 5 minutes before walking or transferring, to reduce this risk of postural hypotension. Some clients need to increase activity in stages. They progress from resting in bed to sitting on the side of the bed and then to sitting in a chair. Walking is the next step.

## Positioning the Client

Regular position changes and good body alignment promote comfort and well-being, make breathing easier, and promote circulation. Proper positioning also helps prevent many complications such as pressure injuries (see [Chapter 22](../B9780323709392000225/B9780323709392000225.xhtml)) and contractures (see [Chapter 15](../B9780323709392000158/B9780323709392000158.xhtml)). The client must be properly positioned at all times.

Most clients can move and reposition themselves. Some need reminding to adjust their position, while others need help or depend entirely on the health care team for position changes.

### Comfort and Safety Measures

Whether in a bed or a chair, clients are repositioned at least every 2 hours, and some more often. Follow your supervisor’s instructions and the care plan.

The physician may order certain positions or position limits. Every time you position a client, check the skin for signs of redness, paleness, or discoloration. Report any of these observations immediately.

To safely position a client, follow these guidelines:

1. • Check with your supervisor and the care plan for the best positions for each client.
2. • Follow the scheduled times for repositioning the client.
3. • Use good body mechanics.
4. • If necessary, ask for help before beginning.
5. • Explain the procedure to the client.
6. • Be gentle when moving the client.
7. • Provide for privacy.
8. • Leave the client in good body alignment; use pillows as directed in the care plan for comfort and support.
9. • Make sure linens are wrinkle-free; change or straighten them, as needed.
10. • Place the call bell (in facilities) within the client’s reach.

### Fowler’s Positions

Fowler’s position is a semi-sitting to upright sitting position in bed. It is used to relax tense abdominal muscles, allowing for improved breathing in clients. It is also used for birth parents in the postpartum period after delivery to improve uterine drainage. Most clients find that it is more comfortable to have their legs slightly bent and their arms supported on pillows while in this position. Supporting the client’s arms on pillows may also prevent the client from slipping to one side of the bed.

To place a client in the low Fowler’s position, raise the head of the bed to an angle of 15 to 30 degrees. The client’s head and shoulders can alternatively be raised with extra pillows.

The supine position is the back-lying position ([FIGURE 14.17](#f0120)) that is used for sleeping and resting.

To place a client in the supine position, do the following:

* 5. Place a small pillow under the client’s knees to keep them flexed. This can reduce back strain.

### Sims’ Position

Sims’ position is the left-side-lying position in which the right leg is sharply flexed so that it is not resting on the left leg. The left arm is positioned along the client’s back. This position, in which the client is lying partly on the abdomen ([FIGURE 14.19](#f0130)), is used for administering enemas and other procedures since it optimizes the position of the client’s intestines for enema absorption. It can also be used for resting if the client is comfortable in this position. It is, however, usually not comfortable for older persons, so if you must put them in this position, position them well with plenty of pillows. Check with your supervisor before positioning an older person in Sims’ position. Check the client frequently for comfort and circulation in the lower arm and hand.

To place a client in Sims’ position, do the following:

* 1. Make sure the bed is flat. Lower the head of the bed if necessary.
* 2. Position the client onto the left side (see the procedure Log-Rolling the Client, on page 225).
* 3. Bend the upper (right) leg and position it such that it does not lie on the lower (left) leg.
* 4. Bend the upper (right) arm, and position the hand, palm down, near the head.
* 5. Place the lower (left) arm behind the client, palm facing up. If the client cannot tolerate this position, move this arm to a more comfortable position.
* 6. Place a small pillow under the head and neck. Ensure that the pillow is not covering the client’s face and that the client can breathe freely.
* 7. Place small pillows under the upper arm and hand and under the upper leg.

### Prone Position

The prone position is the front-lying position on the abdomen, with the head turned to one side ([FIGURE 14.20](#f0135)). Most clients, especially clients with limited range of motion in their necks, cannot tolerate the prone position. It is usually used, if at all, for only a short period at a time. The prone position is used mainly to prevent shortening (contractures) of the thigh muscle in clients who have had leg
amputations and sit in a wheelchair for long periods. The prone position will help keep the thigh muscles in the amputated leg stretched. Use the prone position only if it is called for in the care plan.

Clients in chairs or wheelchairs must hold the upper body and head erect to avoid poor alignment ([FIGURE 14.21](#f0140)). Assist with providing postural supports, as needed. * 2. Make sure the back is straight. The client should not be leaning to the side.

  The Difference Between a Lift and a Transfer

  It is important that you know the difference between a transfer and a lift. A transfer is the method used to move a client who is able to weight-bear to any degree. A lift is a device used to move—or the action of moving—a client from one place to another without the client’s weight-bearing or assistance. ## Transferring the Client

The term transfer means helping a client move from one place to another. See the Think About Safety: The Difference Between a Lift and a Transfer box for the difference between a lift and a transfer. For a transfer, the client must be able to weight-bear (have the ability to shift body weight) to any degree. Clients are often moved from their beds to chairs, wheelchairs, commodes, toilets, or stretchers. Some clients need only minimal help with transferring, but others need two (or occasionally three) people to help them. Your supervisor and the care plan will tell you how much help a client needs. Place the chair or wheelchair with its back aligned with the head of the bed. Lock the bed and wheelchair wheels, and raise the footrests.

### Know the Client

Never attempt a transfer without assistance if the client cannot help. Always make sure that you have enough room for a safe transfer. Clear the area of potential hazards. You may have to move furniture to make room for a chair, wheelchair, or stretcher. Before transferring a client, check the care plan or ask your supervisor if the client has a side affected by disease or disability. The unaffected side is the side not affected by disease or disability, and it pulls the affected side along.

Transfers from the affected side are awkward and unsafe and may also frighten the client. The client may have limited vision on the affected side, and the affected arm and leg may not be able to bear any of the client’s weight. For example, if a client’s left side has less strength as a result of a stroke or injury, the right side is the unaffected side. In this case, plan to move the client so that the right side moves first.

### Follow Principles of Safety and Good Body Mechanics

The number of people needed for a transfer depends on the client’s physical capabilities, condition, and
size. You should always check the care plan to see what equipment and how many people are required to transfer that client. Many long-term care facilities require the use of transfer belts when residents are transferred to or from chairs or wheelchairs.

### Applying Transfer Belts

A transfer belt (see [FIGURE 14.24](#f0155)) is also used in some agencies to transfer unsteady clients and those with disabilities ([FIGURE 14.26, A](#f0165)). It helps prevent falls and other injuries. In many agencies, the belt goes around the client’s waist, although some agencies require you to place the transfer belt around the client’s hips. You should be aware of your agency’s policies for placement before using a transfer belt. Grasp the belt, with your folded fingers pointing up, to support the client during the transfer.

A wider belt with vertical handles ([FIGURE 14.26, B](#f0165)), called a walking or gait belt, is used for walking with a client. Many facilities require staff to use these belts when transferring or walking a client. Always check the client’s care plan before using a transfer belt.

The sit-to-stand mechanical lift is commonly used in long-term care facilities and in private homes. ## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Using good body mechanics will help you protect yourself and your client from injury during moving, positioning, transferring, and lifting activities.

• Remember to use good hand hygiene practices before and after any moving, positioning, transferring, or lifting activity.

• Body mechanics refers to movement of the body in an efficient and careful way, the use of good posture and balance, and the use of the longest and largest muscles of the body.

• Body alignment is the way the body parts are positioned in relation to one another. Good body alignment allows the body to move with strength and efficiency, reduces strain on muscles and joints, and prevents injury.

• When positioning clients, ensure that they are left in a safe, wrinkle-free, and comfortable position. Ensure that pillows, padding, or stabilizers are used correctly.

• Regular position changes and good body alignment promote comfort and well-being, make breathing easier, and promote circulation.

• Always check the client’s care plan, and know employer policy before moving a client.

• The client’s skin must be protected from friction and shearing when the client is being moved.

• Know what turning pads are and how to use them properly. They must be positioned from the head to the mid-thigh, keeping the client’s neck in a stable position.

• All movements with another worker or helper should be done in a synchronous manner (at the same time) to prevent injury.

• A transfer is the method used for moving a client who can weight-bear to any degree. If a client cannot assist by weight-bearing, a lift is used.

• Most agencies have a no-lift policy because of the serious risk for back injury to the workers.

• The DIPPS principles should always be observed whenever a client is moved, positioned, transferred, or lifted at any time.

Most people move about and function without help, but illnesses, surgery, injuries, pain, and aging can cause weakness and limit activity. Clients may be weakened by chronic illnesses, have to stay in bed for a long time, have permanent paralysis, or have progressive disorders (e.g., multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, arthritis, muscular disorders—see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)), causing decreases in activity. Inactivity, whether mild or severe, affects not only the normal functions of all body systems but also the client’s mental well-being.

Deconditioning is the loss of muscle size, strength, and function (muscle atrophy) that results from immobility and inactivity. Deconditioning can occur at any age—from infants to older persons; however, older clients become deconditioned more quickly. So each client must be encouraged to be as active as possible. If a client cannot move without assistance, it is important that you assist the client to move in order to prevent permanent loss of joint function.

It is also important to remember that
support workers can sustain injuries while assisting clients with exercise and activity.
 You need to practise proper body mechanics at all times (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)). Choosing healthy lifestyle habits such as performing muscle stretches daily before assisting clients, as well as paying attention to your posture and body mechanics at all times, may decrease your own risk for injury.

Physiotherapists and occupational therapists are responsible for assessing the client’s joint function and for suggesting exercises or mobility aids that can assist the client in regaining muscle strength and function. The care plan tells you about the client’s activity level and the exercises they are to perform.

To help promote exercise and activity, you need to understand the following:

1. • Bed rest
2. • How to prevent complications from bed rest
3. • How to help clients exercise

## Bed Rest

Bed rest is ordered by the physician to treat a health problem and often also to do the following:

1. • Reduce physical activity
2. • Reduce pain
3. • Encourage rest
4. • Regain strength
5. • Promote healing

The activities allowed for each client will be specified in the care plan. Check with your supervisor if you have questions. The following types of bed rest are common:

1. •
   Strict bed rest
   —The client must remain in bed at all times and be assisted with all activities of daily living (ADLs) as needed. If the client is continent, they must use a bedpan or urinal.
2. •
   Bed rest with commode privileges
   —Some ADLs are allowed, such as self-feeding, oral hygiene, bathing, shaving, and hair care. The client can use the bedside commode for elimination needs (see [Chapters 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml) and [30](../B9780323709392000304/B9780323709392000304.xhtml)); the care plan will state whether the client must receive assistance.
3. •
   Bed rest with bathroom privileges (bed rest with BRP)
   —The client can use the bathroom for elimination needs.

Always ask your supervisor what bed rest means for each client.

### Complications of Bed Rest and Immobility

Long-term bed rest and immobility can cause serious health complications, affecting every system in the body. Immobility is an excessive lack of exercise and activity. Pressure ulcers, constipation, fecal impaction, urinary tract infections, renal calculi (kidney stones), blood clots (thrombi), and pneumonia (infection of the lung) are some of the complications that can occur (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)) with immobility.

Contractures and muscle atrophy occur in the musculoskeletal system. A contracture is the lack of joint mobility caused by the abnormal shortening of a muscle.
Contractures can form in as little as 48 to 72 hours if the client is unable to move the muscle and joint and it has not been exercised by a caregiver.
 The contracted muscle becomes fixed into position, deformed, and unable to stretch ([FIGURE 15.1](#f0010)). Contractures are not reversible. Common sites are the fingers, wrists, elbows, toes, ankles, knees, and hips. Contractures can also occur in the neck and spine. The person with a contracture suffers permanent deformity and disability. Atrophy, as defined in [Chapter 43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml), is the decrease in size or the wasting away of tissue. Muscle atrophy is a decrease in size or a wasting away of muscle ([FIGURE 15.2](#f0015)). These complications from lack of activity must be prevented to maintain normal body movement.

Orthostatic hypotension and blood clots (see [Chapters 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml) and [46](../B9780323709392000468/B9780323709392000468.xhtml)) can occur from a lack of mobility. Orthostatic hypotension (or postural hypotension) is a drop in (hypo) blood pressure when the client stands up (ortho and static). When the client moves from lying or sitting to a standing position, the blood pressure drops, and the client experiences dizziness, weakness, and spots before their eyes, followed by syncope (fainting), which is a brief loss of consciousness. [BOX 15.1](#b0010) lists measures that can be taken to prevent orthostatic hypotension, such as changing positions slowly.

#### Range-of-Motion Exercises

Moving a joint to the extent possible without causing pain is the range of motion (ROM) of that joint. ROM exercises are very important because they prevent problems such as joint contractures and muscle atrophy (see [Chapter 43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml)). ROM exercises involve exercising the joints through their complete ROM ([BOX 15.2](#b0015)). Clients who need these exercises usually do them at least twice a day. ROM exercises are active, passive, or active–assistive. Music can help some clients to relax while ROM exercises are performed. It is important to ask your clients if they would like to have music played, which could relax them or motivate them.

BOX 15.2 Joint Movements

* Abduction—moving a body part away from the midline of the body
* Adduction—moving a body part toward the midline of the body
* Extension—straightening a body part
* Flexion—bending a body part
* Hyperextension—straightening a body part excessively
* Dorsiflexion—bending the toes and foot up at the ankle
* Rotation—turning the joint
* Internal rotation—turning the joint inward
* External rotation—turning the joint outward
* Plantar flexion—bending the foot down at the ankle
* Pronation—turning downward
* Supination—turning upward

1. •
   Active
    ROM exercises are done by the client independently.
2. •
   Passive
    ROM exercises involve a staff member moving the joints of the client through the ROM. The client does not participate in joint movement.
3. •
   Active–assistive
    ROM exercises involve the client’s participation as much as possible, with a support worker assisting in the exercises the client is not able to perform.

ROM exercises occur naturally during ADLs such as bathing, hair care, eating, reaching, and walking—all of which involve joint movements. Clients on bed rest and clients who have mobility loss from illness or injury, however, get very little activity. Therefore, their care plans usually call for ROM exercises. The care plan tells you which joints to exercise and whether the exercises are to be active, passive, or active–assistive. If you have questions, ask your supervisor (see the [Focus on Children: Play](#b0030) and [Focus on Long-Term Care: Activity Programs](#b0035) boxes).

ROM exercises can cause injury, such as muscle strain, joint injury, and pain, if not done properly.

Activity Programs

Canada’s provinces have long-term care legislation that requires facilities to have an assessment and care-planning process to prevent unnecessary reduction in a client’s range of motion (ROM). Prevention involves active, active–assistive, or passive ROM exercises. Splints and braces are used, if needed. The territories currently do not have this legislation.

In most provinces, for a long-term care facility to be licensed to operate, it needs to have activity programs in place. Recreational activities performed exercise the joints and muscles and stimulate the circulation. They also provide social opportunities and are mentally stimulating. Long-term care facilities hire activity coordinators to plan and execute activities and programs with residents. Support workers are responsible for bringing residents to and from the scheduled activity, assisting the activity coordinator, and observing residents during the activity to ensure that they are safe.

The activities must meet the interests and physical and psychosocial needs of each client. Facilities often arrange for bingo, movies, dances, exercise groups, gardening activities, shopping trips, museum trips, concerts, and guest speakers.

The right to personal choice is protected in all arrangements, so the client chooses which activities to take part in. Activities should promote physical, intellectual, social, spiritual, and emotional well-being, the last of which is promoted when the client attends activities of personal choice. Clients must not be forced to take part in activities that hold no interest for them.

Clients may need help getting to an activity and participating in it. As a support worker, you need to provide assistance as necessary.

Activity ideas are always welcome from anybody. Clients may share ideas with you or tell you about favourite pastimes, or you may have your own ideas. Share these with the health care team so that they are given to the team that plans activities.

It is important to follow the client’s care plan. Some ROM exercises (especially involving the neck) can cause serious client injury. Follow the guidelines in [BOX 15.3](#b0020) when performing or assisting with ROM exercises.

After performing ROM exercises with the client, report and record the following:

1. • The time the exercises were performed
2. • The joints exercised
3. • The number of times the exercises were performed on each joint
4. • Complaints of pain or signs of stiffness or spasm
5. • The degree to which the client took part in the exercises

BOX 15.3 Guidelines for Performing Range-of-Motion Exercises

1. • Exercise only the joints you are instructed to exercise.
2. • Expose only the body part being exercised.
3. • Use good body mechanics.
4. • Support the part being exercised.
5. • Move the joint slowly, smoothly, and gently.
6. • Do not force a joint beyond its present range of motion or to the point of pain.
7. •
   Perform range-of-motion exercises to the neck only if allowed by employer policy.
    In some facilities and agencies, only physiotherapists or occupational therapists do neck exercises because of the danger of neck injuries.

## Ambulation

Ambulation, the act of walking, helps prevent deconditioning if done regularly. A number of clients need help walking. Some clients become strong enough to walk independently, but some continue to need help, possibly even permanently.

After bed rest, activity is usually increased slowly and in steps. First, the client dangles the legs over the side of the bed. The next step is to sit in a bedside chair, followed by walking about in the room and then in the hallway. Ambulation is not a problem if complications (such as contractures and muscle atrophy) have been prevented by proper positioning and exercise.

### Obesity and Lifestyle and Their Effect on Ambulation

People who are overweight subject their joints to increased wear, which in turn can contribute to the development of osteoarthritis (OA). The most common type of arthritis, OA causes the cartilage within the joints of the body to be worn away, resulting in pain and disfigurement in the joints (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)). Obesity and a sedentary lifestyle are the main contributors to OA.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur151) Excess weight puts stress on weight-bearing joints, such as knees and hips, and increases the risk for cartilage breakdown.[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur151) People who are overweight are encouraged to lose weight in order to reduce the load on their joints.
