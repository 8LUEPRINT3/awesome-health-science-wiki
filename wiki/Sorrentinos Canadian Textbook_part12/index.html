    Focus on Children

    Dehydration

    The bodies of infants and young children contain large amounts of water. Infants and children are at risk for dehydration, and death from dehydration can occur quickly in them. Report any liquid or watery stool immediately. Note the number of wet diapers in 24 hours. Infants wet less when they are dehydrated. Fewer than six wet diapers in 24 hours may indicate that an infant is dehydrated. Report any concerns immediately.

    Symptoms of dehydration for babies and young children can be different than for adults:

    1. • Dry mouth and tongue
    2. • No tears when crying
    3. • Dry diapers for 3 hours
    4. • Sunken eyes, cheeks, soft spot on the top of the skull
    5. • Sleepiness, lack of energy, or irritability

    Source: WebMD. (2020). What is dehydration? What causes it? Retrieved from https://www.webmd.com/a-to-z-guides/dehydration-adults#1.

    Focus on Older Persons

    Dehydration

    In older people, dehydration is one of the most frequent causes of hospitalization after age 65. Kidney function is less efficient in older people, and body water content decreases. There is also less ability to notice changes in body temperature, as well as a reduced sense of thirst. Older people also eat less, which means they are getting fewer fluids from food. Existing illnesses such as diabetes can also increase the risk for dehydration in older people.

    Older persons are at risk for dehydration for many reasons: They may be on medications that promote urine production or increased sweating; many older people experience a decreased thirst sensation; decreased kidney function may result in their bodies being less able to conserve water; and some older persons with dysphagia may be hesitant to drink fluids for fear of choking.

    Diarrhea is, therefore, a very serious condition in older people—any signs of diarrhea should be reported immediately, as death can result from unrecognized and untreated dehydration. Pathogens are often the cause of diarrhea, so preventing the spread of infection is important. In addition, always follow Standard Practices when you come into contact with stools.

    Source: Comfort Keepers. (2019). Senior dehydration: Its dangers for Canada’s seniors and ways to prevent it. https://www.comfortkeepers.ca/senior-dehydration-dangers-canadas-seniors-ways-prevent/.

    Hypertension

    Hypertension is persistent blood pressure measurements above the normal systolic (140 mm Hg) or diastolic (90 mm Hg) pressures (see Chapter 24). Elevated measurements must occur on two consecutive occasions in order to be considered a diagnosis of hypertension. The risk factors identified by the Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada are listed in BOX 33.8.

    Narrowed blood vessels are a common cause of hypertension because when vessels become narrowed, the heart has to pump with more force to move blood through the vessels. Other causes of hypertension include underlying medical issues such as kidney disorders, head injuries, complications of pregnancy, and tumours.

    BOX 33.8 Risk Factors for Hypertension

    1. • Age. Blood pressure tends to rise with age, beginning at about age 35.
    2. • Ethnicity. The incidence of hypertension is statistically higher among Canadians of South Asian, Indigenous, or African descent.
    3. • Family history. If a parent has hypertension, the adult child has a greater chance of also developing it. The risk increases if both parents have hypertension.
    4. • Obesity. The risk increases if the excess weight is stored around the abdomen.
    5. • Diabetes. People with diabetes are at increased risk for hypertension.
    6. • Stress. Repeated exposure to stress may raise blood pressure levels.
    7. • Alcohol. Excessive alcohol consumption increases blood pressure.
    8. • Smoking. Cigarette smoking may contribute to hypertension in some people.

    Hypertension can damage other body organs. The heart may enlarge so that it can pump with more force. Blood vessels in the brain may burst and cause a stroke, and blood vessels in the eyes, kidneys, and other organs may be damaged.

    At first, hypertension may not cause signs or symptoms, so many people with hypertension do not even know they have it. Usually, it is discovered when blood pressure is measured during routine checkups. Signs and symptoms develop as the disorder progresses. Headache, blurred vision, and dizziness may be reported. Complications of hypertension include stroke, heart attack, kidney (renal) failure, and blindness.

    Certain medications can lower blood pressure. The client with hypertension needs to quit smoking if they do smoke, exercise, and get enough rest. A sodium-restricted (low-salt) diet and, if the client is overweight, a low-calorie diet are often ordered by the physician.

    Coronary Artery Disease

    Coronary arteries are located in the heart and supply the heart with oxygen-rich, nutrient-rich blood. Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a condition in which the coronary arteries are narrowed or blocked by thickening and narrowing of the artery walls—called atherosclerosis. Atherosclerosis is caused by a buildup of cholesterol (a soft, waxy substance) and other fatty substances along the inside walls of the arteries (FIGURE 33.16). When coronary arteries become narrowed or blocked, blood flow to the heart is slowed or stopped. The heart muscle does not get enough oxygen and nutrients, so it cannot work properly, leading to chest pain and heart attack. Risk factors for CAD include the following:

    FIGURE 33.16 A, Normal artery. B, Fatty deposits (plaque) on the walls of arteries with atherosclerosis.

    1. • Hypertension
    2. • High blood cholesterol
    3. • Lifestyle factors (lack of exercise, obesity, smoking, excessive alcohol, stress)
    4. • Uncontrolled diabetes
    5. • Age (CAD is more common in older persons)
    6. • Gender (CAD is more common in men)
    7. • Family history of CAD

    Treatment for CAD involves reducing the risk factors. Nothing can be done about the person’s gender, age, and family history. However, efforts can be directed at weight loss, regular exercise, quitting smoking, and maintaining a healthy diet. Controlling blood pressure and diabetes also is important.

    Some clients need coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) to treat or limit their CAD. This surgery creates a bypass (new path) around the diseased part of the artery and thus increases blood flow to the heart. Less invasive procedures such as angioplasty and vascular stenting are now available. There are pros and cons to each procedure. CABG involves general anaesthesia and a longer recovery time, but the beneficial effect of the surgery lasts longer as arteries remain patent (clear) longer. Stenting is done under local anaesthesia, is less invasive, and has a shorter recovery time, but the effect of the procedure may be more limited.

    Angina Pectoris

    Angina pectoris means chest (pectoris) pain (angina) caused by CAD. It occurs when the heart muscle does not get enough oxygen. Clients with angina are at risk for a heart attack. Physical exertion is the most common trigger for angina. Other triggers include emotional stress, extreme cold or heat, exposure to heavy meals, alcohol, and smoking.

    The chest pain associated with angina has been described as a feeling of heaviness, tightness, or pressure. Usually, the pain occurs on the left side, and some clients feel severe pain that lasts 2 to 15 minutes. Pain may travel (radiate) to the jaw, neck, shoulders, back, and arms (FIGURE 33.17). Sometimes, angina is mistaken for indigestion or heartburn when felt in the upper abdominal area. Besides pain, the client may experience shortness of breath, nausea, sweating, dizziness or light-headedness, fatigue, or palpitations (quick heartbeats). Women, older persons, and people with diabetes may have symptoms that differ from these usual symptoms. The signs and symptoms the client experiences will cause the client to stop all activity and seek rest. Rest often relieves the symptoms in 3 to 15 minutes, as it reduces the heart’s need for oxygen. Therefore, normal blood flow is achieved and heart damage is prevented.

    Besides resting and avoiding common triggers, clients are often prescribed nitroglycerin in tablet, ointment, patch, or spray form to relieve angina. The client carries the medication at all times and takes a dose whenever an angina attack occurs. If your client has taken the maximum amount of nitroglycerin and there is no pain relief, call emergency services and your supervisor.

    Myocardial Infarction

    Myocardial infarction (MI) means death (infarction) of heart tissue (myocardium) caused by lack of oxygen to the heart because of sudden interruption of blood flow to the heart. Atherosclerosis or a thrombus (blood clot) obstructs blood flow through an artery, and sudden cardiac death (cardiac arrest) can occur as a result. The area of damage may be small or large (FIGURE 33.18). Common terms for MI are heart attack, coronary, coronary thrombosis, and coronary occlusion. An estimated 1.6 million Canadians are living with heart disease or the effects of stroke, and 2.4 million Canadians are living with diagnosed heart disease.31 Defibrillation, when used with cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), can improve cardiac arrest survival rates to more than 50% if delivered in the first few minutes. For every 1-minute delay in defibrillation, the survival rate decreases by 7 to 10%.

    A client having an MI may have one or more of the signs and symptoms listed in BOX 33.9. Many clients deny that they are having an MI. The average client waits almost 5 hours before getting help; however, it must be remembered that an MI is an emergency. Research being conducted on atypical MIs indicates that many women, especially older women, ignore the early warning signs that can occur at least a month before an MI. Unusual fatigue, sleep disturbances, shortness of breath, indigestion, and anxiety are signs often ignored by women. As a support worker, you need to report any of these symptoms in your client to your supervisor. Treatments are directed at relieving pain, stabilizing vital signs, giving oxygen, and calming the client; medications need to be given as well.

    The client is treated in a hospital coronary care unit (CCU), which has emergency equipment and medications needed to prevent life-threatening complications. The client is kept in the CCU for 2 to 3 days (depending on the area) and then transferred to another nursing unit once stable.

    The client who has suffered an MI is allowed to increase their level of activity gradually. Medications and measures to prevent complications are continued. Rehabilitation, with the goal of preventing another heart attack, is begun in a hospital and continued when the client returns home. Rehabilitation includes an exercise program and teaching about medications, dietary changes, activity, and lifestyle modifications. Activities, including sexual activity, can return to normal levels slowly, and the client can return to work when advised by the physician.

    BOX 33.9 Signs and Symptoms of Myocardial Infarction

    Note: Women may feel no pain at all, but may instead experience nausea, sudden fatigue, or shortness of breath (these are the signs more often reported by women).29

    1. • Sudden, severe chest pain, usually on the left side
    2. • Pain described as crushing, stabbing, or squeezing; most people complain of feelings of tightness, heaviness, pressure, fullness, or burning in the chest
    3. • Pain that radiates to the neck and jaw, and down the arm or to other sites
    4. • Pain that is more severe and lasts longer than with angina
    5. • Pain that is not relieved by rest and nitroglycerine
    6. • Indigestion
    7. • Shortness of breath
    8. • Nausea or vomiting
    9. • Dizziness
    10. • Perspiration
    11. • Cyanosis (bluish lips and nail beds from lack of oxygen in the blood)
    12. • Cold and clammy skin
    13. • Low blood pressure
    14. • Weak and irregular pulse
    15. • Fear and anxiety; a feeling of doom (disaster)

    Congestive Heart Failure

    Congestive heart failure (CHF), or simply heart failure, is an abnormal condition that occurs when the heart cannot pump blood normally. Blood backs up and causes an abnormal amount (congestion) of fluid in the tissues. CHF may affect the right side, left side, or both sides of the heart.

    The right side of the heart receives blood from body tissues and pumps it into the lungs to get oxygen. With right-sided heart failure, blood backs up into the veins. Fluid collection in the body produces weight gain. Pitting edema (swelling due to excess fluid in the tissues—see Chapter 27) becomes evident in the feet and ankles, and the neck veins become enlarged. The liver becomes engorged, which impairs its function. Congestion in the abdomen may cause digestive concerns, including loss of appetite, abdominal pain, and (eventually) weight loss.

    The left side of the heart receives blood from the lungs and pumps it into the rest of the body. With left-sided heart failure, blood collects in lung tissue, resulting in difficulty breathing (dyspnea), increased sputum (mucus in the lungs), cough, and gurgling sounds in the lungs. Dyspnea is worse when the client is active or lying down, so it disrupts sleep. The client may wake up with a feeling of suffocation. Fatigue and weakness in the limbs are common.

    In advanced CHF, the brain may not get enough oxygen, causing confusion and behaviour changes. Poor blood flow to the kidneys results in impaired kidney function and low urine output.

    CHF can be treated and controlled by medications that can strengthen the heart and reduce the amount of fluid in the body. Treatment also includes a sodium-restricted diet and administration of oxygen. Weight is measured daily to check for weight gain, an early sign of fluid buildup. Most clients with CHF prefer the semi-Fowler’s or Fowler’s position for ease of breathing. As a support worker, you may be involved in the following:

    1. • Helping the client maintain bed rest
    2. • Measuring the client’s intake and output
    3. • Measuring daily weight, ideally before the client eats and after voiding
    4. • Helping the client restrict fluids, as ordered by the physician
    5. • Assisting with transfers or ambulation
    6. • Assisting with self-care activities
    7. • Maintaining the client’s good positioning and body alignment according to the care plan
    8. • Applying elastic stockings to reduce leg swelling in the client

    See the Focus on Children: Congestive Heart Failure and Focus on Older Persons: Congestive Heart Failure boxes.

    Focus on Older Persons

    Congestive Heart Failure

    Because the heart is a muscle and muscles weaken with age, many older people develop CHF. Some may need home care or long-term care.

    Older people with CHF are at risk for skin breakdown. Tissue swelling, poor circulation, and fragile skin combine to increase the risk for developing pressure ulcers. Effective skin care and regular position changes are essential.

    Arrhythmias

    Arrhythmias are abnormal (a) heart rhythms (rhythmias). They happen when the heart’s electrical system malfunctions. Sometimes, heartbeats are skipped, or extra beats occur. Usually, the client’s health is not affected, but some arrhythmias are more serious. They can cause dizziness, shortness of breath, fainting, or death. Avoiding caffeine (coffee, tea, colas, and chocolate) and alcohol can sometimes prevent arrhythmias. Medications are needed for severe arrhythmias.

    Pacemakers—medical devices that are implanted in the client’s body to regulate the heartbeat—are used to treat some arrhythmias. They monitor heart rates, and when necessary, they give small electric shocks to stimulate heartbeats. Pacemakers can run on batteries for several years. Clients with pacemakers should avoid any areas or equipment that has strong electrical or magnetic fields, such as some microwaves, security systems, or therapeutic magnetic mattress pads. People who have pacemakers should also ask their doctor about being around cell phones and MP3 players, high-tension wires, metal detectors, industrial welders, electrical generators, and even some machines for medical procedures, such as those used for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

    Phlebitis

    Phlebitis is inflammation of a vein. It occurs most often in the legs. Phlebitis can be caused by bacterial infection, chemical irritation, trauma, lupus, or a genetic condition. Signs and symptoms include redness and warmth in the area, pain, burning, and swelling. Provide supportive care according to the client’s care plan.

    Thrombus

    A thrombus is a blood clot that forms in a blood vessel. If it occurs in a large blood vessel, it will decrease blood flow through the blood vessel. If it occurs in a small blood vessel, it may completely stop blood flow, which can result in the death of the tissue below the blockage. A thrombus can form as a result of a stroke, a heart attack, deficiencies in the blood’s clotting ability, or extended periods of inactivity, such as following surgery (see Chapter 46). A thrombus or a part of it may dislodge and travel through the body until it lodges in another blood vessel. A travelling blood clot is called an embolus, which can eventually move to the lungs and cause severe respiratory problems and death.

    To avoid the formation of blood clots, clients should be encouraged to keep active. It is especially important to encourage leg exercises if they are confined to bed. Follow the client’s care plan to reduce their risk for thrombus.

    Supporting a Client Who Is Living With a Cardiovascular System Disorder

    Clients who are living with a cardiovascular system disorder may feel frightened and vulnerable, and they may be afraid of dying. Besides the suggestions listed within this section, here are some other ways you can best support them:

    1. • Listen and use empathy when speaking to the client. This conveys respect for the client.
    2. • Practise pain-reducing comfort measures. Ensure that the client is in good body alignment at all times. Ensure that they are sitting or lying in a clean, dry, wrinkle-free base at all times (see Chapters 21 and 22). Report and record any pain the client experiences to ensure the client is comfortable and medicated according to their care plan.
    3. • Always obey rules of confidentiality. Never discuss the client or details about the client to anyone not involved in the client’s care.

    Respiratory System Disorders

    The respiratory system is made up of the lungs and their airways. The airways bring oxygen into the lungs and remove carbon dioxide from the body. Respiratory disorders interfere with this function and can threaten life.

    Asthma

    Asthma is a respiratory disorder characterized by recurring episodes of paroxysmal dyspnea (intermittent spasms of shortness of breath); wheezing on expiration, inspiration, or both, caused by constriction (narrowing) of bronchi; coughing; and thick bronchial secretions of mucus. The client with asthma becomes short of breath and produces wheezing sounds, which may be frightening for them. The client may also experience a rapid pulse, perspiration, and cyanosis. Allergies; exercise; cold air; outdoor activity in hot, humid weather; smog; and emotional stress are common causes of asthma attacks.

    Clients with asthma have medications to treat the disorder but, for severe attacks, may require emergency department treatment. You must call emergency services as soon as you see your client in distress. The client and family need to be taught how to prevent asthma attacks, as repeated attacks can damage the respiratory system.

    Pneumonia

    Pneumonia is an infection of lung tissue. Alveoli (tiny air sacs within the lungs) in the affected area fill with pus, mucus, and other liquid, and oxygen and carbon dioxide are not exchanged properly, causing insufficient oxygen in the blood.

    Both bacteria and viruses can cause pneumonia, and both can pose serious health risks to people. Bacterial pneumonia can be life-threatening to clients who are immobile, older, or have a decreased cough reflex. Viral pneumonia can lead to breathing complications in people that are serious enough to require hospitalization and may even cause death. Pneumonia is the eighth leading cause of death in Canada.33 Influenza can lead to pneumonia in some people, especially among older persons and clients with challenged immune systems.34

    The onset can be gradual or sudden. Signs and symptoms include shaking, chills, severe chest pain, a cough that produces rust-coloured or greenish sputum (phlegm, or mucus), high temperature, rapid breathing and pulse rate, cyanosis (blue-tinged skin), and confusion.

    While providing support care, follow Standard Practices. Transmission-Based Practices (see Chapter 18) may be ordered by the physician to prevent the spread to others, especially in facilities, where frail clients with compromised immune systems may be very vulnerable to contracting pneumonia. It is important that support workers be aware of what the precautions are and how to strictly adhere to isolation procedures. Immunizations for pneumonia for high-risk clients are now available.

    Bronchitis

    Acute bronchitis is inflammation of the bronchi (the large airway passages entering the lungs; singular, bronchus) caused by bacteria or viruses and can last for several days or weeks. Chronic bronchitis is not generally caused by a virus (see Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease, below).

    A client with acute bronchitis usually has a cough that produces sputum. The colour of the sputum will indicate if the infection is viral or bacterial. Green-coloured mucus may indicate a bacterial infection, but only a lab test will confirm the type of infection and will determine the treatment the physician will prescribe. Fever, fatigue, shortness of breath, and chest pain may accompany the cough. Bacterial bronchitis is treated with antibiotics, and viral bronchitis will usually disappear on its own. While providing support care to a client, if you observe blood-tinged sputum, report it immediately to your supervisor.

    Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease

    Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) refers to chronic bronchitis and emphysema, which often occur together. It is the fourth leading cause of death worldwide and is expected to climb to third place by 2030, according to the World Health Organization.35 For Canadian women, COPD causes more deaths than breast cancer does.35 COPD is a persistent lung disorder that obstructs (blocks) the airways and makes breathing difficult. It is a progressive disease, gradually worsening over time.

    Smoking is the most common cause of COPD. Long-term exposure to chemical fumes and to certain dusts is another cause.

    COPD cannot be cured but it can be controlled. The client must quit smoking and avoid secondhand smoke. Medications to open the airways are usually delivered by metered-dose inhalers (MDIs), or “puffers” (see Chapter 45). Breathing exercises and oxygen therapy may also be ordered. Fluid intake is encouraged, to decrease the thickness of secretions, and efforts are made to prevent respiratory tract infections. If one occurs, prompt treatment is necessary.

    Chronic Bronchitis

    Chronic bronchitis is a persistent inflammation of the bronchi. Large amounts of mucus are produced in the bronchi, and the bronchial walls swell. The mucus is thick and difficult to cough up, so it cannot be cleared completely and thus obstructs the airways, making airflow into and out of the lungs difficult. Therefore, the body cannot get normal amounts of oxygen. The excess mucus also provides a place for the growth of pathogens, so infection is a risk. Coughing is the first and most common symptom of chronic bronchitis. The client also has difficulty breathing and tires easily. Eventually, breathing becomes difficult even when the client is resting.

    Emphysema

    Emphysema occurs when the walls of the alveoli (small cavities; singular, alveolus) are damaged and become less elastic than normal. They do not expand and shrink as normal during inhalation and exhalation, and as a result, when the client breathes out, air is trapped in some of the alveoli and not exhaled. As the disease progresses, more alveoli become involved, and more air is trapped. The normal exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide cannot occur in the affected alveoli. As more air remains trapped in the lungs, the lungs begin to overinflate in an attempt to absorb more oxygen. Over time, this overinflation of the lungs will cause the client’s rib cage to push out permanently and will develop a barrel shape. This is known as a barrel chest (FIGURE 33.19).

    Breathing is very difficult for people with emphysema. Sometimes, they lose weight because eating is difficult when they are out of breath. Clients with emphysema usually prefer to sit upright and slightly forward because breathing is easier in this position.

    Treatments include the following:

    1. • Antibiotics to fight infection
    2. • Medications to ease chest pain, cough, and fever
    3. • Proper diet
    4. • Proper fluid and hydration
    5. • Fowler’s or semi-Fowler’s position to make breathing easier
    6. • Oxygen therapy

    Influenza

    Influenza (“the flu”) is a communicable respiratory tract infection caused by a virus. In Canada, the flu season lasts from November or December through April or May. Millions of people are infected with the flu each year. Influenza is highly contagious and spreads when an infected person coughs or sneezes. It can also be spread by indirect contact—for example, by touching an object such as a doorknob or a telephone that was recently handled by an infected person.

    Onset is usually sudden, with headache, chills, and cough, followed by fever, loss of appetite, muscle aches, and tiredness. Coldlike symptoms, including runny nose, sneezing, watery eyes, and throat irritation, occur, as well as nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea, especially in children. Recovery is usually complete in 1 to 2 weeks. However, some people develop serious and life-threatening complications—for example, pneumonia (see page 797).

    H1N1, COVID-19, and Subsequent Viral Variants

    Two known coronaviruses are the H1N1 influenza virus (and all subsequent viruses that have since emerged from that strain) and COVID-19 (see Chapter 19 for a list of symptoms, and page 758 for more information). These are extremely contagious illnesses that primarily affect the respiratory system in most people. These germs can also rest on hard surfaces such as counters and doorknobs and can be transferred to a person’s hands and then transmitted to their respiratory system when they touch their mouth, nose, or both. COVID-19 and subsequent variants (strains) have been shown to be especially virulent.

    The H1N1 virus emerged in the spring of 2009 among people in North America and has affected primarily young people. The main symptoms of H1N1 infection are fever and cough. Because humans had little to no natural immunity to this new virus, it caused serious and widespread illness. Further variations of this virus have also emerged. Although the illness was originally called “swine flu,” it is not possible to catch it by eating pork or pork products, or through blood transfusions.

    The initial symptoms of COVID-19 were first thought to be the same as those of H1N1, although the list of symptoms of COVID-19 has increased (see Chapter 19) as more information about the illness is reported. As of this writing, worldwide the number of people infected with the virus is approaching 50 million, with over 1 million deaths.

    When providing supportive care, good hygiene and Standard Practices are essential to reduce the risk for infection. Washing your hands before and after client contact is crucial, as is the frequent use of waterless hand sanitizer after touching surfaces the client has touched. The most effective method of prevention is the flu vaccine, which is necessary yearly for people at high risk. People who are most at risk include the following:

    1. • People 65 years of age and older
    2. • Residents of long-term care facilities
    3. • People who have chronic diseases
    4. • Caregivers and people who live with someone in a high-risk group

    Your employer may request or require that you get a yearly flu vaccination, as it protects both you and your clients.

    Tuberculosis

    Tuberculosis (TB) is a persistent bacterial infection generally transmitted by the inhalation or ingestion of infected droplets and usually affecting the lungs, although infection of multiple organ systems can occur. TB was a major cause of death in the early 1900s. When TB medications were introduced in the 1940s, a dramatic decline in the number of TB cases resulted.

    The bacteria causing TB (Mycobacterium tuberculosis) are spread by airborne droplets (see Chapter 19) when the infected person coughs, sneezes, or speaks and others in the environment inhale the bacteria. Anyone who has close, frequent contact with an infected person is at risk. TB is more likely to occur in places where people live in crowded situations (such as inner-city neighbourhoods, group homes, prisons, and long-term care facilities) and in developing countries that lack adequate health care. People who have immune disorders such as HIV infection are also at risk. If untreated, TB can infect multiple organ systems through a person’s body.

    Sometimes, the bacteria causing TB can reside in a person and not cause an infection until many years later. This is called latent TB. Clients with active TB may not have symptoms at first, and the disease may be found during a routine chest X-ray or a TB skin test. Early signs and symptoms are tiredness, loss of appetite, weight loss, fever, and night sweats. Coughing occurs and becomes more frequent as the disease progresses; increased sputum production and chest pain also occur.

    Clients with active TB may need to be treated in a hospital, and when they return home, they must continue taking TB medications daily for several months. Clients with TB need to cover their nose and mouth with tissues when coughing or sneezing and then flush the tissues down the toilet. In health care facilities, tissues used by clients with TB are placed in a biohazard bag and disposed of according to facility policy. Standard Practices and Airborne Precautions must be practised during care of a client with TB (see Chapter 19).

    TB is still a global health concern—it is more common among people with immune challenges (such as those with HIV or AIDS). Since the late 1980s, the number of cases of TB in Canada has remained the same. In 2017, the rate of active TB in Canada was 4.9 per 100 000 population. The rate was highest among Canadian-born Indigenous people (21.5 per 100 000 population). Worldwide, an estimated 10 million people fell ill with TB in 2017. The Canadian government monitors all new and re-treatment cases of active TB disease from data that are voluntarily submitted from provincial and territorial public health authorities.36

    Supporting a Client Who Is Living With a Respiratory System Disorder

    Clients who are living with a respiratory system disorder may be frightened if they struggle to breathe or if they are uncomfortable as a result of their condition. Besides the suggestions listed within this section, here are some other ways you can best support them:

    1. • Protect yourself from the contagious illness such as influenza. Wear the proper PPE that is recommended in the client’s care plan. Follow Standard Practices (see Chapter 19) at all times. Change out of your uniform before leaving the agency or client’s home to avoid risk of spreading pathogens.
    2. • Practise comfort measures that can assist the client to breathe. Ensure that the client is in good body alignment at all times. Ensure that they are sitting or lying in a clean, dry, wrinkle-free base at all times (see Chapters 21 and 22). Report and record any shortness of breath the client experiences to ensure the client is comfortable and medicated according to the client’s care plan.
    3. • Listen and use empathy when speaking to the client. This conveys respect for the client.
    4. • Always obey rules of confidentiality. Never discuss the client or details about the client to anyone not involved in the client’s care.

    Immune System Disorders

    The immune system protects the body from pathogens, cancer cells, and other harmful substances. It defends against threats inside and outside the body. Immune system disorders occur from impairment of the immune response. The response may be inappropriate, too strong, or lacking. See discussion of the immune system in Chapter 32.

    One immune system disorder that will be discussed here is caused by the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and can lead to acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) in some people. Other disorders of the immune system that are discussed include autoimmune disorders.

    Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)

    Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection is caused by a virus that attacks the body’s immune system, making it unable to fight off certain infections. If the virus goes untreated, it can progress to acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS). People who are infected with HIV are known as HIV-positive. There are antiviral medication regimens available for people with HIV or AIDS that can prolong their lives and enable them to live for years and even decades. Some individuals may go on to develop other health conditions that may be unrelated to HIV or AIDS.

    According to Health Canada, people who are HIV-positive may develop AIDS in 5 to 10 years if they receive no treatment for HIV infection. If they start antiviral treatment early, they can live a longer and healthier life. They can also prevent the sexual transmission of HIV to any partners if they are receiving treatment and do not have any active virus detected in their body.

    It is often assumed that HIV is only a sexually transmitted infection, but it can also be transmitted non-sexually to people in all age groups. HIV is spread when infected body fluid, such as blood, semen and pre-seminal fluid, fluid from the rectum, and fluid from the vagina, from a person with HIV gets into the body of another person. The infected fluid can enter through broken skin or the mucous membrane linings of the body, such as the vagina, rectum, foreskin, or the opening of the penis. It can be spread by sharing drug-use equipment (such as needles, syringes, or other equipment used to inject drugs). Also, a child-bearing individual who is HIV positive can pass the virus to their baby during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding.

    Some people may not develop any symptoms immediately but then develop mild flulike symptoms 2 to 4 weeks after being infected with HIV. During the first few weeks of HIV infection, some tests may not be able to detect the virus. Other people may have HIV but not know it because they may not experience any symptoms for up to 10 years. The symptoms of HIV infection may last from several days to weeks and then seem to disappear. Unfortunately, people who are HIV-positive can still pass the virus to other individuals during this time. Common early symptoms, which may be mistaken for other illnesses, include the following:

    1. • Fever
    2. • Fatigue
    3. • Chills
    4. • Sore throat
    5. • Headache
    6. • Muscle aches
    7. • Joint pain
    8. • Swollen glands (lymph nodes)

    If a person suspects they might have the symptoms of HIV or AIDS, they need to see a health care provider to get tested, counselled, and, if positive for the virus, treated as soon as possible. It is also important that they notify any sexually intimate partners or people with whom they have shared drug-use equipment.

    Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS)

    AIDS is an HIV infection that has progressed, usually because it was not treated. Starting treatment early can help a person who is infected with HIV live a near-normal lifespan. Symptoms of AIDS include certain types of the following conditions:

    1. • Pneumonia
    2. • Cancerous tumours on the skin
    3. • Fungal infections, such as yeast infections
    4. • Viral infections, such as shingles
    5. • Long-term diarrhea
    6. • Rapid, unexplained weight loss

    Supporting a Client Who Is Living With HIV or AIDS

    1. • Listen. Being diagnosed with HIV is life-changing news. Listen to the client and offer support and reassure them that HIV is now a manageable health condition.
    2. • Encourage the client to adhere to their medication regimen. It is important for people living with HIV to take their HIV medication every day, exactly as prescribed. Working with the client and their significant others, help them to establish a medication routine and encourage them to stick to it.
    3. • Encourage healthy practices. Encourage the client to eat nutrient-rich foods that follow recommendations from Canada’s Food Guide (2019). Encourage the client to develop healthy practices, such as reducing alcohol intake, getting enough sleep and exercise, avoiding use of recreational drugs without their health care provider’s consent, and practicing safer sex.
    4. • Observe for signs of illness. People living with HIV may be less resistant to illnesses such as colds or influenza. Encourage the client to seek early treatment if feeling unwell.
    5. • Encourage support groups and counselling. Groups such as Canadian AIDS Society (https://www.cdnaids.ca/) can be a valuable source of information and support to the client and significant others. Groups can also suggest what clients can say to intimate partners who are unaware of their diagnosis.
    6. • Encourage the client and family to educate themselves about HIV. Understanding how HIV medications work, the signs of illness to look for, and ways to stay healthy can give the client a sense of empowerment.

    Autoimmune Disorders

    Autoimmune disorders occur when the immune system attacks the body’s own (auto) healthy cells, tissues, or organs. They can affect any part of the body and often affect several parts of the body at the same time (FIGURE 33.20). Autoimmune disorders can change how organs function and can also cause an organ to grow abnormally. Some of the more common autoimmune disorders discussed in this chapter include the following:

    1. • Multiple sclerosis
    2. • Rheumatoid arthritis

    FIGURE 33.20 Body parts that can be affected by autoimmune disease. 5. • Scleroderma 6. • Type 1 diabetes 7. • Celiac disease 8. • Inflammatory bowel disease (including Crohn’s disease, colitis, and irritable bowel syndrome [IBS]).

    Other common autoimmune disorders include the following:

    1. • Hashimoto’s disease. Antibodies react against proteins in the thyroid gland, causing gradual destruction of the gland. Eventually, it will not produce enough thyroid hormone. The person experiences fatigue, weakness, weight gain, sensitivity to cold, muscle aches, stiff joints, facial swelling, and constipation.
    2. • Graves’ disease. Antibodies to a receptor on the thyroid gland can cause the gland to overproduce thyroid hormone, leading to a hyperthyroid condition. The person has anxiety, problems sleeping, rapid heart rate, weight loss, sweating, muscle weakness, shaky hands, and bulging eyes.
    3. • Lupus. This inflammatory disease can damage the joints, skin, kidneys, heart, lungs, and other body parts. A “butterfly rash” across the nose and cheeks is common. Lupus can be difficult to diagnose, as symptoms can vary and be mistaken for those of other illnesses. Clients with lupus may have periods of illness alternating with periods of remission. Some people with lupus are able to live normal lives, whereas others may be very sick. When caring for a client with lupus, follow the care plan carefully, as each client’s treatment will be different.

    Digestive System Disorders

    The digestive system breaks down food for absorption by the body and also eliminates solid wastes. See Chapter 30 for a more detailed discussion of some specific digestive disorders—diarrhea, constipation, flatulence, and fecal incontinence.

    Vomiting

    Vomiting (emesis) is the act of expelling stomach contents through the mouth. It signals illness or injury that can be life-threatening. Vomitus (material vomited) can be aspirated (inhaled into the lungs) and obstruct the airway. Vomiting large amounts of blood can lead to shock. Practise the following measures when a client vomits:

    1. • Follow Standard Practices.
    2. • Turn the client’s head well to one side to prevent aspiration.
    3. • Place a kidney basin or other container under the client’s chin.
    4. • Remove vomitus from the client’s immediate environment.
    5. • Provide oral hygiene to help remove the taste of vomitus.
    6. • Eliminate odours.
    7. • Change linens or the client’s clothing, as necessary.
    8. • Observe vomitus for colour, odour, and undigested food. Vomitus that looks like coffee grounds may contain digested blood, which could indicate internal bleeding and must be reported immediately.
    9. • Estimate the amount of vomitus. In the community, you may not have an accurate means of measuring the amount of vomitus. You could estimate a cupful or plateful or follow your agency’s policy to determine small, medium, and large amounts. Some facilities will require you to measure and record the amount of vomitus according to their policy.
    10. • Report immediately to your supervisor that the client has vomited, and report your observations. Some facilities may require you to keep the vomitus so that it can be observed or sent to a laboratory for analysis.

    Gastro-Esophageal Reflux Disease

    Gastro-esophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a common disorder of unknown cause. The muscle band at the end of the esophagus and the start of the stomach relaxes at inappropriate times, allowing the stomach acids to go up the esophagus. Clients may refer to it as “heartburn,” which is the most common symptom of GERD. Other symptoms include a sour taste in the mouth, bad breath, belching, and difficulty or pain when swallowing. Treatment usually involves medication and may include raising the head of an adjustable bed or putting blocks under the head of the bed to keep the top part of the body at a higher level than the stomach.

    Gallbladder Disease

    The gallbladder is a pear-shaped sac that stores bile (see Chapter 32). For reasons yet unknown, the components of bile often form stones in the gallbladder, which can cause inflammation, called cholecystitis. Stones can obstruct the flow of bile into the digestive system. Symptoms of cholecystitis include severe pain in the abdomen, nausea, vomiting, and, in some cases, jaundice. Treatment may include surgery and a low-fat diet.

    Liver Disease

    The liver plays an important part in carbohydrate and protein metabolism and produces bile. Two of the most common disorders of the liver are hepatitis and cirrhosis. Liver transplantation can successfully treat some liver diseases.

    Hepatitis may be caused by a bacterial or viral infection (viruses can be hepatitis A to hepatitis G), a parasitic infection, alcohol or drug use, toxins, or a transfusion of incompatible blood. (See page 761 for a discussion on communicable hepatitis diseases.)

    Cirrhosis occurs as a result of persistent liver disease. Liver tissue is gradually replaced by scar tissue, which affects liver function. It is most commonly caused by alcoholism and hepatitis C virus. There is no cure for cirrhosis, but treatment can halt further damage. Some signs and symptoms are weight loss, jaundice, enlarged liver, fatigue, and accumulation of fluid in the abdomen.

    Celiac Disease

    Celiac disease is an inborn metabolic disorder that results in the inability to tolerate gluten protein. Gluten is found in wheat, oats, and barley. The disease affects adults and young children and causes abdominal distension, vomiting, diarrhea, muscle wasting, and extreme lethargy. Diarrhea is usually large in volume, foul smelling, and pale in colour. The only effective treatment is a gluten-free diet. As a support worker, you must ensure that you follow the care plan regarding your client’s diet, particularly if they live with celiac disease. Since many foods can contain traces of gluten, accurate reporting of foods that cause symptoms can help identify foods the client should avoid.

    Irritable Bowel Syndrome

    Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a disorder of the bowel characterized by abdominal pain and changes in bowel habits. The two main types of IBS are (1) IBS with diarrhea and (2) IBS with constipation. A client suffering from IBS with diarrhea often has abdominal bloating, urgency, and frequent loose and watery stools that sometimes contain mucus. IBS with constipation can also cause abdominal bloating. Clients frequently need to strain during bowel movement, produce hard or lumpy stools, and are left with the feeling of incomplete bowel emptying. Abdominal pain, which can be quite severe, is usually relieved after bowel movement.

    The cause of IBS is not yet known, although stress is thought to trigger episodes. Treatment will vary, depending on which type of IBS the client is diagnosed with. Medication for IBS with diarrhea consists of antidiarrheals, and medications for IBS with constipation include stool softeners or laxatives. For some clients, avoiding nonsoluble fibrous foods can help control episodes.

    Compassion is required when caring for a client with IBS, since the condition can be quite stressful and embarrassing. You may be asked to record the dietary intake of your client to determine which foods trigger episodes.

    Colitis

    Colitis is inflammation (itis) of the colon (col). Signs and symptoms of colitis include fever, pain, abdominal tenderness, rectal bleeding, and ulcerations in the colon. Treatment may include antibiotics and anti-inflammatory drugs. A reduction in the intake of carbohydrates has helped some clients. Surgery may be required to remove the affected part of the colon.

    Crohn’s Disease

    Crohn’s disease is a persistent, inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract that is characterized by flare-ups and periods of remission. It can affect all age groups, and no exact cause has been identified for this disorder. The disease can affect any part of the GI system, but it most commonly affects the last part of the small intestine and the large intestine. Symptoms can vary, depending on the area affected. The most common symptoms are abdominal pain, diarrhea, and weight loss. Some clients have episodes of bloody diarrhea and can have as many as 20 bowel movements a day. Bowel obstructions can occur because of the narrowing of the intestines from persistent inflammation and, if severe enough, can become life-threatening.

    Crohn’s disease can also affect other body systems. Some complications include skin rashes, arthritis, eye inflammation, mouth ulcers, and anemia.

    Treatment for a flare-up usually involves medications to reduce inflammation and infection, if present. During remission, the goal of treatment is to build up the client’s physical and mental ability to cope with the next flare-up when it occurs. There is no cure for Crohn’s disease, a fact that is difficult for many clients to accept.

    Crohn’s disease affects each client differently, depending on the areas of the bowel and other body systems affected. Your observations, recording, and reporting are very important to help your client during a flare-up. For example, a client vomiting or having a fever could mean a bowel obstruction; a client complaining of pain around the anus could indicate an inflammation or start of an abscess, and joint pain could be the start of arthritis associated with Crohn’s disease.

    It is very important to record the colour and approximate volume of each bowel movement and to describe if blood is visible. During flare-ups, clients can lose weight because of decreased food intake and malabsorption. They may hesitate to eat, being fearful of more bouts of diarrhea. Follow the client’s care plan for dietary intake.

    Diverticular Disease

    Diverticular disease is very common in older persons. It is characterized by the development of small pouches that bulge outward through weak spots in the colon (FIGURE 33.21). Each pouch is called a diverticulum (diverticulare means “turn inside out”). The condition of having these pouches is called diverticulosis (osis means “condition of”).

    When feces enter the pouches, the pouches can become inflamed and infected—which is called diverticulitis (itis means “inflammation”). The client has abdominal pain and tenderness on the lower left side of the abdomen. Fever, nausea and vomiting, chills, cramping, and constipation are likely to be present. Bloating, rectal bleeding, frequent urination, and pain while urinating can also occur.

    A ruptured pouch is a rare complication that results in feces spilling out into the abdomen, leading to a severe, life-threatening infection. A pouch also can cause a block in the intestine (intestinal obstruction) that feces and gas cannot move past. These conditions require surgery.

    A low-fibre diet and constipation are risk factors for diverticular disease. To treat it, the physician orders dietary changes and, in some cases, antibiotics. Surgery to remove the diseased part of the bowel is needed for severe disease, obstruction, and ruptured pouches. Sometimes, a colostomy is necessary (see Chapter 30).

    1. • Listen and use empathy when speaking to the client. This conveys respect for the client.
    2. • Practise pain-reducing comfort measures. Ensure that the client is sitting or lying in a clean, dry, wrinkle-free base at all times (see Chapters 21 and 22). Report and record any pain or discomfort the client experiences to ensure that the client is comfortable and medicated according to the client’s care plan.     Urinary System Disorders

    The kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra are the major structures of the urinary system. Disorders can occur in one or more of these structures.

    Urinary Tract Infections

    Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are common, and infection in one area of the urinary system can lead to infection of the entire urinary system (see Chapter 29).

    Normally, the urinary system is sterile, meaning it has no microbes or pathogens. However, pathogens can enter the system through the urethra. Catheterization, urological examinations, infections through sexual intercourse, poor perineal hygiene, incomplete bladder emptying, and poor fluid intake are common causes of UTIs. UTIs are frequently acquired in health care settings.

    Women are at high risk because pathogens can easily enter the short urethra. Prostate gland secretions help protect men from UTIs. However, an enlarged prostate increases the risk for a UTI, so older men are more likely than younger men to get one.

    Cystitis means inflammation (itis) of the bladder (cyst). Pyelonephritis is an inflammation (itis) of the kidney (nephr) and renal pelvis (pyelo) due to bacterial infection. Signs and symptoms are as follows:

    1. • Urinary frequency and urgency
    2. • Oliguria—scant (olig) urine (uria)
    3. • Dysuria—difficult or painful (dys) urination (uria)
    4. • Pain or burning during urination
    5. • Foul-smelling urine
    6. • Hematuria—blood (hemat) in the urine (uria)
    7. • Pyria—pus (py) in the urine (uria)
    8. • Fever and chills
    9. • Pain in the lower abdomen or back

    In older persons, sudden confusion can indicate a possible urinary infection. Treatment includes antibiotics and increased fluid intake—usually 2 000 mL/day.

    Renal Calculi

    Renal calculi are kidney (renal) stones (calculi). Prolonged bed rest, immobility, and poor fluid intake are risk factors. Stones vary in size and in mineral content. Signs and symptoms include the following:

    1. • Severe, cramping pain in the back and side, just below the ribs
    2. • Pain in the abdomen, thigh, and urethra
    3. • Nausea and vomiting
    4. • Fever and chills
    5. • Urinary frequency and urgency
    6. • Oliguria—scant (olig) urine (uria)
    7. • Dysuria—difficult or painful (dys) urination (uria)
    8. • Hematuria—blood (hemat) in the urine (uria)
    9. • Foul-smelling urine

    Treatment involves medication for pain relief and increased intake of fluids—about 2 000 to 3 000 mL a day. Increased fluids cause stones to leave the body through the increased excretion of urine. All the client’s urine must be strained to catch any stones that may be passed during urination (see Chapter 29). Sometimes, however, surgical removal of stones may be necessary. Dietary changes can help prevent further formation of stones, so dietitians are usually part of the health care team.

    Renal Failure

    Renal failure (kidney failure) occurs when the kidneys do not function or are severely impaired. When the kidneys fail, waste products are not removed from blood, and the body retains fluids. As fluids build up in the bloodstream, the client may have puffy and swollen face, hands, and feet. Hypertension and CHF can result. Renal failure can be acute or persistent.

    Acute Renal Failure

    Acute renal failure occurs suddenly after severely decreased blood flow to the kidneys caused by severe bleeding, heart attack, CHF, burns, infections, or severe allergic reactions. Acute renal failure is usually a temporary condition. With treatment, the condition may be reversed. However, it is a very serious condition, as it can result in death.

    Acute renal failure occurs in phases. First, oliguria (scant amount of urine) occurs, when urine output is less than 400 mL in 24 hours. This phase lasts a few days to 2 weeks. Then diuresis—the process (esis) of passing (di) urine (uria)—occurs, and large amounts of urine are produced, about 1 000 to 5 000 mL/day. Kidney function improves and returns to normal during the recovery phase. The disease process can take from 1 month to 1 year. However, some people do not recover and develop chronic renal failure.

    The physician will order medications, restricted fluids, and diet therapy. The care plan will likely include the following:

    1. • Measuring and recording urine output every hour if the client has a catheter (an output of less than 30 mL per hour should be reported immediately)
    2. • Measuring and recording intake and output of fluid
    3. • Restricting fluid intake
    4. • Daily weight measurements using the same scale
    5. • Frequent oral hygiene
    6. • Measures to take if the client is confined to bed
    7. • Measures to prevent infection

    Chronic Renal Failure

    In chronic renal failure, the kidneys cannot meet the body’s needs. The nephrons of the kidneys are destroyed over many years. Hypertension and diabetes are common causes; some others are infections, urinary tract obstruction, and tumours.

    Signs and symptoms appear when 80 to 90% of kidney function has been lost. A few of the many signs and symptoms are yellow skin; dry, itchy, or brittle skin; inflammation of the mouth; bruises and bleeding; hypertension; and a burning sensation in the legs and feet.

    Each body system is affected because waste products build up in the blood. There is no cure; in the early stages, however, diet therapy, fluid restriction, and medications may slow kidney damage. The general care required for clients in chronic renal failure is described in BOX 33.10.

    Once the person has lost considerable kidney function, dialysis becomes necessary. Dialysis is the process of removing wastes and excess water from blood. Your supervisor will ensure that you are aware of your responsibilities and scope of practice when giving care. Clients for whom dialysis is not adequate treatment may require kidney transplantation.

    Reproductive System Disorders

    Aging affects the reproductive system. Injuries, diseases, and surgeries can also affect reproductive structures and functions. Some more common reproductive system disorders include benign prostatic hypertrophy (in clients with male genitalia), pelvic organ prolapse (in clients with female genitalia), and infertility.

    Benign Prostatic Hypertrophy (BPH)

    Benign prostatic hypertrophy (BPH) is an enlarged prostate gland. The prostate gland surrounds the urethra of a client with male genitalia, the tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body. As the prostate enlarges, it may partially block the urethra. As a result, affected clients may have difficulty urinating.37

    BPH is very common in older men. In fact, about half of all men older than 75 have some symptoms of it. BPH is not a form of cancer, nor does it cause prostate cancer. It also does not cause erection difficulties or affect a client’s ability to produce children.37

    BPH can cause urinary concerns, however, such as the following:37

    1. • Difficulty stopping or starting urination
    2. • Frequently feeling the need to urinate, which can interrupt sleep
    3. • A weak or slow urine stream
    4. • A feeling that the bladder is not empty even after urination
    5. • Urinary dribbling (leaking)
    6. • Blood in the urine, especially when trying to force a urinary stream

    If your client cannot urinate without extreme difficulty, the bladder may be blocked, and your supervisor should be notified. This problem is rare but may cause backed-up urine (urinary retention), leading to bladder infection, bladder stones, or kidney damage.37

    Pelvic Organ Prolapse (POP)38

    In individuals with female genitalia, pelvic organs such as the uterus, rectum, and bladder are held in place by muscles, tissue, and ligaments. Prolapse happens when one or more of these organs slip down from their normal position and into the vagina because their supportive tissues have become weak. It is often associated with urinary incontinence. A prolapse of the bladder is known as a cystocele, and a prolapse of the rectum is known as a rectocele.

    Most clients with pelvic organ prolapse (POP) will notice a bulge or feel pressure or heaviness in the vagina. It is not a life-threatening condition and does not need to be treated unless the symptoms are bothersome. However, with large prolapses, bulging can be visible from the vaginal area. When performing perineal care (see Chapter 17) on a client with a visible POP, health care workers must be very careful not to irritate the delicate exposed tissues.

    Although the specific causes of POP are unknown, there are risk factors for developing POP that are related to tissue, nerve, or muscle weakness or damage. Previous pregnancies and vaginal births can cause injury to these structures and may be related to the development of POP at any time in the future. Other risk factors include obesity, aging, nerve and connective tissue disorders, and increased abdominal pressure due to heavy lifting or chronic coughing.

    General recommendations for POP include the following:

    1. • Avoid straining to void or have bowel movements. Keep stools soft and avoid constipation.
    2. • Empty the bladder completely with each void.
    3. • Watch for symptoms of UTI, such as urinary urgency or frequency, blood in the urine, or burning with voiding. As a support worker, It is important to report any of these symptoms in your clients.
    4. • Lose weight if necessary. Excess weight will cause increased pressure on the pelvic organs and is a very important aspect of bladder control and POP.
    5. • Use Kegel exercises (also known as pelvic floor exercises) to strengthen the muscles of the pelvic floor that support the vagina, bladder, and urethra.
    6. • A vaginal estrogen cream, pill, or ring may be prescribed, especially if the vaginal tissue is irritated or thin.

    Infertility

    For some people, getting pregnant can be a real challenge. Infertility can be traced to either the man or woman, or a combination of both. Infertility usually refers to a lack of conception (getting pregnant) after a reasonable period of sexual intercourse or medical fertilization treatment without contraception (1 year for women who are under 35 years of age, 6 months for women over 35 years of age). Roughly 16% (or 1 in 6) couples in Canada experience infertility. This number has doubled since the 1980s.

    Causes of infertility in women may include the following:

    1. • Age (fertility decreases after age 35)
    2. • Difficulty producing eggs (which shows up when the client with female genitalia has no period or menstrual cycle, or no regular one)
    3. • Having an STI, such as chlamydia (which can cause blockages in the fallopian tubes)
    4. • Health concerns involving the uterus (such as fibroids or polyps)
    5. • Issues with the fallopian tubes (such as missing tubes or blockages)
    6. • Endometriosis (excess of tissue that gathers around the reproductive organs)
    7. • Hormonal imbalances
    8. • Early menopause (before age 40)

    Causes of infertility in men may include the following:

    1. • Poor sperm quality (their rate of movement and shape)
    2. • Low sperm count, or lack of sperm
    3. • A history of STI, such as chlamydia
    4. • Hormonal imbalances

    Other causes of infertility in clients may include the following:

    1. • Past treatments for cancer (e.g., chemotherapy, radiation, and/or surgery)
    2. • Some chronic illnesses (such as diabetes), as well as their treatments
    3. • Tobacco and alcohol use
    4. • Being underweight or overweight

    Fertility challenges may also respond to medical treatment, especially if the menstruating client has severe pain during periods (menstruation) or while having sex (these may be signs of endometriosis); has no periods or has irregular periods (this may point to problems with ovulation); has had past surgery in the abdomen or uterus; has had more than one miscarriage; has a history of an STI; has had cancer treatment; or has a family history of early menopause (before age 40). Clients with male genitalia should seek medical treatment if they have had surgery or experienced an injury to the testicles in the past, have premature ejaculations or have difficulty having an erection, have a history of an STI, or have had cancer treatment.

    Key Points

    • Cancer includes a group of diseases characterized by out-of-control cell division and growth leading to the development of a lump or mass of cells. When cancer cells break off from the tumour, they travel to other body parts, where new tumours grow. Cancer can occur in almost any body part. Common cancers are bowel, breast, prostate, and skin cancers.

    • Integumentary system disorders involve the skin and its appendages. They include urticaria (hives), dermatitis, eczema, scabies, psoriasis, boils (furuncles), cysts, impetigo, and skin cancer.

    • Musculoskeletal system disorders affect bones, joints, muscles, and the ability to move. Some disorders are caused by injury, and others result from aging or disease. The conditions described here for this system are fractures, osteomyelitis, amputation of a limb, osteoporosis, arthritis, fibromyalgia, scleroderma, and gout.

    • Nervous system disorders involve the disruption of signals between the brain and other parts of the body. These disorders can affect physical and cognitive functions. Conditions described include epilepsy, stroke, acquired brain injuries (ABIs), Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, multiple sclerosis (MS), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and spinal cord injuries.

    • Endocrine system disorders involve the glands that secrete hormones. These disorders result in hormone levels that are too high or too low. The three most common endocrine disorders are diabetes, hyperthyroidism, and hypothyroidism.

    • Cardiovascular system disorders involve the heart and blood vessels. Disorders described here are phlebitis, thrombus, hypertension, coronary artery disease (CAD), congestive heart failure (CHF), and arrhythmias.

    • Respiratory system disorders involve the lungs and their airways. Disorders described here are asthma, pneumonia, bronchitis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

    • Immune system disorders occur from malfunctioning of the immune response. Autoimmune disorders occur when the immune system attacks the body’s own (auto) healthy cells, tissues, or organs. Common autoimmune disorders include MS, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), scleroderma, type 1 diabetes, celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease (including Crohn’s disease, colitis, and irritable bowel syndrome [IBS]), Hashimoto’s disease, Graves’ disease, and lupus.

    • Digestive system disorders affect the proper breaking down of food for absorption by the body and also the elimination of solid wastes. Disorders include gastro-esophageal reflux disease (GERD), gallbladder disease, liver disease, celiac disease, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), colitis, Crohn’s disease, and diverticular disease.

    • Urinary system disorders involve the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra, which are the major structures of the urinary system. Disorders can include urinary tract infections (UTIs), renal calculi, and renal failure, which can be acute or persistent.

    • Reproductive system disorders such as benign prostatic hypertrophy (BPH) in men and pelvic organ prolapse (POP) can lead to difficulty in urination and urinary incontinence. Infertility can lead to inability to conceive children.

    The senses of sight and hearing are important for communicating, learning, moving about, and performing activities of daily living (ADLs). They also help keep us safe by alerting us to danger. People who are dying are believed to be able to hear up until their death.

    Hearing and vision challenges are common among all age groups. Common causes include conditions present at birth, diseases, and accidents. Some hearing and vision impairment (and loss of other senses) is a natural part of aging. As a support worker, you will care for many clients with hearing and vision disorders. Some clients may have minor disorders that can be corrected with hearing aids and eyeglasses, whereas others may have severe impairments.

    Ear Disorders and Hearing Impairment

    The ear is needed for hearing and balance. Ear disorders range from slight hearing impairments to complete deafness. The most common causes of hearing impairment are aging and prolonged exposure to loud noise. However, hearing impairment can also result from a number of other things, including an accident, illness, exposure to certain drugs or chemicals. In some cases, genetics are responsible for causing impairment at birth.1

    Hearing impairments may occur suddenly, but they are usually gradual in onset. To prevent hearing impairment, there needs to be more public awareness that ear protection should be used when using any loud machinery, even lawn mowers. Of greater concern are the consequences of children, teens, and young adults listening to music that is too loud—for example, blasting the music on car stereos or in their earphones—which can lead to future hearing impairment. Hearing impairment may affect one or both ears. See Chapter 32 for a review of the structure and function of the ear. Ear structures are shown in FIGURE 32.19.

    Common causes of hearing impairment include the following:

    1. • Otitis media —infection (itis) of the middle (media) ear (ot)—is common in infants and children and may be acute or chronic. Normally, the ear’s Eustachian tube (see Chapter 32) allows fresh air to enter the middle ear and fluids to drain from it. However, a cold or flu, large adenoids or tonsils, or an allergy such as hay fever can block the Eustachian tube, causing otitis media.2
    2. In very young children, the symptoms of acute otitis media—irritability, poor appetite, or ear pain—may be thought to be teething or a fever, which is a common effect of immunization. Therefore, it is very easy for otitis media to go undetected in infants and young children.2
    3. In chronic otitis media, the fluid in the middle ear builds up slowly and becomes thick and more difficult to drain. Chronic otitis media can damage the eardrum and the bones of the middle ear (the ossicles), which conduct sound to the inner ear and are essential for hearing. Permanent hearing impairment can result from chronic otitis media.
    4. • Otosclerosis—a condition (osis) in which there is an abnormal formation of new bone (sclero) in the middle ear that gradually immobilizes the stapes (stirrup bone) and prevents it from vibrating in response to sound, causing a progressive loss of hearing. Otosclerosis interferes with the transmission of sound. It is a hereditary condition that is a common cause of hearing impairment and tinnitus (ringing in the ear) in adults. Surgery can often restore some hearing.
    5. • Ménière’s disease—an increase of fluid in the inner ear that causes pressure in the middle ear, resulting in vertigo (dizziness), tinnitus, and hearing impairment. Clients experiencing dizziness must lie down to prevent a fall. Severe dizziness can cause nausea and vomiting. Usually, only one ear is affected.
    6. • Presbycusis—the gradual hearing (cusis) loss associated with aging (presby). It usually occurs after age 50 years. There is no cure, but hearing aids and speech reading (lip reading) are helpful to those persons affected.
    7. • Temporary hearing impairment —the blockage of the ear canal with cerumen (ear wax). Temporary hearing impairment is common in older persons. A buildup of cerumen can lead to a client’s gradual loss of balance. Both hearing and balance may be improved when the cerumen is removed by a physician or a nurse. Always encourage your client to allow the cerumen to remove itself naturally rather than cleaning the auditory canal with cotton swabs, which can cause irritation, increase cerumen production unnecessarily, and potentially perforate the eardrum.

    Effects of Hearing Impairment

    Signs and symptoms of hearing impairment vary and are not always obvious. A person’s behaviour or attitude may change because of a hearing impairment. For example, a person may not respond when others talk or ask questions and may become angry that others are not speaking loudly enough. Family and friends may not be aware of the issue, and they may become frustrated when communicating with the affected person and may avoid talking with that person.

    Some people deny that they have a hearing impairment, as they may not want to believe that they are any different from others or admit to a sign of aging. Some people refuse to get or use a hearing aid.

    The obvious signs of hearing impairment in adults and children include the following:

    1. • Speaking too loudly
    2. • Leaning forward to hear
    3. • Turning and cupping the unaffected ear toward the speaker
    4. • Responding inappropriately
    5. • Asking for words to be repeated

    Hearing impairment can affect all aspects of a person’s health—physical, emotional, social, intellectual, and spiritual.

    Additionally, hearing impairment may cause speech challenges. Not being able to hear one’s own voice affects pronunciation and the volume of voice. Hearing impairment may result in slurred speech and faulty pronunciation. Some people with severe hearing impairment speak in a flat tone and drop word endings, and some cannot speak at all.

    The Canadian Hearing Society is an excellent resource for clients, families, and support workers. It offers information and educational material on hearing impairment as well as aids for people who are hearing impaired.

    Aids for People With Hearing Impairment

    A number of aids help people with a hearing impairment to communicate. These include hearing aids, special telephone systems, signalling devices, and service dogs (see 825–826).

    Hearing Aids

    A hearing aid is a device that fits inside the ear, behind the ear, or in the ear canal and makes sounds louder (FIGURE 34.1). It does not cure the hearing impairment, but it improves a client’s hearing. Since both background noise and speech are made louder, you should help minimize background noise to enable your client to adjust to the hearing aid. Remember that the hearing aid makes speech louder—not clearer.

    Hearing aids can cost up to $5 000 per aid. Dropping a hearing aid can damage it and cause an added expense for your client or their family, so be very careful when handling these devices. Follow your supervisor’s instructions regarding the appropriate care for a hearing aid, and report a missing or damaged hearing aid to your supervisor. Ensure that your client’s name is on the device and its container.

    Modern hearing aids are smaller and less noticeable than older versions, and most can be linked to the wearer’s smartphone so that conversations can be transmitted through the hearing aids. In addition, devices can be connected to the wearer’s television to transmit sounds from the television directly into the wearer’s hearing aids. Some contain rechargeable batteries, allowing the hearing aids to be recharged in a recharging case without having to remove the batteries (see FIGURE 34.1, B).

    Cochlear Implants4

    A cochlear implant is a medical device to improve hearing. It helps people who have a significant hearing impairment and who cannot benefit from regular hearing aids. A cochlear implant has two parts: (1) an implant, which is placed into the inner ear during surgery, and (2) a sound processor, which is worn behind the ear like a hearing aid. The sound processor works alongside the implant and assists in sound transmission to the wearer in the following ways:

    • a. The sound processor picks up and filters sounds.
    • b. The sound processor translates these filtered sounds into digital information and sends it to the internal implant.
    • c. The internal implant converts the digital information into electrical signals and sends them to a tiny, delicate collection of electrodes that sit gently inside the cochlea.
    • d. The electrical signals from the electrodes stimulate the hearing nerve, bypassing the damaged cells that cause hearing impairment, allowing the brain to “hear” sound.

    Both children and adults may be candidates for a cochlear implant. Adults must meet a minimum set of requirements in order to qualify for a cochlear implant:

    1. • They must be 18 years of age or older.
    2. • They must have severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing impairment in both ears.
    3. • They possess little or no speech understanding, even with well-fitted hearing aids.
    4. • They must communicate primarily through spoken language.
    5. • They need to have realistic expectations.
    6. • They need to be strongly motivated and committed to being part of the hearing world.
    7. • They cannot have any medical contraindications to this procedure.

    Children (12 months to 17 years) must also meet a minimum set of requirements in order to qualify for a cochlear implant:

    1. • The child must be 12 months of age or older.
    2. • They must possess severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing impairment.
    3. • They have experienced limited benefit from binaural amplification trial.
    4. • There are no medical contraindications to having a cochlear procedure.
    5. • They must be enrolled in an appropriate educational program with emphasis on development of auditory and oral skills when they are old enough.
    6. • Both the child and family must possess good motivation and realistic expectations about the cochlear implant.

    Auditory Processing Disorders (APDs)1

    Children who are labelled “poor listeners” may have normal hearing abilities but experience a disconnect between what is heard and what is understood—this is called an auditory processing disorder (APD). APD also occurs in the adult population. The difficulty in diagnosing APD is that it is not identified by a routine hearing test. For a diagnosis of APD, advanced testing is required. Just as with children, adults with APD have a brain that interprets what it hears as if there were some delay or distortion in the sound. This makes listening, learning, and memorization very difficult. For persons who believe they have APD and need help, several therapeutic approaches and support groups are available.

    Technology for Hearing Assistance

    Technology is advancing rapidly to provide assistance to clients with hearing impairment or complete hearing impairment.

    1. • Enhanced headphones. These headphones amplify the sound of people’s voices. Some can be used for watching television or listening to music.
    2. • Special telephone systems and cell phone applications.  TTYs (once called telephone teletypes) allow people with hearing impairment to communicate through their landline telephone system. Users type messages back and forth to one another. Amplified telephone handsets make the caller’s voice louder. Extension bells make the telephone ring more loudly. Bluetooth applications in many newer hearing aids allow the client to stream sounds from their television and cell phones directly through their hearing aid.
    3. • Signalling devices. These devices attach to such items as telephones, doorbells, and smoke alarms. When the device makes a sound, a light flashes to alert the person. Some devices include a vibrating option that can be felt.
    4. • Emails. Text messaging through computers and cell phones has improved the ability of clients with hearing impairment to communicate with those who can hear. Speaking computer software programs are also available.
    5. • Closed captioning. Television programs can provide dialogue on the screen that people with hearing impairments can read. Since 1993, all televisions with screens larger than 33 cm (13 inches) have had built-in decoder circuitry that allows clients with a hearing impairment to enjoy the same programs as everyone else, using closed captioning.
    6. • Service dogs. Special dogs are now being trained to alert a person with a hearing impairment to different sounds, such as the ringing phone, the doorbell, an alarm, a baby’s crying, and so on.5

    Caring for Clients With Hearing Impairment

    Some clients with hearing impairment wear hearing aids, while some others have hearing impairment that cannot be improved by the use of hearing aids. Other clients prefer not to wear a hearing aid or feel that they cannot afford the cost of a hearing aid. Some clients learn sign language (FIGURE 34.2), and some have service dogs that alert them to sounds. Other clients speech read by watching facial expressions, gestures, and body language. Follow the guidelines listed in BOX 34.1 on page 820 when caring for clients with severe hearing impairment.

    Some clients who have experienced a gradual loss of hearing may not be fully aware that they no longer hear clearly. Unfortunately, this hearing impairment can affect the client’s ability to communicate comfortably in social situations, leading to feelings of frustration and embarrassment because of having to frequently ask people to repeat themselves. As a result, a client who has experienced gradual hearing loss may begin to avoid socialization with others, increasing their isolation (see the Supporting Mrs. Lopez: The Isolating Effects of Hearing Impairment box on page 820).

    Clients with significant hearing impairment are also at risk for injury if they cannot hear a smoke alarm or the kettle whistling (see Chapter 20). It is the responsibility of all health care team members to ensure that the client remains safe at all times. Remember, some clients with hearing impairment also have a speech or language disorder. Follow the guidelines listed in Chapter 35 for communicating with clients with speech or language disorders.

    Eye Disorders and Vision Impairment

    Eye disorders and vision impairment, ranging from very mild vision impairment to complete vision impairment, can occur at all ages. Health conditions, accidents, and eye diseases are among the causes of vision impairment. The level of vision impairment varies—some people cannot sense light at all and have no vision; others can sense some light but cannot see details; and still others have some vision but cannot see well. Someone who is legally blind is able to see objects at 6 m (20 ft.), whereas a person with normal vision (FIGURE 34.3, A on page 821) can see objects at 60 m (200 ft.). The retina is the inner layer of the eye that senses light and colour. The macula is a small spot in the retina where vision is keenest and where the highest concentration of cones for colour vision are. AMD is associated with aging and is the most common cause of vision impairment in people over 50. In fact, for adults over the age of 55, AMD is the leading cause of blindness in North America.6 The condition starts with gradual or sudden partial loss of vision but is not painful. Central vision in the affected eye gradually becomes blurry. As dry AMD gets worse, a blurred spot is seen in the centre of the eye. Central vision is gradually lost. Usually, both eyes are affected. Dry AMD is more common than wet AMD.

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Clients With Severe Hearing or Vision Impairment

    1. • Dignity. Clients with hearing impairment or vision impairment need patience and respect, not pity. Speak clearly in a normal tone of voice. Do not shout, and do not mumble or use slang terms. Do not talk to the client as you would to a child. Include the client in conversations with others. Never ask another person (such as a family member) to speak for the client. When speaking of a client’s disability, always use the noun form of the disability, not the adjective form—for example, “Mrs. Kabir has a hearing impairment,” not “She is the deaf client.” If a client uses sign language and has an interpreter, speak to the client, not to the interpreter.
    2. • Independence. Remember, clients who are impaired in one area are not impaired in all areas. Do not make assumptions about their abilities or limitations. Clients with hearing impairment or vision impairment should be allowed to do as much for themselves as possible. Tell the client what you are doing step by step. Indicate when the procedure is over. When clients use self-care (assistive) devices to maintain independence, make sure they have access to these needed devices.
    3. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. Clients with hearing or vision challenges may feel isolated and lonely. Plan activities that are best suited to their strengths.
    4. • Preferences. Follow your clients’ choices and directions. Ask how you can help. Assist only if the client wants you to.
    5. • Privacy. Respect your client’s privacy. Always let the client know when you are in the room. If the client cannot hear you knock, make sure the client can see you entering the room. If the client cannot see you, announce your arrival clearly. Also, tell the client if others join you in the room.
    6. • Safety. A safe setting is critical to clients with hearing impairment or vision impairment. Clients with hearing impairment cannot hear sounds that signal danger, and those with vision impairment cannot see obstacles in their path, so they are at great risk for falls. Follow the safety measures described in Chapter 20. Follow the care plan for safety measures specific to each client. In facilities, keep the call bell within easy reach for the client.

    Always tell your client when you are leaving the room. Remember your role and relationship with your clients at all times. It is important to keep information about clients confidential. Communicate in a professional manner.

    Effects of Vision Impairment

    Severe vision impairment affects a client’s physical, emotional, social, intellectual, and spiritual health. For example, Mr. Barak, 78, has AMD. He feels the loss of his independence and self-esteem, as even simple tasks such as dressing take much longer, and he is not able to read the newspaper. He does not go out alone because he is afraid of falling or getting lost, so he no longer attends his mosque. There are many resources available from the Canadian National Institute for the Blind (CNIB) that can help him—for example, talking books, many different types of aids to help with ADLs, technology advances, support for clients and families, devices for entertainment, and medical devices. Computers and computer software are also available for clients with vision impairment.

    Although the process is long and hard, many people with severe vision impairment learn to lead independent lives. They learn to move about, perform ADLs, and read using a different method.

    Some people learn to navigate their world independently with the help of a guide dog or by using a special white cane with a red tip. Both are recognized worldwide as signs that the person using them has vision impairment. The guide dog serves as the eyes of the person with vision impairment, recognizing danger and guiding the person through traffic. Clients are trained by a professional on the use of a white cane, and clients for whom a guide dog is appropriate will attend special training sessions.

    Vision Aids and Corrective Measures

    Eyeglasses

    Eyeglasses correct vision impairment and are worn for reading or seeing objects at a distance, or both. Some people wear them all the time while awake. Some people feel upset when they first realize they need glasses, but adjustment is usually rapid. Most accept the fact that glasses will be needed as they grow older.

    Eyeglasses are costly, so protect the client’s glasses from damage. When not worn, they should be kept in their case. Glasses should be labelled with your client’s name. Lenses are made of hardened glass or plastic to avoid breakage.

    Glasses must be kept clean in order to be of assistance to the wearer. Glass lenses are washed with warm water and dried with soft tissue. Plastic lenses are easily scratched, so special cleaning solutions, tissues, and cloths are used to clean and dry them.

    Remove eyeglasses by holding the part of the frames sitting on both ears. Lift the frames off the ears, and bring the glasses down and away from the client’s face. Avoid touching the lenses or placing glasses down on their lenses.

    Contact Lenses

    Contact lenses (contacts)—hard or soft—fit directly on the eye. Many people prefer contacts because they cannot be seen, do not break easily, and can be worn even while playing sports. The disadvantage is that contacts can be easily lost. Depending on the type of lens, contacts can be worn continuously for 12 to 24 hours or even for 1 week. Contacts are usually removed for swimming and sleeping.

    Remove and clean clients’ contact lenses according to the manufacturer’s instructions and your employer policy. Report any eye redness or drainage to your supervisor. Also, report client complaints of eye pain or blurred vision. Ensure that you use gloves to decrease the risk of pathogen spread.

    Laser surgery is used to correct a number of different vision conditions. Not all people are suited for laser vision correction, and suitability must be determined by the eye surgeon who specializes in this procedure.

    Intra-Ocular Lens Implants

    Cataract surgery, as mentioned, involves replacing the eye’s natural lens with an artificial lens. The intra-ocular lens implant used in this surgery is a clear hard plastic, soft plastic, or silicone disc about the size of a small button that is usually implanted behind the iris.     Aids for Reading

    Many people with vision impairment learn to read Braille—a writing system that uses raised dots in a certain pattern to represent each letter of the alphabet. The first 10 letters also represent numbers 0 to 9 (FIGURE 34.4). The person uses their fingers to feel the pattern of dots and determine the letter. Numerous books, magazines, newspapers, and computer keyboards are available in Braille. Braille is hard to learn, especially for older persons, so some people with vision impairment never learn to read Braille. Braille is also available in elevators, subways, bus stops, and other means of transportation to assist passengers with vision impairment.

    Other reading aids include books with large print, audiobooks, and magnifiers (some with reading lamps attached to them).

    Communication Aids

    Examples of communication aids are calendars in Braille, large-print clocks that voice the time in hours or minutes, telephones with Braille keypads and extra-large numerals, and cheque- and envelope-writing guides. Computer software is continually being developed to assist people with visual impairment.

    Devices for Entertainment

    Examples of devices for entertainment are televisions with descriptive captions for movies and shows (usually available through the network provider), as well as playing cards and bingo cards with large numbers or in Braille.

    Medical Devices

    Examples of medical devices for people with visual impairment include pillboxes with the days or times written in Braille that let the person feel if the pill has been taken and “talking prescription devices” that provide voice messages describing how to take the medication.

    There are important points to remember while providing support to a client who has a service dog:10

    Key Points

    • Common causes of hearing impairment include otitis media, otosclerosis, Ménière’s disease, dominant progressive hearing loss, presbycusis, and temporary hearing impairment.

    • The effects of hearing impairment will vary from client to client. Some clients will blame others for not speaking clearly or loud enough for them to hear. Others may withdraw in embarrassment because they are not able to hear conversations.

    • There are a number of aids to help people with a hearing impairment, including hearing aids, special telephone systems, signalling devices, text messaging, service dogs, and closed captioning on TVs.

    • Guidelines to providing care to a client with hearing impairment are as follows: (1) Alert the client to your presence; (2) adjust the lighting; (3) reduce background noise; (4) focus your attention on your client; (5) speak in a normal tone; (6) check communication aids; (7) adjust your language; (8) use other communication methods; and (9) watch for signs of fatigue in the client.

    • Vision impairment can range from mild to complete blindness. Common causes are (1) age-related macular degeneration, (2) retinal detachment, (3) diabetic retinopathy, (4) glaucoma, (5) cataract, and (6) presbyopia.

    • The effects of vision impairment depend on the age of the person and when the symptoms started.

    • The aids available for a client with vision impairment include (1) eyeglasses, (2) Braille books, (3) books with large print, (4) audiobooks, (5) magnifiers, and (6) large-print clocks, and more aids are in development.

    • When caring for clients with vision impairment, (1) adjust the lighting; (2) alert the client to your presence; (3) assist with walking when required; and (4) assist with eating if required.

    • There are specially trained service dogs that can assist people who have hearing challenges, while other service dogs can assist clients who have vision challenges.

    Imagine what life would be like if you were unable to speak or communicate! That is a daily reality for people who have speech and language disorders. Speech and language disorders involve difficulty speaking, understanding, reading, or writing, and unfortunately, they can occur at any age.

    Chapter 9 discussed effective communication between clients who have no physical communication disorder. This chapter builds on the information presented in Chapter 9 and applies it to the process of communicating effectively with clients who have speech and language disorders. It should be noted that clients of all ages may have speech and language disorders. Some causes of communication difficulties include hearing impairment (see Chapter 34), neurological disorders and brain injury (see Chapter 33), vocal cord injury, autism, intellectual disability, emotional or psychiatric disorders or drug abuse (see Chapter 37), physical impairments such as cleft lip or palate, and developmental disorders (see Chapter 36). They should not be confused with the difficulty that clients who speak another language may have with understanding English.

    Clients with speech or language disorders should always have a thorough health history taken, usually by a doctor or a nurse, as soon as possible before treatment begins. The client’s history will provide details that can help the health care team better understand the health challenges the client has faced.

    Aphasia

    Aphasia is the partial or complete loss (a) of speech and language skills (phasia), whether it is understanding the words or trying to say them correctly. It is caused by brain injury. Stroke (also called cerebral vascular accident, or CVA), head trauma, and brain tumours are the most common causes of aphasia. Some clients with aphasia regain some or all of their language skills, but in some, aphasia is permanent. For example, clients with dementia who develop aphasia do not typically regain verbal communication skills (see Chapter 38).

    For communication to occur, a message must be sent, received, and interpreted. Clients with aphasia are unable to send messages, unable to understand the message received, or both.

    Types of Aphasia

    There are three basic types of aphasia:

      1. Receptive aphasia—difficulty understanding language, including both spoken and written words. Clients with receptive aphasia cannot understand their own words or others’ words, so their speech is mixed up or muddled. They also mix up sounds within words. A client trying to say “hospital,” for example, may say “posital.”
      1. Expressive aphasia—difficulty speaking or writing. Clients with expressive aphasia can understand spoken and written words, but their speech or written language is jumbled or slurred and difficult to understand. They think one thing but say or write another. For example, a client may want food but ask for a newspaper. Clients with expressive aphasia cannot think of the right words or put the right sounds together to form words and sentences. For example, a client trying to say, “Please give me a glass of water” may actually say “Come. Come. Come. Come.” Some may leave out connecting words. Others can produce only meaningless sounds or syllables. Because clients with expressive aphasia are very aware of their mistakes since they can understand what they are saying, they may become frustrated or depressed, leading them to cry or swear for no apparent reason.
      1. Expressive–receptive aphasia (also known as global aphasia)—difficulty speaking and understanding written and spoken language. Some clients with expressive–receptive aphasia can say only “yes” and “no” and make sounds such as “da da.” Some others have lost all speech and language skills.

    Apraxia of Speech

    Apraxia of speech (verbal apraxia) is the inability (a) to correctly move (praxia) the muscles used to speak, even though the muscles themselves are intact. Clients with this disorder are not able to control their lip, jaw, or tongue movements. As a result, they are not able to say the desired sounds and words. Apraxia of speech is caused by brain injury resulting from stroke, accident, brain tumour, or infection. Some babies are born with this disorder. Apraxia of speech can occur alone or with aphasia.

    Understanding the speech of clients with apraxia of speech is difficult, as they usually speak very slowly and may use words that sound similar to but not be exactly what they are trying to say. For example, a client may say “me” instead of “see.” As well, the order of sounds within words may be mixed up. For example, a client may say “thootshub” instead of “toothbrush.” Some clients have difficulty putting words in the right order or finding the right words. Inconsistent speech is common—that is, a person may say something correctly one time and incorrectly another time.

    Dysarthria

    Dysarthria is difficulty (dys) speaking clearly (arthria) because of weakness or paralysis in the muscles used for speech. Common causes of dysarthria include cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, head injury, tumour, and infection.

    Clients with dysarthria usually have slurred, slow, and soft speech and speak in flat, harsh, or nasal tones. They often have difficulty forming words, spacing their words, and breathing while speaking. Speech errors are usually consistent and predictable, so as you work with a client with dysarthria you may become familiar with their speech. (See TABLE 35.1: A Comparison Between Aphasia, Apraxia, and Dysarthria).

    Emotional Effects of Speech and Language Disorders

    How would you feel if you were not able to express your thoughts and feelings? How would you feel if you were not able to understand what others are saying? Clients with speech and language disorders experience emotions such as frustration, depression, and anger, as well as low self-esteem, shame, and guilt. BOX 35.1 contains comments from clients who have had aphasia.

    Communication is important for functioning and for maintaining relationships with others. Being unable to communicate may cause a client to avoid social situations, or family and friends may avoid visiting the client instead. Speech and language disorders can be very stressful for families, as the relations between all family members are usually affected.

    Many of us often find sharing our thoughts and feelings with others difficult. It is even more difficult for someone who has a speech or language disorder since even everyday conversations require great effort. Clients with speech and language disorders may speak slowly, may struggle with words, or may mispronounce words frequently. These communication difficulties might be frustrating for both the affected person and the listener, who may have to struggle to understand. Family members may find themselves trying to correct the person or becoming irritated with the person if the same word or phrase is repeatedly mispronounced. The person who cannot speak clearly might be tempted to avoid speaking.

    Clients with speech or language disorders may have difficulties communicating at school or not be able to work, so they may suffer the added stress of financial concerns. For some adults, even routine tasks like shopping, cooking, paying bills, and doing household repairs may be impossible, so they are forced to depend on others to do what they used to be able to do themselves.

    Treatment for Speech and Language Disorders

    A speech therapist (also called a speech–language pathologist) helps the client with a speech disorder learn to communicate and also helps family members learn new communication techniques. Methods used depend on the age of the client, their disorder, its cause, and its severity. Practice and exercises may help the client learn (or relearn) speech and language skills. In the case of dysarthria, the client practises muscle-strengthening exercises and learns how to breathe while speaking.

    Clients with speech and language disorders also learn how to improve existing skills. For example, a client learns to use body language and facial expressions to make communication more effective. Some clients learn new skills, such as sign language.

    Those with severe speech and language challenges may never regain their ability to speak and understand language. These clients may be helped by the following aids:

    1. • Communication boards. These are boards with pictures or words that show functions or tasks (FIGURE 35.1). There are pictures or words related to activities of daily living, such as sleep, food, drink, medicine, and eyeglasses. The client points to the things that are needed. The type of communication board depends on the client’s needs. For those who can read, words rather than pictures are often used. For those with quadriplegia who are not able to speak or point, eye-gaze boards may be used. The client indicates their needs by gazing at the picture or word on the board and either blinking or using another signal to accept it.
    2. • Mechanical and electronic devices. These devices range in complexity and cost—from large computers that cannot be moved easily from place to place to handheld devices such as electronic talking aids (FIGURES 35.2 and 35.3). The client touches a picture displayed on the screen, and the message is then voiced by the device. For example, the client touches a picture of a sad face. A recorded message says, “I am sad.” The message may also be printed on a screen, and some devices convert words into pictures.

    Supporting and Communicating with Clients

    As a support worker, to effectively communicate with your clients who have speech and language disorders, you must first know how you communicate in general. Do you speak clearly, or do you mumble? Do you use gestures? With these clients in particular, you may have to change the way you speak. Follow the care plan and your supervisor’s instructions. Use the communication methods that are best for your client. While you are speaking, you need to remember to be mindful of your facial expressions, as they can reflect any impatience or frustration you feel with the client.

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Communicating With Clients Who Have Speech and Language Disorders

    1. • Dignity. Showing respect, warmth, and compassion are important ways to promote dignity. Never talk about a client as if the person were not present. Address questions for and comments about the client to the client, not to others who are present. Some clients with language disorders are embarrassed to speak in front of others, especially strangers, so do not force a client to talk in front of others (see the Supporting Mr. Hamilton, Part 1: Communicating With Clients Who Have Expressive Aphasia box).
    2. • Independence. Clients with speech and language disorders must gain, regain, or maintain independence and control. Support, encouragement, and patience are important. Never show impatience, frustration, or worry when a client is having difficulty speaking or understanding. Do not talk down to the client. (The Supporting Mr. Hamilton, Part 2: Communicating With Clients Who Have Expressive Aphasia box describes a support worker patiently using body language and symbols to learn about a client’s needs.) The care plan may list ways to encourage communication.
    3. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. Post ways to communicate effectively with the client so that they can be easily referred to. Avoid situations that force the client to embarrass themselves when attempting to communicate.
    4. • Preferences. Limit the number of choices to help the client express preferences without difficulty. Be encouraging and supportive. A useful tip is to write down the available choices beforehand so that the client can pick one easily.
    5. • Privacy. Keep information about your client confidential. Remember your role and relationship with the client at all times. Be professional in your communication. Learn to be comfortable with silence. Do not feel that you need to talk when the client is silent.
    6. • Safety. Take extra time to explain procedures. Do not explain all the steps at once. Explain the step just before you do it. Avoid using medical terms. Speak clearly and slowly. Be alert for signs that the client has not understood you.

    BOX 35.2 Guidelines for Communicating With Clients Who Have Speech and Language Disorders

    1. • Minimize distractions. Distractions make concentration difficult and can also upset the client. Reduce background noise and activity. Close doors and windows. Turn off the television and radio (if the client agrees), and keep cell phones on silent mode. Make sure there is nothing in your hands or on your lap.
    2. • Adjust the lighting. Make sure the client can see your face clearly and that you can see the client’s face. Turn down lights and adjust window coverings to reduce glare. Do not position yourself between the client and a window. The client may not be able to see you in the glare.
    3. • Give the client your full attention. Sit close by and face the client. Look directly into the client’s eyes so that the client can look at your face. Get the client’s attention before you speak. Show that you are listening by using gestures and facial expressions. Do not perform other tasks while you are talking to the client.
    4. • In the beginning of your work relationship with the client, you can start by asking questions to which you know the answer. Doing so helps you become familiar with the client’s speech.
    5. • Determine the subject being discussed. Knowing the topic helps you understand the main points. Look for nonverbal clues. Do the client’s gestures, eyes, and body language tell you anything? Does the client use words or partial words that might describe an item or concept?
    6. • Follow the client’s lead. Alter your communication method, as needed. For example, Mrs. Schmidt becomes frustrated when you ask her questions. You learn that she responds better when you use gestures to demonstrate your question. If Mrs. Schmidt seems tired or has lost interest, allow her to rest. Bring up the subject at a later time.
    7. • Speak slowly, clearly, and in a normal tone of voice. Adjust your speech to suit the client’s needs. Talk to adults in a mature, professional manner. Do not use slang or figures of speech. Some clients may not understand them.
    8. • Give the client time to respond. Do not rush the client. Pause between sentences to allow the client time to think and respond. Do not answer questions, including your own questions and those from others, when they are addressed to the client.
    9. • Use simple words and short sentences. Avoid long, complex sentences. Focus on key words—mostly action words and words for clients, places, or things. For example, say “Let’s go for lunch” instead of “It’s time for me to transfer you to the dining room for lunch.”
    10. • Be patient. Repeat your words, as needed. Rephrase your sentences if the client does not understand you.
    11. • Use positive statements. Positive statements are easier to understand than negative statements. For example, say “Bend your arm” instead of “Don’t straighten your arm.”
    12. • Use appropriate questioning and paraphrasing techniques. Ask questions that require only a short answer or a shake of the head. Paraphrase (summarize in your own words) what the client has said. Ask the client if you have understood correctly (see Chapter 9).
    13. • Provide cues, as needed. Help clients express themselves by giving them cues about a word they cannot recall. If it is an object, ask, “What does it look like?” “What is it used for?” and “Where is it found?” Encourage the use of gestures and pointing. If you think you understand what the client says, verify your assumption by asking if this is what they mean. If the client’s gestures or words suggest you have misunderstood, encourage the client again to use gestures, pointing, or symbols. For example, Mrs. Lin points to her sweater. You get it for her, and she puts it on. You have understood her need.
    14. • Try other communication methods. Some clients may write better than they speak. Follow the care plan. Use writing and communication boards, as needed.
    15. • Pay attention to your own facial expressions and nonverbal cues. Your facial expressions can reveal if you are frustrated or impatient, and your client could easily detect this.

    3. • Learning
    4. • Mobility (i.e., getting around independently)
    5. • Self-direction (e.g., solving problems, making choices and decisions)

    This chapter will discuss the causes of the most common developmental disorders and disabilities and how they affect an individual’s functioning. As with all other clients, it is important to understand that while one client with a developmental disorder may demonstrate certain abilities or require certain types of care, another client with the same type of developmental disorder may be very different. Most clients with developmental disorders and disabilities need lifelong assistance, support, and special services, which are provided by the health care team involved in the client’s care.

    Caring for a Client Who is Living with a Developmental Disability or Disorder

    Clients who are living with developmental disorders and disabilities often have complex care needs, especially if their disabilities are severe. As a support worker, you must be familiar with special equipment and self-help devices needed for each client. Follow the client’s care plan and consult with your supervisor if you have questions. For example, Gordon, age 10, has severe cerebral palsy. He cannot walk and has limited use of his hands. He tends to slide down in his wheelchair, so a postural support is used to help keep him in good body alignment. He must be repositioned frequently to prevent pressure ulcers from developing. Gordon also has a hearing impairment and a speech disorder; he wears hearing aids in both ears and uses a computer to communicate. Gordon has splints on his ankles to prevent contractures. He uses a special spoon strapped to his wrist to help him eat.

    Clients living with developmental disorders or disabilities have the same rights and needs as everyone else. As always, your priority when caring for these clients is to promote their dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, and safety (see the Think About Safety: Caring for Clients Who Are Living With Developmental Disorders and Disabilities box and the Providing Compassionate Care: Caring for Clients Living With Developmental Disorders and Disabilities box, on page 853.)

    As a support worker, you can help a child with a developmental disorder or disability and the child’s family in the following ways:

    1. • Encourage the family to maintain a consistent daily schedule.
    2. • Assist the client in maintaining a regular bedtime.
    3. • Use lists, charts, or memory aids to remind the client about important duties, dates, and times.
    4. • Encourage regular physical activity.
    5. • Encourage structured social activities, such as sports or dancing. Such activities can also improve the child’s social strengths and self-esteem.
    6. • Suggest that the client study in a quiet area, away from noisy distractions.
    7. • Suggest counselling, which can assist the client and family in coping and help the client learn how to adapt to their environment.
    8. • Encourage the client to take medication at the same time every day and to report any adverse effects to the client’s health care provider immediately.
    9. • Encourage the client to eat a nutritionally balanced diet and to follow Canada’s Dietary Guidelines (see Chapter 27).

    Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorders (ADHD)

    Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) causes inappropriate levels of inattention, as well as hyperactive and impulsive behaviour. ADHD is one of the most common childhood disorders, along with attention-deficit disorder (ADD). The two disorders are grouped together within the same category because of the similarities between symptoms. Approximately 3 to 7% of school-aged children have ADHD, and some research indicates that larger cities may have rates as high as 10 to 15%.1 The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5), states that ADHD is two times more common in boys than in girls,2 although some researchers suggest that girls with ADHD may be overlooked because they tend to be more inattentive than hyperactive. A child is more likely to develop ADHD if a close biological family member also has it. Children with learning or communication challenges are also more likely to have ADHD.3

    Autism Spectrum Disorder

    Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that impairs a person’s ability to communicate and interact with others. Autos means “self,” and the client with ASD withdraws into the self. It may seem as if some clients with ASD are in their own world and uninterested in others. For example, some clients with ASD may not notice when someone enters a room, whereas others may prefer to be alone. Some individuals avoid physical contact and become very upset when touched.

    ASD begins in early childhood—between the ages of 18 months and 3 years. In Canada, about 1 child in 94 is diagnosed with ASD.5 It is one of the most common brain disorders affecting children; boys are affected more often than girls.6

    ASD consists of a number of related complex neurobiological conditions that can affect the normal functioning of the gastrointestinal, immune, hepatic, endocrine, and nervous systems. It can impact normal brain development, leaving individuals with symptoms such as communication challenges, decreased interest in various activities, reduced willingness to engage in activities, difficulty with typical social interactions, and a tendency to repeat specific patterns of behaviour. Both verbal and nonverbal communication are affected, and some individuals are unable to understand the facial expressions of others. This disorder may also affect the development of fine and gross motor ability in some clients.

    ASD can range from mild (e.g., an individual with Asperger’s syndrome who has difficulties with social function but who otherwise functions well intellectually and at work) to severe (e.g., a child with severe autism who is developmentally delayed and who is unable to function independently). The following symptoms are common:

    1. • Develops language skills slowly, if at all
    2. • Repeats words or phrases
    3. • Cannot start or maintain conversations
    4. • Repeats body movements (hand flapping, finger flicking, rocking)
    5. • Has a short attention span
    6. • Avoids eye contact
    7. • Spends time alone
    8. • Shows little reaction to pain
    9. • Dislikes cuddling
    10. • Has frequent meltdowns
    11. • Has strong attachment to a single item, idea, activity, or person
    12. • Needs routines; dislikes change
    13. • Lacks fear
    14. • Does not respond to others
    15. • Is too active or too quiet
    16. • Displays aggressive or violent behaviour
    17. • Tends to injure self

    Since clients on the autism spectrum may have varying degrees and combinations of symptoms, treatment needs to be specific to the individual. Every client is very different.

    Supporting a Client Who Is Living With Autism Spectrum Disorder

    Care depends on the severity of the ASD and the needs of the client. The goal is for the client to be as independent as possible. Support workers caring for children or adults with ASD should remember the following:

    1. • Maintain strict routines. The client may become very upset if the routine is disrupted. Follow the client’s routine whenever possible. Warn the client if the routine must be changed.
    2. • The client needs to develop social and work skills. Some adults with ASD work and live independently, but some need support and help from family and community services. Some may live in group homes or residential care facilities.
    3. • Children with ASD require careful supervision. Some individuals should never be left alone, to ensure their safety. Follow the client’s care plan for supervision for a client with autism. Some clients, especially children, will need constant supervision, as they are not aware of dangerous situations in the home or can walk out of the home at any time and not be aware of any dangers on the streets; they also may not know how to get home.

    There is no cure for ASD, but with therapy, clients may learn to change or control behaviours. Behaviour modification—rewarding positive behaviours and correcting negative behaviours—is used to encourage social behaviours. Speech and language therapy is encouraged to assist the client in improving communication skills. Music, auditory, recreation, and sensory therapies may also assist the client.

    Cerebral Palsy

    Cerebral palsy (CP), a disorder affecting muscle control (palsy), is caused by an injury or abnormality in the motor region of the brain (cerebral). Depending on which area or areas of the brain have been injured, one or more of the following may occur:

    1. • Involuntary movements
    2. • Poor coordination and posture
    3. • Muscle weakness
    4. • Difficulty or inability to walk
    5. • Difficulty or inability to speak

    CP may occur before, during, or shortly after birth. Lack of oxygen to the fetal or newborn brain is the usual cause. Infants at risk include the following:

    1. • Those born prematurely
    2. • Those with a low birth weight
    3. • Those who do not cry in the first 5 minutes after birth
    4. • Those who need mechanical ventilation
    5. • Those who have bleeding in the brain
    6. • Those with heart, kidney, or spinal cord abnormalities
    7. • Those with blood disorders
    8. • Those who have seizures

    Acquired brain injury in infancy or early childhood also can result in CP (see Chapter 37 and TABLE 36.1, on page 842).

    Certain body movements and body parts are affected by CP. The following types of CP are the most common:

    1. • Spastic cerebral palsy.  Spastic means uncontrolled contractions of skeletal muscles (spastic comes from the Greek spastikos, meaning “to draw in”). Muscles contract or shorten and remain stiff and unable to relax. One or both sides of the body may be involved, so posture and balance are affected, and movement is stiff and jerky. If the client’s arms are affected, they may have difficulty eating, writing, dressing, and doing other activities of daily living (ADLs). If the client’s legs are affected, they may have difficulty walking or moving. The spastic form is the most common type of CP (about 50% of all cases).
    2. • Athetoid cerebral palsy. The client cannot control body movements (athetoid comes from the Greek athetos, meaning “not fixed”). The client has involuntary, constant, slow-weaving, or writhing motions that occur in the trunk, arms, hands, legs, and feet. The client might have difficulty reaching for and grasping objects and remaining upright for sitting or standing. Sometimes, the muscles of the tongue, face, and neck may be affected, causing the client to drool or grimace.
    3. • Ataxic cerebral palsy. The client has weak muscle tone and difficulty coordinating movements (absence of [a] arrangement or order [taxis]), so the person appears very unsteady and shaky when walking and has trouble maintaining balance.

    The following terms are used to describe the types of CP according to the body parts affected:

    1. • Monoplegia— mono means “one.” This type of CP is a paralysis of a single limb, usually an arm.
    2. • Hemiplegia —hemi means “half”; plegia means “complete or partial loss of ability to move.” CP affects one side of the body—the right arm and leg or the left arm and leg. The other side functions normally. The client may be able to walk but might look a little awkward.
    3. • Diplegia —di means “two.” With diplegia, there is a loss of ability to move (plegia) corresponding parts on both (di) sides of the body. In most cases of diplegia caused by CP, both legs are affected—so the client has difficulty walking—but the upper body is not affected. (In extremely rare cases, both arms are affected, but not the legs.)
    4. • Quadriplegia —quad means “four.” CP affects all four limbs (both arms and both legs). The client cannot walk or use their arms. Usually, the client also has difficulty with movement of the facial muscles and the trunk. Talking and eating may be difficult, and the client needs a wheelchair to get around.

    Some clients with CP are only mildly affected. Their movements are awkward, but they can walk independently, and they do not have intellectual disability. However, other clients with CP are severely affected and can have many other accompanying impairments and challenges, including the following:

    1. • Intellectual disability
    2. • Learning disability
    3. • Hearing impairment
    4. • Speech impairment
    5. • Vision impairment
    6. • Difficulty swallowing
    7. • Drooling because of difficulty swallowing saliva
    8. • Bladder and bowel control issues
    9. • Seizures
    10. • ADHD (short attention span, poor concentration, and increased activity)
    11. • Breathing difficulties due to poor posture
    12. • Pressure injuries from immobility

    Supporting a Client Who Is Living With Cerebral Palsy

    Care depends on the severity of the CP and the needs of the client. The goal is for the client to be as independent as possible. As a support worker, you may be required to assist the client with CP with range-of-motion (ROM) exercises and ADLs and to reduce the client’s risk of injury. Other supports may come from physio-, occupational, and speech therapy teams as well as the use of eyeglasses or hearing aids. Depending on the client’s needs, surgery or medications to reduce muscle challenges may also be ordered.

    Epilepsy

    Epilepsy is a condition characterized by recurrent (occurring repeatedly from time to time) seizures (epilepsia means “seizure”). A seizure is a brief disturbance in the brain’s normal electrical function, which affects awareness, movement, and sensation.

    Seizures that affect only one part of the brain are called partial seizures. Seizures that affect the whole brain are called generalized seizures. The area of the brain affected by the seizure temporarily loses its ability to function normally, so seizures may cause different reactions, depending on the part of the brain affected. For example, some seizures cause the person to briefly stare and appear unresponsive; others (as in tonic–clonic seizures) involve a violent and sudden contraction or tremor of muscle groups. The person loses consciousness and falls to the floor. All muscle groups contract and relax, causing jerking and twitching movements of the body. Urinary and bowel incontinence may occur. A tonic–clonic seizure usually lasts for 1 to 7 minutes.

    A single seizure does not mean that the person has epilepsy, since a number of factors can cause a single seizure. For a diagnosis of epilepsy, the person must have recurring seizures. The electrical system in the brain is permanently damaged, making it susceptible to seizures. In severe cases, epilepsy may limit the activities of those affected. For example, a person who has frequent seizures may not be allowed to drive, which may limit job choices and opportunities.

    Children and young adults are commonly affected, but epilepsy can develop at any time during a person’s life. Often, the cause of epilepsy in a particular person is not known. It can be caused by any condition affecting the brain—for example, CP, intellectual disability, ASD, Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, tumours, and acquired brain injury (see TABLE 36.1, on page 842).

    There is no cure for epilepsy; medications are prescribed by a physician to try to prevent seizures. These medications may control seizures in many people but may not work in others, so some individuals may need brain surgery to reduce the frequency of seizures. When controlled, epilepsy usually does not affect learning and the ability to perform ADLs. Clients with epilepsy have an increased risk for premature death. They also have higher rates of suicide; sudden, unexplained death; or accidental death, especially drowning.

    Supporting a Client Who Is Living With Epilepsy

    Care for a client with epilepsy depends on the severity of the epilepsy and the needs of the client. The goal is for the client to be as independent as possible.

    1. • As a support worker, you must observe and report all activity before, during, and after a seizure. Call for help immediately if needed.
    2. • Clients with frequent or unpredictable seizures may injure themselves if they fall. Safety measures are thus required, whether the client is at home, in the workplace, taking transportation, or engaging in recreation activities.
    3. • Close supervision is required at all times when the client is swimming, boating, or in a hot tub, to prevent drowning.
    4. • If your client has a seizure, ensure that there is nothing within reach that could harm the client. Do not hold the client down; keep track of the duration of the seizure and the parts of the body affected. If the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, call the emergency medical service in your area. After the seizure has stopped, place the client on their left side in case the client vomits; stay with the client until they recover.

    Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASD)

    Fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD) are a group of physical and mental abnormalities that occur in a child as a result of alcohol consumption by the birth parent during pregnancy. In Canada, one child is born with FASD every day. It is the most common preventable developmental disorder.

    Physical challenges include low birth weight, weak muscle tone, and poor weight gain. Cardiac (heart) disorders, hearing impairment, and abnormalities of the spine and joints may also occur. The client may have characteristic facial features, including an abnormally small head, small eye openings, a thin upper lip, and a small chin.

    A child with FASD may have not have any outward signs of a disability, but may have an intellectual disability or other behavioural, learning, and emotional disorders, including poor attention span, hyperactivity, poor motor skills, and slow language development. Older children and adults with FASD may often have memory impairment, poor judgement, difficulties with ADLs, inability to manage anger, poor social skills, and mental health challenges. Some adults with FASD work and live independently, but others will need support and help from family and community services. Women with FASD can have a normal baby as long as they do not consume alcohol during pregnancy.

    Supporting a Client Living with Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders

    FASD is a lifelong condition. Care depends on the severity of the FASD and the needs of the client. The goal is for the client to be as independent as possible. With appropriate interventions and support, people living with FASD can be successful in many areas of their lives. It is most helpful to intervene as early as possible and get a diagnosis. You can support clients living with FASD by:

    1. • Encouraging self-esteem. Focus on what the client can do and not on what they cannot do.
    2. • Encouraging self-awareness. The client may overreact to normal everyday stresses or may feel “different” than other people. Encouraging self-awareness can be very empowering for the client living with FASD.
    3. • Promoting independence by encouraging daily functioning like cooking and hygiene.
    4. • Encouraging adaptability, like learning how to cope with new situations.
    5. • Providing names of programs and supports that are available for people living with FASD. The Government of Canada website has a list of links (see https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/diseases/fetal-alcohol-spectrum-disorder/support.html).

    Intellectual Disabilities

    An intellectual disability (cognitive disability) is an impaired ability to learn and results in below-average intelligence and limitations in the ability to function in certain areas of daily life. (Intelligence relates to learning, thinking, and reasoning.) The client can learn new skills but does so at a slower than normal rate. A client with an intellectual disability often has difficulties with communication, self-care, and social interaction. In the past, intellectual disability was called mental retardation, a term now considered unacceptable in Canada.

    An intellectual disability can be caused by any genetic abnormality, injury, or disease that impairs the development of the brain. Some common causes of intellectual disabilities are listed in BOX 36.1 on page 849. People with an intellectual disability often have other disabilities as well.

    Intellectual disabilities range from mild to severe. Tests that measure intelligence are called IQ (intelligence quotient) tests. An average person without an intellectual disability has an IQ score of about 90 to 110. People with IQ scores between 55 and 70 are considered to have mild intellectual disability. They may learn slowly but are able to attend regular schools. As adults, they can function in society with some support. They can work and live in the community and need only occasional support. People who have IQ scores below 55 are considered to have moderate intellectual disability and need daily support at home and at work. People with IQ scores below 25 are considered to have severe intellectual disability and need constant support in all areas.

    The Canadian Association for Community Living is a national association dedicated to serving people with intellectual disabilities and their families. The association’s goal is to ensure that people with intellectual disabilities have opportunities to live meaningful, dignified lives. The association’s philosophy is that people with intellectual disabilities can and should be allowed to participate in all aspects of community living. Children should live in families, be integrated into regular schools whenever possible, and interact with other children with or without disabilities. Adults with intellectual disabilities have the right to control their lives to the fullest extent possible—that is, they should make choices and decisions about their care and how they live. They should have friends, work at jobs, enjoy adult activities, and contribute to their communities.

    2. • Creating their own life plans and to try and reach their goals. This is called person-directed planning.
    3. • Taking part in community classes or recreational programs. In this way, the client can maintain social skills and physical health.
    4. • Hiring a support worker for their own care. In this way, the client is able to advocate for their own care and respite is provided for their family or caregivers.

    Spina Bifida

    Spina bifida is a congenital disorder caused by improper closing of the spine before birth (spina means “backbone,” and bifida means “split in two parts”). Spina bifida is a neural tube defect (NTD) that can range from mild to severe. With NTDs, the brain, spinal cord, and the protective coverings for these organs do not fully develop. These defects occur during the first months of pregnancy. Consuming sufficient folic acid before conception and during early pregnancy greatly reduces the risk of having a baby with NTDs (see Chapter 27).

    Bones of the spinal column (vertebrae) protect the spinal cord. In spina bifida, the vertebrae do not close properly, leaving the spinal cord unprotected. The spinal cord contains nerves that send messages to and from the brain, and because in spina bifida the spinal cord is unprotected, nerve damage occurs. Affected body parts do not function properly, and partial or complete paralysis and loss of bowel and bladder control may result. Infection of the exposed spinal cord is also a risk.

    Spina bifida can occur anywhere in the spine. The lower back is the most common site. Types of spina bifida include the following:

    1. • Spina bifida occulta —(occult means “hidden”). This type is the mildest form of spina bifida. A slight deficiency occurs in the vertebral closure. However, the spinal cord and the membranes (meninges) that cover it remain in place. Skin usually covers the defect; in other words, the defect is hidden. The person may have a dimple or tuft of hair on the back (FIGURE 36.2); however, the spinal cord and nerves are normal. It rarely causes disability, and often there are no symptoms. Foot weakness as well as bowel and bladder disorders may occur.

Loss of function occurs below the level of damage. Leg paralysis, lack of sensation, and lack of bowel and bladder control are common. The baby’s back is closed with surgery, usually soon after birth. Some children and adults with this form of spina bifida walk with braces or crutches, but some will need wheelchairs.

    Clients with spina bifida may have other disorders or conditions—for example, those related to learning, attention, language, reading, and math. They are at risk for obesity, gastrointestinal disorders, and mobility issues. Skin breakdown, depression, and social, emotional, and sexual issues are other risks. Hydrocephalus (see TABLE 36.1 on page 842) often occurs with certain types of spina bifida.

    Supporting a Client Who Is Living With Spina Bifida

    Care depends on the type of spina bifida and the needs of the client. The goal is for the client to be as independent as possible.

    1. • There is no known cure for the nerve damage caused by spina bifida. The standard method of treatment following birth is surgery to prevent further damage from occurring and to prevent infections.
    2. • When caring for a client with spina bifida cystica, be careful when working around the sac, as the tissue can be very fragile. Injury can lead to infection.
    3. • Clients who live with spina bifida may develop health challenges, including reduced or inability to walk, infection of their sac, and bladder and bowel issues. The goal of support care is to prevent these from occurring or reducing the extent of these health challenges.9
    4. • Depending on the severity, ongoing treatments and surgeries may be required from a collaborative team of psychologists, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, urologists, neurosurgeons, and orthopedists.9

    The Impact of Developmental Disorders and Disabilities on the Family

    Families of children who live with developmental disorders and disabilities may face many challenges. As the support worker, you need to be sensitive to the family’s situation. Some family members you work with may be adjusting to a recent diagnosis of a disability in a child in their family. They may still be figuring out the new roles and routines for everyone in the family, so they may be under great stress. Other families may have already established their roles and routines. Remember, every family situation is different.

    Most children with disabilities live with their families at home, and many attend regular schools with the help of teachers’ aides. Being a primary caregiver for a child with a disability can be an all-consuming task, as it often takes great amounts of time, energy, and work. Many caregivers must balance caregiving with other responsibilities, including full-time jobs and caring for other children. Parents of children with disabilities may not ever leave their child with others so they can spend time alone with each other because they do not want to impose on family or friends to care for the child. Also, they may not be able to spend as much time with their other children as they would like.

    Some parental caregivers may feel that their child is considered “different” by society and worry about the stigma associated with their child’s disorder or disability. They may encounter discrimination and other barriers. The constant stress and work of caregiving can lead to burnout (see Chapter 8)—where the caregiver becomes physically and emotionally exhausted. There can be economic and financial burdens as well, because special equipment and adaptive and assistive devices are expensive and may not be covered under the family health plan or the provincial or territorial plan.

    Home care and other community agencies often provide needed support and services. The types of community agencies available vary by region. Families in large cities will more likely have easier access to support groups and agencies than families in small towns and rural communities. Many families may have to rely on the Internet for access to information and group support.

    As a support worker providing care in a family with a child with disabilities, you need to work closely with the family caregivers. You might take on some household tasks so that the parents can spend more time with the child with a disability and with their other children. You might provide respite care so that the parents can have a break from caregiving. You also might directly assist the child, according to the care plan. For example, you might help with the child’s rehabilitation activities under the supervision of a physiotherapist or an occupational therapist. You might accompany the child to and from school (FIGURE 36.4) or work with children with disabilities in schools. Children with severe disabilities may need long-term care in special facilities.

    As the child and parents grow older, caring for the adolescent or adult child often becomes physically difficult. Parents may not be able to lift or move the adolescent or adult child. Parents who are the caregivers may become ill, injured, or disabled, or may die. Through all these situations, the client with the developmental disorder or disability continues to need care.

    Adolescents and adults with developmental disabilities live in community or residential settings, in their own homes, or in group homes. Some may live in specially licensed long-term care facilities. Support workers in these facilities require special training to meet the needs of clients with developmental disabilities. Often, the client’s family continues to be involved in the care. Remember that family members have cared for the client for most of the person’s life, so they understand the client’s condition and needs very well. Include family members, whenever appropriate, when you provide care and services.

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Caring for Clients Who Are Living With Developmental Disorders and Disabilities

    1. • Dignity. Adults and children with developmental disabilities deserve to be treated with respect and in a manner that promotes their dignity. Remember to consider the client before the disability. The words you use are an important way to show respect. For example, say, “clients with disabilities.” Do not refer to them as “handicapped,” “disabled,” or “crippled.” Also, do not identify the client by the disability. For example, it is correct to say, “Mr. Joshi lives with autism spectrum disorder.” Do not say, “Mr. Joshi is autistic.” Say, “Susan lives with an intellectual disability,” not “Susan is retarded.”
    2. Have empathy, not pity. Remember, empathy is understanding the experiences and feelings of others; pity is feeling sorry for others, which implies that you are superior to the person.
    3. Also, promote dignity through your actions. Extend the same courtesies and consideration that you show all clients. Be friendly, and talk with your clients. Be a good listener, and show interest in their lives. Children with disabilities are like all children; they need attention and love. They also need to play and have fun. Adults living with disabilities are like all adults; they need support and encouragement. Treat them like adults. If appropriate, shake hands or nod your head and smile when introduced. Offer to shake hands even if the client has an artificial limb, has limited function in the hand, or has to use the left hand. Speak directly to the client in a normal tone of voice. Address adults using their titles and last names, unless they tell you otherwise.
    4. • Independence. The goal for clients with disabilities is for them to live as independently as possible. Do not make assumptions about what the client can and cannot do. Follow their care plan. Encourage the client to use self-help devices whenever possible. Be patient, and do not rush the client.
    5. • Individualized care. When working with children and adults with developmental disorders or disabilities, remember to consider the client before the disorder or disability. Each client is unique. The effects of disabilities vary, depending on the client. Your supervisor and the care plan will tell you how to meet each client’s needs in the best way. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. Rather than assume that the client wishes to be assisted with bathing, transferring, toileting, or eating in the same way as other clients, customize all care that is given to each client.
    6. • Preferences. Clients have the right to personal choices. Provide opportunities for the client to make choices and decisions. Ask first if the client wants help and how you can provide help. All clients must consent to procedures before you start. If the client does not want you to continue, stop. Contact your supervisor. Also, adapt your work to allow for the family’s choices and preferences (unless directed otherwise in the care plan).
    7. • Privacy. Medical and personal information about the client is confidential. Talk about the client only with members of the health care team who need to know. Do not expose the client. Provide privacy when performing procedures. Drape the client so as not to expose the client’s body. Remember that adults with disabilities have sexual needs. Many have partners or are married. Allow for privacy for sexual activity.
    8. • Safety. Most developmental disorders or disabilities create safety hazards for the client. Check with your supervisor and the care plan to determine how to ensure the client’s safety. Practise the safety measures described in Chapter 20. Also, be alert to signs of abuse. Report any suspected abuse to your supervisor immediately. Remember, you also must report child abuse directly to a public authority (see Chapter 12).

    • There are various developmental conditions, and each condition can have a wide range of symptoms.

    • A developmental disability can occur in the child because of the condition of the birth parent during pregnancy, or it can occur during birth, after birth, or during childhood or adolescence. The disability can interfere with or impair normal development in a child.

    Mental health disorders can affect all dimensions of a person’s life—physical, emotional, spiritual, social, and intellectual. As a support worker, you will care for and support clients and family members with physical health challenges, mental health challenges, or both. There may be no relationship between a client’s physical and mental health issues, but in some clients, physical conditions can result from mental health challenges or the reverse. To be able to provide care to your clients and their families while ensuring dignity, respect, and compassionate care, you need to understand what they are experiencing and be aware of your own attitude toward mental health and mental illness.     Providing Compassionate Care

    Supporting Clients With Mental Health Disorders

    1. • Dignity. Treat clients with mental health disorders as you would any other client. Show respect, and promote dignity. Do not label them—for example, it is not acceptable to say that a client is “a schizophrenic” or “a drug addict.” It is acceptable to say that the client “has schizophrenia” or “has a substance-dependence disorder” or “lives with schizophrenia” or “lives with a substance-dependence disorder.”
    2. • Be patient and understanding. Do not patronize (talk down to) the client. Do not feel offended or hurt by the client’s remarks or actions. Remember, the client may have difficulty relating to others. Be accepting of the client and help them feel good about themselves. Treat the client as a valued, worthy individual.
    3. • Independence. Clients with mental health disorders have the right to autonomy. They should be encouraged to be as independent as is safely possible. People with mental health disorders are impaired in some areas but are rarely impaired in all areas. For example, a client with schizophrenia usually has no problems with ambulation.
    4. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit each client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. It is important to establish trust with the client and their family. It is important to communicate appropriately with each client (see Chapter 9).
    5. • Preferences. Allow clients to make choices in their care, according to the care plan. Having options gives them a greater sense of control. Feelings of loss of control can cause some people with mental health disorders to become withdrawn, so encourage clients to express their wishes, likes, and dislikes.
    6. • Privacy. Discuss the client only with the health care team. Respect the client’s and the family’s right to confidentiality and privacy. You must respect the client’s right to have boundaries, especially when they refuse to discuss certain aspects of their life with you.
    7. • Safety. Stress, fatigue, and physical illness make mental health disorders worse and may trigger episodes. Observe the client carefully for any signs of stress or illness, and report such signs at once. Remember also that clients with mental health disorders may be more likely to fall or injure themselves if they are unable to focus on their surroundings. Provide a calm, stress-free, and safe environment. The client’s care plan will list activities and situations that should be avoided. Follow the safety guidelines in Chapter 20. For guidance on handling abusive situations, see Chapter 12.

    BOX 37.1 How Do I Know If I Have a Mental Health Disorder?

    Most people, at one time or another, have asked themselves this question: “Are my problems or symptoms getting in the way of my life?” If you think the answer is yes, then you may have a mental health disorder.

    The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5), published by the American Psychiatric Association, is used throughout North America to classify mental health disorders. The manual states that you may have a mental health disorder if what you feel is severe enough to cause “clinically significant distress or impairment in [your] social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.”

    People with a mental health disorder experience a disturbance in their ability to cope with or adjust to stress. They may have an alteration in their thinking, mood, or behaviour or in some combination of these aspects. They may feel high levels of distress and fear and often have trouble coping with everyday life. They may have difficulties keeping a job, staying in school, forming strong family and social relationships, and performing daily routines. People with mental health disorders often behave differently from what is considered “normal,” and their thinking, mood, or behaviours may be affected or impaired, with symptoms ranging from mild to severe.

    If you think that you may have a mental health disorder, you should have yourself thoroughly evaluated by your doctor as soon as possible. When you see your doctor, you should describe how you are feeling as completely as you can. Your doctor may then take a complete medical history and examine you for any physical conditions that might be contributing to your condition. The doctor may also advise you to refrain from drinking alcohol; to avoid taking any nonprescribed medications or drugs; to eat properly, reducing sugar and caffeine intake; to get plenty of exercise; and to get at least 8 hours of sleep.

    If you still feel that you have a mental health disorder after making these changes to your lifestyle, your doctor may then prescribe medications, propose another course of treatment, or refer you to a mental health professional. Remember that it is always best to seek treatment as early as possible!

    Mental Health and Mental Health Disorders

    A mental health disorder, which can be caused by a combination of genetic, biological, personality, and environmental factors, is a disturbance in a person’s ability to cope with or adjust to stress; as a result, the person’s thinking, mood, and behaviours are affected, and functioning is impaired. Twenty percent of Canadians (one in five) will personally experience a mental health disorder during their lifetime.1 Mental health disorders affect people of all ages, cultures, and educational and income levels. Many people may be uncertain whether they have a mental health disorder (see BOX 37.1 on page 859). The onset of most mental health disorders occurs during adolescence or young adulthood. The terms mental illness, mental disorder, emotional illness, and psychiatric disorder all refer to mental health disorder.

    Mental health is a state of mind in which a person copes with and adjusts to the stressors of everyday living in socially acceptable ways. It is influenced by three factors: (1) inherited characteristics, (2) childhood nurturing, and (3) life circumstances. With good mental health, a person can cope with life’s difficulties and challenges and can “bounce back,” or recover, from difficult situations.

    People who are mentally healthy may feel anxiety, sadness, grief, and loneliness from time to time, but they can handle normal amounts of stress and are able to express and control their emotions appropriately. They feel capable and competent, know their own needs, can form stable and satisfying relationships, and lead an independent life.

    The statistics regarding mental health disorders are staggering:1

    1. • 20% of Canadians will personally experience a mental health disorder during their lifetime.
    2. • Schizophrenia affects 1% of the population in Canada.
    3. • Anxiety disorders affect 5% of the population.

    Causes of Mental Health Disorders

    The causes of mental health disorders are complex, as there are numerous contributing factors, including the following:

    1. • Biological factors. Chemical imbalances in the body can cause mental health disorders. Some disorders run in families, which suggests that they can be inherited (passed from parent to child).
    2. • Childhood experiences. Childhood traumas or conflicts, particularly when memories of them are repressed, can cause mental health disorders. Repression means the keeping from the conscious mind of (or “burying of”) unpleasant or painful thoughts. For example, a client who was sexually abused during childhood has no recollection of the abuse.
    3. • Social and cultural factors. These include poverty, discrimination, and social or physical isolation.
    4. • Stressful life events. Although stress is a fact of everyday life, too much stress can cause serious health concerns, especially if it is not dealt with in a healthy-lifestyle manner. Family situations and workplace pressures can be stressful. Change is also stressful (see Chapter 8), especially when associated with loss (such as the death of a loved one or a divorce), and can lead to physical and mental health issues.
    5. • Poor physical health or disability. People with serious illnesses, injuries, or disabilities are at risk for certain mental health disorders.

    Supporting Clients and Their Families

    It is now known that mental health disorders can be treated successfully in the vast majority of circumstances. Until the 1960s, many people with chronic or severe mental health disorders lived in psychiatric facilities. Today, only those who are severely ill live in facilities. Unfortunately, a few people with mental health disorders end up living on the streets, as they are too ill to hold a job or apply for financial assistance, may fear receiving treatment, or are not aware of their own illness.

    Most people with mental health disorders are able to live in the community, where they may be offered treatment as well as assistance with life skills and employment. For example, once depression is recognized, treatment and support can make a difference for 80% of people who are affected, allowing them to get back to their regular activities.1 People with chronic or moderately severe mental health disorders are able to live in their homes, in group homes, or in assisted-living facilities, depending on their needs. Support workers provide valuable care and support to clients with mental health disorders and to their families in a wide variety of settings.

    The Importance of DIPPS

    While you are caring for and providing support to a person with a mental health disorder, your actions should always focus on respect for and acceptance of the client and their family. It is important to remember that the client is not defined by their diagnosis. Remember the principles of DIPPS (see the Providing Compassionate Care: Supporting Clients With Mental Health Disorders box on page 858). BOX 37.2 lists the principles that govern health care workers’ care and support for clients with mental health disorders and for their families.

    Mental health disorders affect people in different ways, and people with mild disorders tend to have fewer challenges than those with more pronounced mental health issues. Severe mental health disorders, however, almost always cause distress not only to the affected individuals but also to their families. The individuals with the disorder may be unable to function, and their behaviours may become disruptive at times. The support worker often provides the needed reassurance and support to these clients and to their families.

    Because they might have been exposed to negative attitudes in the past, your clients and their families may be particularly sensitive to any verbal or nonverbal signs that indicate disapproval of them or stigmatization of people with mental health disorders. Therefore, you need to pay particular attention to your body language and verbal language and make every attempt to convey to them that you are respectful, nonjudgemental, and accepting of their behaviours (see Chapter 9 for a discussion of ways to communicate acceptance and respect).

    Think About Safety

    Maintaining Client and Worker Safety

    It is not uncommon for clients in any setting to feel comfortable enough to share confidential information about themselves with their support workers. However, if the client shares an intention to do self-harm or to harm others, you must report this immediately to your supervisor or the appropriate person. You must then accurately record the conversation in the client’s records (see Chapter 26). Clients should also be informed that health care workers are obligated to report this information.

    When supporting a client, remember that your personal safety is also a priority (see Chapter 20). Remember to maintain a safe distance from any client who might strike you, intentionally or otherwise. Do not enter premises or leave them if there are unlocked firearms or weapons present. Always maintain a clear path by which to leave any premises suddenly, and immediately notify the appropriate person on your cell phone, according to your employer’s policies.

    BOX 37.2 Principles of Mental Health Care

    • 1. Follow the care plan. Tell your supervisor if support measures are not working.
    • 2. Do no harm. Provide a safe, comfortable setting for the client. You need to provide both safe care and protection from harm at all times. A safe, quiet, and neat setting can calm the client. You should remember to act as the client’s advocate whenever necessary to ensure the client’s safety (see the Think About Safety box on page 861).
    • 3. Accept each client as a whole person. You should be accepting of the client and refrain from making any judgements.
    • 4. Be patient and supportive. Speak calmly; avoid speaking in a loud or sharp tone of voice.
    • 5. Develop mutual trust. Remember to do what you say you will do. Being on time, providing the care as promised, and explaining all procedures will promote trust and reduce your client’s anxiety.
    • 6. Explore behaviours and emotions. Many clients will share with you what they are feeling and thinking if they trust you and if you take the time to listen to them.
    • 7. Observe the client carefully. Observe for any changes in the client’s behaviour, mood, and thinking, such as signs and symptoms of fatigue, stress, anxiety, fear, and frustration, as well as signs and symptoms of illness. Report and record all of your observations, according to employer policy.
    • 8. Encourage responsibility. Taking responsibility for their own actions helps clients build self-worth, dignity, and confidence.
    • 9. Encourage effective adaptation. Clients who are mentally stressed may behave in socially unacceptable ways or may be harmful to themselves or others. It is often your responsibility to intervene when this behaviour happens and to help clients find better ways of coping.
    • 10. Provide consistency. Maintaining a routine promotes a sense of control. Consistency and reliability of your care will also provide security and stability for the client. This will, in turn, help reduce the client’s stresses and anxieties.

    Team Approach

    The care-planning process aims at addressing the needs of clients with mental health disorders, including their physical safety and emotional needs, and usually requires input from various members of the health care team. The physician may order medications, depending on the client’s disorder, signs, and symptoms, as a number of mental health disorders can be controlled effectively with medication.

    The health care team may include a family physician, nurse, occupational therapist (who helps the person learn or relearn skills for the performance of life tasks), social worker (who provides assistance such as helping a client resolve employment problems), support worker, and one or more of the following mental health specialists:

    1. • Psychiatrists —physicians who specialize in mental health disorders and who can prescribe medications for treatment
    2. • Psychologists and psychotherapists —health care providers educated in treatments for mental health disorders that do not involve prescribing medications
    3. • Indigenous mental health providers— elders, healers, and cultural counsellors, who are vital parts of many Indigenous mental health services, and other members of a treatment or mental health and wellness staff, who assist clients and provide services to Indigenous people and communities. Some practitioners use exclusively traditional Indigenous healing methods or a combination of traditional and Western health care methods.

    Treatment of mental health disorders often involves psychotherapy, which is a form of therapy in which a client explores thoughts, feelings, and behaviours with the help and guidance from a mental health specialist. The various forms of psychotherapy include the following:

    1. • Psychoanalysis —explores the unconscious conflicts and reasons behind the person’s mental health issues.
    2. • Behaviour therapy —attempts to change behaviour by using various techniques. The focus is on the behaviour, not on the underlying reasons for the behaviour.
    3. • Cognitive behavioural therapy —a practical, short-term form of psychotherapy. It focuses on the challenges a person may face in day-to-day life and is problem-focused and goal-oriented. It teaches the client strategies and skills for coping.4
    4. • Group therapy —a group of people who meet regularly to discuss their concerns under the guidance of a mental health specialist.
    5. • Family therapy —family members meet regularly with a mental health specialist to discuss their issues or conflicts.

    Stigma of Mental Health Disorders

    Chapter 6 included a discussion about how some people feel uncomfortable or fearful in the company of people with illnesses and disabilities. They may stare or avoid eye contact and may treat these people differently from others who are well or appear able-bodied. This altered treatment is commonly faced by people living with mental health disorders, who are often discriminated against because of a lack of understanding of mental health disorders. Some people fear being with a person with a mental health disorder; they do not know what to expect and may believe that the person is dangerous. Some may blame people with mental health disorders for their own difficulties. Such attitudes lead people to avoid and exclude persons with mental health disorders, often causing them to feel ashamed, rejected, and isolated.

    Stigma is social shame associated with a particular circumstance, quality, or person, such as “the stigma of a mental health disorder.” A report from the Mental Health Commission of Canada stated that for individuals with mental health disorders, stigma is a major reason for not seeking treatment, taking prescribed medication, or attending counselling.5 It also stated that stigma affects men more than it does women and influences the person’s successful reintegration into the family or into society in general. The stigma extends to the workplace, with employers often having concerns about the person’s ability to function at the level of other employees. Although it is against current human rights legislation to openly discriminate against people with mental health disorders, many employers still do.

    The Canadian Alliance on Mental Illness and Mental Health (CAMIMH) is an organization that represents mental health professionals and individuals concerned with mental health. The main goal of the CAMIMH is to prevent stigma and discrimination against people with mental health disorders. Through educational programs, the organization promotes greater understanding and acceptance of people with mental health disorders.6

    As a support worker, you must remember that all clients (and their families) have the right to caring, nonjudgemental support and the right to be treated with dignity and respect, as stressed throughout this textbook.

    Effect on the Family

    Often, family members of clients with mental health disorders must make difficult decisions about care, treatment, and housing. They may feel anxious about an uncertain future, and the financial burden of caring for a loved one who is ill may be significant. Some family members may feel guilty and blame themselves for the illness, and some others may be at risk for depression themselves.

    Family members are also affected by the stigma of mental health disorders. Friends and acquaintances who feel uncomfortable may not offer their time or social support. One woman described how differently people reacted to her husband’s physical illness than to her son’s mental health disorder: “When my husband had cancer, neighbours and friends were very kind. The phone rang constantly with offers to help. People brought over meals and sent flowers and cards. When my son was diagnosed with schizophrenia, everything was different. Nobody called. Nobody asked how we were doing. They pretended everything was fine. We felt very much alone.”

    Culture and Its Influence on Mental Health Disorders and Treatment

    The need for understanding cultural differences among clients receiving support care has been emphasized repeatedly in this book. As stated in Chapter 7, culture is not limited to ethnic background but can extend to any group of interacting individuals who share similar learned characteristics, such as families, groups of friends, or even people who have shared similar experiences in the past. Culture has a profound influence on understanding mental health disorders and their treatments. People in one culture may not find it hard to seek medical attention for their mental health disorder, while people in another culture may see it as a sign of weakness. Similarly, some family members may refuse to acknowledge a loved one’s mental health disorder, whereas others may readily be open about it. Some cultures distrust traditional medicine and prefer to consult their local healer for herbal treatments or other remedies.

    Interpretations of the signs and symptoms of what some call “mental illness” vary greatly from culture to culture and even from person to person. What may be appropriate behaviour in one culture or group of people may be considered “insanity” in another. For example, someone who has lived through severe poverty might be afraid or hesitant to throw anything away, even items such as rubber bands or paper clips; some, in contrast, may consider keeping these things odd behaviour (see the Supporting Mrs. Hill: The Effect of Past Experiences box). Understanding your clients’ cultural background before caring for them will help you show respect for their cultural differences and preferences as you provide care. Make sure that you follow the DIPPS principles.

    Providing Care and Support for Clients With Mental Health Disorders

    There are different types of mental health disorders, and this chapter briefly addresses the most common of these, paying particular attention to the role of the support worker in caring for clients with these disorders and for their families. Common mental health disorders addressed in this chapter include acquired brain injuries; schizophrenia spectrum disorders; bipolar and related disorders; major depressive disorders; anxiety disorders; trauma and stressor-related disorders; obsessive–compulsive and related disorders; feeding and eating disorders; sleep–wake disorders; suicidal behaviour disorder; disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders; substance-related and addictive disorders; and personality disorders. Disorientation, delirium, and dementia, which are conditions and mental health disorders of particular significance in older persons, are discussed in Chapter 38.

    Acquired Brain Injuries

    An acquired brain injury (ABI), which was introduced in Chapter 33, is any type of sudden injury or illness that causes temporary or permanent damage to the brain. It is also discussed in this chapter because clients who live with an ABI may be at increased risk for feelings of frustration, anxiety, mood swings, or depression (see Supporting Greg Wood: How an ABI Can Affect Behaviour, on the next page). Families of clients must learn to adjust to any new changes in personality or if the clients can no longer contribute to the family income as they used to. Some clients who have suffered an undiagnosed (or mild) ABI can be mislabelled, misdiagnosed, and misunderstood, which can increase their feelings of frustration or anxiety.

    Supporting Clients With Acquired Brain Injury (ABI)

    While providing support to clients with ABI, keep the following in mind:7

    1. • Follow clients’ care plans in reassuring them that what they are experiencing is a result of brain injury. Clients with ABI may have forgotten about the injury that occurred but may be aware that they feel “different” from before. One goal of the health care team is to teach and reinforce information about the reasons for their symptoms. Doing so will help to reduce the fear and anxiety that clients may feel.
    2. • Encourage clients to establish routines and structure in daily tasks. Being able to establish a routine when carrying out everyday tasks such as bathing, dressing, eating, or toileting will reinforce a sense of accomplishment and self-esteem. It can also reduce anxieties resulting from trying to decide what to do.
    3. • Encourage clients to set both long-term and short-term goals. Goals help the client with ABI to focus on a specific task and can motivate the client to work toward reaching the goals. Goals should always be realistic and achievable. If a certain goal is not achieved, the client may need to be supported to accept the failure and to try again.
    4. • Ensure that clients’ adaptive devices are within reach. If a client must use an adaptive device (such as a wheelchair, memory aid, or picture board) to assist with ambulation or communication, it should be easy for the client to reach and use.
    5. • Encourage clients to “ put the past behind them.” A client who focuses on lost abilities may refuse to participate in rehabilitation activities. Although this reaction is normal for a short period, if prolonged, it may prevent the client from reaching their full potential.
    6. • Follow the steps in Supporting Clients With Anxiety Disorders, on page 868, and Supporting Clients With A Depressive Disorder, on page 872, if directed to do so by your supervisor.
    7. • Observe for and report signs indicating alcohol or substance use. The client with ABI who is feeling depressed may wish to drink alcohol or take recreational drugs or substances and may lack the ability to reason that these substances may be harmful.

    Greg Wood is an athletic 18-year-old high school student who had a promising career as a hockey player. During a hockey game, Greg was tripped by another player, causing him to fall face-first on the ice. Although he had a helmet on, he lost consciousness for an hour. He was taken to the hospital and diagnosed with a concussion. In the 6 months following this accident, Greg suffered daily headaches and dizziness and was unable to go to school or to skate, even for short periods. He frequently yelled at his parents and younger sister, and he punched several holes in his bedroom wall. Because his parents did not wish to leave Greg by himself while they were at work, they hired a support worker to stay with him during the day and to make his meals.

    One day, Sam, his support worker, noticed that Greg smelled of beer and that he was unsteady on his feet. Sam found several empty beer bottles in the bathroom next to the shower. When Sam asked Greg about them, Greg began to swear and throw books across the room at Sam, almost hitting him. Sam reported the event to his supervisor. At the end of Sam’s shift, Greg began to cry and apologized to him. Greg told Sam that his hockey teammates did not come over anymore because he seemed so “different” from how he was before.

    Anxiety Disorders

    Anxiety disorders are a group of mental health disorders whose main symptom is extreme anxiety, with fears and worries so disproportionate to the situation as to affect normal functioning. Anxiety is a vague, uneasy feeling in response to stress. Anxiety disorders affect about 5% of the Canadian population.1 Most of these disorders begin in childhood and tend to continue if not treated, and they are twice as likely to affect women as men.8 An anxious person feels a sense of dread, danger, or harm. Some amount of anxiety is normal, but persons with anxiety disorders experience extreme anxiety. While some people can be debilitated with anxiety, others are high functioning and able to go to work or school. Many anxiety disorders can be treated with medication, and clients will generally notice a reduction of symptoms as long as they take their medications continuously. The main types of anxiety disorders are discussed in this chapter.

    Panic Disorder

    Panic is an intense and sudden feeling of fear, anxiety, terror, or dread for no obvious reason. The physical symptoms of fear (shortness of breath, racing heart, sweat) escalate very quickly when a person has a panic attack. Many affected people experience symptoms that are similar to those of a heart attack (e.g., chest pains).

    Clients who wonder if they have panic disorder should ask themselves the following questions:

    1. • Do I have sudden bursts of fear for no reason?
    2. • Do I feel physically unwell when they happen?
    3. • Are there times when, for no reason, I experience chest pains, a racing heart, dizziness, sweating, stomach pain, nausea, and a feeling of loss of control?
    4. • Do I fear at those times that I am dying or going crazy?

    Panic attacks can happen anytime, anywhere, and without warning. Clients with panic attacks may live in fear of having another attack and may avoid places where they experienced an attack in the past. For some clients, fear takes over their lives, and they are not able to leave their homes.

    Panic disorder is more common in women than in men. It usually starts in young adulthood, often during times of excessive stress. Panic disorder can last for months or years.

    The treatment for panic disorder includes teaching the client to recognize and change thinking patterns before they lead to a panic attack, as well as medications prescribed by a doctor.

    Phobic Disorder

    Phobia means fear, panic, or dread. A person with a phobic disorder has intense fear of a particular thing or situation. Often, phobias occur after an event—for example, a fear of flying may develop after a person hears reports of a recent plane crash. People who struggle with phobias often feel embarrassed and stupid because they know their fear is exaggerated. Common phobias include agoraphobia (fear of open, crowded, public places) and claustrophobia (fear of small, enclosed places). Symptoms of phobic disorder include the following:

    1. • Excessive worry
    2. • Sleep disruption from worrying
    3. • Fatigue or irritability
    4. • Difficulty concentrating
    5. • Physical symptoms such as increased sweating, heavy breathing, irregular heartbeats (palpitations), dizziness, faintness, muscle pain or tension, and even diarrhea from interference with digestion

    Other Types of Anxiety Disorders

    The following anxiety disorders do not fit within the categories above but may affect some clients:8

    1. • Separation anxiety disorder. Persistent and excessive worrying about losing major attachment figures (such as parents or significant others), which can result in the client’s reluctance to leave the house to separate from them or in nightmares about losing them.
    2. • Selective mutism. A condition that lasts for a month or more in which the client experiences consistent inability or extreme reluctance to speak in a specific social situation, despite being able to speak in other situations.
    3. • Social anxiety disorder. Severe distress or feelings of anxiety involving one or more social situations in which the client may be exposed to criticism or rejection, lasting for more than 6 months.

    Trauma and Stressor-Related Disorders

    Trauma and stressor-related disorders are anxiety disorders that can develop after an individual has experienced or witnessed a major trauma. Any traumatic event, such as experiencing residential schools, war, fires, accidents, burns, torture, kidnapping, concentration camps, incest, or violent crimes, can overwhelm the normal psychological coping mechanisms of the person witnessing or experiencing these events.
