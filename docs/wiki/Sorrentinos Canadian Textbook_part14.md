### Incentive Spirometry

An incentive provides encouragement. A spirometer is a machine that measures the amount (volume) of air inhaled. The client is encouraged to inhale until a preset volume of air is reached. The incentive spirometer is made up of a breathing tube, an air chamber, and an indicator (called a piston), which will rise with each inhalation. The piston allows the client to see air movement during inhalation ([FIGURE 39.7](#f0045)). The piston is made from a flat disc, plastic balls, or bars. The client inhales as deeply as possible and holds that breath for a certain time, usually at least 3 seconds.

Incentive spirometry is also called sustained maximal inspiration (SMI) (sustained means “constant”; maximal means “the most or the greatest”; and inspiration means “breathing in”).

The goal of using a spirometer is to improve lung function and prevent respiratory complications. A spirometer may be given to some clients to use at home and to those in hospital who have had surgery, but they can be used by anybody the doctor thinks would benefit from their use. By taking long, slow, and deep breaths, the client moves air deep into the lungs; secretions become loose; and O2–CO2 exchange occurs between the alveoli and capillaries.

The device is used as follows:

* 1. The spirometer is placed upright.
* 2. The client exhales normally.
* 3. The client seals the lips around the mouthpiece of the device.
* 4. The client takes a slow, deep breath until the balls in the spirometer rise to the desired height.
* 5. The client holds the breath for 3 to 6 seconds to keep the balls floating.
* 6. The client removes their mouth from the mouthpiece and exhales slowly. The client may cough at this time.
* 7. After taking some normal breaths, the client uses the device again.

Your supervisor and the client’s care plan will tell you the following:

1. • How often the client needs to use incentive spirometry
2. • How many breaths the client needs to take
3. • The desired height that the floating balls should reach

Follow employer policy for cleaning and replacing the mouthpiece.

### Oxygen Sources

Whether the client is at home or in a continuing care facility, oxygen may be supplied by one of three main delivery systems: oxygen concentrator, oxygen
cylinder, or liquid oxygen system. Oxygen is strictly regulated under Health Canada. #### Oxygen Concentrator

This machine makes oxygen by taking in room air and filtering out the oxygen ([FIGURE 39.8](#f0050)). It must be plugged into a grounded electrical outlet. Because the oxygen concentrator “makes” the oxygen, it does not need to be replaced or refilled regularly. Ambulatory clients may use portable oxygen cylinders along with their concentrators to help them be mobile and also be prepared for a power failure. Oxygen concentrators are used in facilities and community settings, whereas cylinders are used by the client while on outings from home. Some suppliers offer lightweight aluminum cylinders for easier handling.

#### Oxygen Cylinder

An oxygen cylinder is a tank of compressed oxygen. Large tanks are used inside the home, and small tanks are used for outings or travel. Some clients may be ambulatory but still need continuous oxygen therapy as they go about their daily lives, so they use portable oxygen cylinders (tanks) carried in a shoulder bag or wheeled in a cart ([FIGURE 39.9](#f0055)). Large cylinders are often used for babies who need special breathing equipment.

The length of time a cylinder lasts will depend on the amount of oxygen the client uses. A gauge on the cylinder shows how much oxygen is left in it ([FIGURE 39.10](#f0060)). Check the gauge often, and tell your supervisor if the oxygen in the cylinder is low.

Oxygen cylinders are used for clients in facility or community settings. In hospitals and some continuing care facilities, oxygen is piped directly to each client’s unit by way of a wall oxygen outlet ([FIGURE 39.11](#f0065)) from a centrally located oxygen supply.

#### Liquid Oxygen System

The liquid oxygen system stores oxygen in its liquid form. The liquid is kept in large stationary containers called reservoirs. A portable unit that can be worn over the shoulder is filled from a stationary unit ([FIGURE 39.12](#f0070)) and has enough oxygen for about 8 hours of use. The liquid turns into gas before it leaves the container and is breathed in by the client.

A gauge shows the amount of oxygen in the unit. When checking the unit, tell your supervisor if the amount is low. Liquid oxygen is very cold and, if touched, can freeze the skin. Follow employer procedures and the manufacturer’s instructions when working with liquid oxygen. Liquid oxygen systems are used for clients in facility or community settings.

With the oxygen systems described above, oxygen is delivered continuously while the client inhales and exhales. However, a lot of the oxygen is wasted when the client exhales. Oxygen-conserving devices help reduce this wastage so that the client’s oxygen supply lasts longer, and the client can thus go for longer outings. Conserving devices also cut down on the number of oxygen cylinders that have to be delivered to the client.

#### Oxygen and Fire Safety

Oxygen is the chemical necessary for combustion to take place. With higher-pressurized oxygen—such as with compressed oxygen cylinders—the risk for combustion increases when in the presence of both a source of ignition and a combustible material. For example, if a client had compressed oxygen nearby and was close to a source of ignition (e.g., a lit cigarette), the client’s clothing would ignite more easily and burn hotter and faster than it would if surrounded only by room air. Fires caused by an accident with oxygen devices can cause significant damage to property and even loss of life. It is very important to keep an oxygen source away from heat and open flame. Follow employer policies and the fire safety guidelines in [BOX 39.5](#b0030).

The physician, nurse, or respiratory therapist is responsible for teaching the client and family members about oxygen safety. However, people sometimes forget or disregard safety rules (see the Supporting Mr. Kaluwa: Oxygen Safety at Home box on page 941). ### Oxygen Administration Devices

The physician orders the device used to administer oxygen. The devices that are commonly ordered are described below.

#### Nasal Cannula

The nasal cannula ([FIGURE 39.13](#f0075)) is used at home or in a hospital setting to deliver oxygen to a client in need of extra oxygen. It consists of a set of two prongs that are placed in the client’s nostrils and a plastic tube that fits behind the ears. The prongs from which oxygen flows curve downward to prevent drying of the sinuses.

The nasal cannula is connected to an oxygen tank, to a portable oxygen generator, or to a wall connection in a hospital through a flowmeter,
which regulates the flow of oxygen. The client can eat and talk with the cannula in place. If the prongs are placed too tightly, nasal irritation or pressure on the ears and cheekbones may occur. You should always report signs or complaints of skin irritation or soreness.

### Preparing for Oxygen Administration

#### Humidified Oxygen

Oxygen is a dry gas. If it is not humidified (made moist), it can dry the mucous membranes of the airways. A humidifier ([FIGURE 39.19](#f0105)) is a system by which oxygen is forced through distilled water,
allowing it to bubble up and be collected in the tubing for the client to breathe. Distilled water is pure, sterile water that has been boiled and allowed to cool and condense. Tap water is not used, as it contains impurities that can irritate the mucous membranes and damage the equipment. The humidifier is then attached to the oxygen administration system (wall outlet, oxygen tank, or oxygen concentrator). It then produces humidified oxygen—that is, oxygen that contains water vapour. Humidified oxygen is less drying and irritating to delicate mucous membranes than plain oxygen is.

![A wall installed oxygen outlet shows a bottle of humidifier attached to an air-oxygen blender with a flowmeter at the top.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000390/main.assets/f39-19-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 39.19 Oxygen administration system with humidifier.

Low flow rates (1 to 2 L/min) by nasal cannula usually do not need humidification. You may be responsible for checking the water level in the humidifier and cleaning and refilling it. Follow employer procedures and your supervisor’s directions.

## Assisting with Assessment and Diagnostic Testing

The physician orders tests to determine the cause of a breathing problem. Numerous respiratory tests are available ([BOX 39.6](#b0035)). Most of these tests are done at hospitals or clinics, and some are done in continuing care facilities. They are performed by a physician or a specially trained nurse, respiratory therapist, or laboratory technician. You may assist the client before and after a test, as directed by your supervisor, the care plan, and the assignment sheet.

As a support worker you are likely to assist with pulse oximetry and collect sputum specimens (see page 946). Both procedures may provide the physician with information about the state of the client’s respiratory system.

### Pulse Oximetry

A pulse oximeter is a device used to measure (meter) oxygen (oxi) concentration in arterial blood as well as to measure pulse rate (see [Chapter 24](../B9780323709392000249/B9780323709392000249.xhtml)). Oxygen concentration is the amount (percent) of hemoglobin that contains oxygen. The normal range is 95 to 100%. For example, if 97% of all the hemoglobin carries O2, tissues are getting enough oxygen. If only 90% of the hemoglobin contains O2, tissues are not getting enough oxygen to function. The pulse oximeter can detect low oxygen levels before any signs or symptoms appear, and the measurements are used to prevent and treat hypoxia.

A pulse oximeter, which consists of a computerized monitor and a sensor (probe), is a very fragile and expensive piece of equipment that should always be handled gently.
Never drop or handle a pulse oximeter roughly, as doing so can affect the delicate sensors within it
. Most employers now consider pulse oximetry a regular part of taking a client’s vital signs, so the support worker needs to be aware of the correct method of attaching the pulse oximeter and reading it (see the procedure Measuring the Pulse and Oxygen Concentration Using a Pulse Oximeter in [Chapter 24](../B9780323709392000249/B9780323709392000249.xhtml)).

The sensor is attached to the client’s finger, toe, nose, earlobe, or forehead ([FIGURE 39.20](#f0110)). Two light beams on one side of the sensor pass through the tissues. BOX 39.6 Common Respiratory Tests

1. •
   Chest X-ray (CXR)—
   an X-ray of the chest; taken to evaluate changes in the lungs.
2. •
   Lung scan
   —performed to see which areas are not getting air or blood. The client inhales radioactive (radioactive means “giving off radiation”) gas and is injected with a radioisotope (a radioisotope is a substance that gives off radiation). Lung tissue getting air and blood “takes up” the radioactive substances, and a scanner then senses the areas with radioactive substances.
3. •
   Bronchoscopy
   —a scope (scopy) passed into the trachea and bronchi (broncho). Airway structures are checked for bleeding and tumours. Tissue samples (biopsy) are taken, or mucous plugs and foreign objects are removed. After the procedure, the client is NPO (receives nothing by mouth) and watched carefully until the gag and swallow reflexes return, usually in about 2 hours.
4. •
   Thoracentesis
   —the pleura (thora) is punctured, and air or fluid is removed (centesis) from it. The physician inserts a needle through the chest wall into the pleural sac when injury or disease causes it to fill with air, blood, or fluid, and respiratory function is affected. Sometimes fluid is removed for laboratory study, and sometimes anticancer medications are injected into the pleural sac. After the procedure, which takes only a few minutes, the client is checked frequently for shortness of breath, dyspnea, cough, sputum (see page 946), chest pain, cyanosis, vital sign changes, and other respiratory signs and symptoms.
5. •
   Pulmonary function tests
   —tests that measure the amount of air moving into and out of the lungs (volume) and how much air the lungs can hold (capacity). The client takes as deep a breath as possible and, using a mouthpiece, blows into a machine. The tests are used to evaluate clients at risk for lung diseases or postoperative lung complications and to measure the progress of lung disease and its treatment.
6. •
   Arterial blood gases (ABGs)—
   the puncturing of a radial or femoral artery to obtain arterial blood and measure the amount of oxygen in it. Hemorrhage from the artery must be prevented, so pressure is applied to the artery for at least 5 minutes after the procedure and longer if the client has blood clotting issues.

Small, handheld oximeter units are available for home use. Usually, the client’s oxygen concentration and vital signs are measured simultaneously. Because the device is portable and used for many clients, when using it you must test its accuracy: After applying the sensor, check the client’s radial pulse and compare it with the displayed pulse. The pulse rates should be the same.

Selecting the ideal sensor site on the client is important. Your supervisor or the care plan will tell you which site to use, based on the client’s condition. Swollen sites and sites with skin breaks should be avoided. Because aging and vascular disease often cause poor circulation, blood flow to the fingers or toes is sometimes poor. In this case, the earlobe, nose, or forehead site is used.

Bright light, nail polish, artificial nails, and movements can affect measurement accuracy. To prevent inaccurate readings, place a towel over the sensor to block out bright light; remove the client’s nail polish or use another site; and do not use a finger site if the client wears artificial nails. Movements from shivering, seizures, or tremors can affect finger sensors. The earlobe is a better site if a client is experiencing these conditions. Blood pressure cuffs affect blood flow, so if you are using a finger site, do not measure blood pressure on that side.

It is important to report and record measurements accurately. Use the abbreviation SpO2 (S = saturation, p = pulse, O2 = oxygen) when recording the oxygen saturation value. Also, report and record the following:

1. • The date and time
2. • What the client was doing at the time of measurement
3. • Oxygen flow rate and the device used
4. • Reason for the measurement (routine or change in the client’s condition)
5. • Other observations, such as the client’s cooperation with wearing the pulse oximeter

### Collecting Sputum Specimens

Respiratory disorders cause the lungs, bronchi, and trachea to secrete mucus. Mucus from the respiratory system is called sputum when expectorated (expelled) through the mouth. Note that sputum is not saliva (spit), which is a thin, clear liquid produced by the salivary glands in the mouth.

Sputum specimens can be collected from clients in the community, in continuing care facilities, and in the hospital. Sputum specimens are studied for blood, microbes, and abnormal cells. For this test, the client must cough up sputum from the bronchi and trachea (not saliva from the mouth); doing so can often be painful and hard to do. Specimens should be collected in the morning because secretions are more easily coughed up upon awakening.

The client should rinse their mouth only with water to decrease saliva and remove food particles. Instruct the client
not
 to use mouthwash before the procedure because it can destroy some of the microbes in the mouth and give a false reading.

Ensure that the client has privacy for this procedure. The procedure can be embarrassing for the client, as the sputum is unpleasant to look at, and the coughing and expectorating sounds may disturb others nearby. If possible, collect the specimen when the roommate or family members are out of the room. Cover the specimen container, and place it in a bag in order to keep the contents out of sight. Always follow Standard Practices (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)) when collecting a sputum specimen.

#### Postural Drainage and Chest Percussion

Some clients do not have the strength to cough up sputum, so the nurse or respiratory therapist will place them in a certain position to allow secretions to drain by gravity (gravity causes fluids to flow downward). Coughing is then made easier for the client after postural drainage. The client is positioned in such a way that a lung part is at a higher level than the airway ([FIGURE 39.21](#f0115)). Positioning depends on the lung part that needs draining.

Chest percussion (also known as chest clapping) is a technique that loosens thick, sticky mucus that is trapped in the lungs into the larger airways. It is performed by family members or trained health care workers, such as a respiratory therapist. It is done by holding one’s hands in a cupped position and clapping the client’s chest in a forceful but steady beat, moving from the distal areas of the chest toward the main bronchus. Most of the movement is in the wrist with the arm relaxed, making percussion
less tiring to do. If the hand is cupped properly, percussion should not be painful or sting. Special attention must be taken to avoid clapping over the client’s spine, breastbone, stomach, and lower ribs or back to prevent injury to the spleen on the left, the liver on the right, and the kidneys in the lower back.[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur452)

### Tracheostomies

A tracheostomy is a surgically created opening (ostomy) through the neck into the trachea (tracheo). A tracheostomy tube is usually inserted through this
opening, and the client breathes through the tracheostomy tube. A humidifier attachment may be needed for about 1 month after tracheostomy surgery because the trachea is now exposed to drier air.

#### Types of Tracheostomies

Tracheostomies can be temporary or permanent, depending on the client’s circumstances. In temporary situations, tracheostomies may have been inserted because of a severe breathing crisis or blockage. In permanent cases, airway structures have been surgically removed, along with the client’s larynx and epiglottis. Clients who have head and neck cancers, severe airway trauma, or brain injury may require permanent tracheostomies. Generally, a surgeon will consider a tracheostomy only when there is no other option (see the Focus on Children: Congenital Conditions box). Certain groups—for example, babies, smokers, and older persons—are more vulnerable to complications following a tracheostomy.

The long-term care of these clients depends on whether the tracheostomy tube is temporary or permanent. If it is temporary, it will be removed when no longer needed, and the incision will be allowed to heal closed, leaving a small scar in the neck area. If the tracheostomy is permanent, the hole (stoma) will stay open. However, the opening tends to narrow with time, and further surgery may be needed to widen it. The outer tracheostomy tube needs to be changed every few months, and the site is inspected at each change. The client may be referred to a speech pathologist for learning alternative means of communication.

## Suctioning an Airway

Injury and illness often cause secretions to collect in the upper airway, resulting in the following concerns:

1. • They obstruct airflow in and out of the airway.
2. • They provide an environment for microbes.
3. • They interfere with O2–CO2 exchange.
4. • Hypoxia can occur.

Usually, coughing removes secretions, but some clients are unable to cough, or the cough is too weak to remove secretions. Some clients cannot expectorate or swallow secretions after coughing. These clients need suctioning to remove secretions.

Suctioning is the process of withdrawing or sucking up fluid (secretions). A tube connects to a suction source (wall outlet or suction machine) at one end and to a suction catheter at the other end. The catheter is inserted into the airway, and secretions are withdrawn through the catheter.

### Suctioning Sites

The upper airway (nose, mouth, and pharynx) and the lower airway (trachea and bronchi) are suctioned.

1. •
   Oropharyngeal route
   —the mouth (oro) and pharynx (pharyngeal) are suctioned. A suction catheter is passed through the mouth and into the pharynx.
2. •
   Nasopharyngeal route
   —the nose (naso) and pharynx (pharyngeal) are suctioned. The suction catheter is passed through the nose and into the pharynx.
3. •
   Lower airway suctioning
   —done through an ET tube or through a tracheostomy tube.

### Safety Measures Related to Suctioning

If not done correctly, suctioning can cause serious harm to the client. As suctioning removes oxygen from the airway, the client may be unable to breathe during suctioning, and hypoxia and life-threatening complications can arise. The respiratory, cardiovascular, and nervous systems can be affected, and cardiac arrest, infection, and airway injury are possible.
It is your responsibility to ensure that suctioning is within your scope of practice for your province or territory and within your agency.

The client’s lungs are hyperventilated—given extra (hyper) breaths (ventilate)—before suctioning from an ET tube or tracheostomy. An Ambu bag ([FIGURE 39.26](#f0140)) is attached to an oxygen source; then the oxygen delivery device is removed from the ET or tracheostomy tube, and the Ambu bag is attached to the ET or tracheostomy tube. To give a breath, the bag is squeezed with both hands. A nurse or respiratory therapist gives three to five breaths. Remember, oxygen is considered a medication, and you do
not
 administer medications. Therefore, you may operate an Ambu bag attached to an oxygen source only if it is within your scope of practice.

## Mechanical Ventilation

With some severe conditions or diseases, a client may not be able to breathe, or normal blood oxygen levels are not maintained. These conditions include (1) hypoxia caused by weak muscle effort, airway obstruction, and damaged lung tissue; (2) nervous system diseases and injuries that can affect the respiratory centre, interfering with messages between the lungs and the brain; and (3) drug overdoses that can depress the brain’s hypothalamus or the breathing control centre. In these situations, mechanical ventilation may be needed to save the client’s life.
Mechanical ventilation is the use of a machine to move air into and out of the lungs ([FIGURE 39.28](#f0150)), allowing O2 to enter the lungs and CO2 to leave them.
It is your responsibility to ensure that assisting with mechanical ventilation is within your scope of practice for your province or territory and your agency.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Oxygen is a basic necessity of life. Death occurs within minutes if breathing stops, and serious illnesses can occur without enough oxygen.

• Factors affecting oxygen needs include (1) respiratory system status, (2) cardiovascular system status, (3) red blood cell count, (4) nervous system function, (5) aging, (6) exercise, (7) fever, (8) pain, (9) drug (narcotic or barbiturate) overdose, (10) smoking, (11) allergies, (12) pollutant exposure, (13) nutrition, and (14) alcohol use.

• Healthy adults take 12 to 20 breaths per minute on average. Tachypnea is rapid breathing (24 breaths or more per minute), and bradypnea is slow breathing (12 or fewer breaths per minute).

• All health care workers should be familiar with the signs and symptoms of hypoxia and must know to report them to the supervisor.

• As a support worker, you can promote oxygenation through (1) proper positioning and frequent rest periods and (2) encouraging coughing and deep breathing.

• As a support worker, you must be familiar with oxygen therapy and aware of the scope of practice for support workers in your province or territory.

• You may be responsible for assisting with oxygenation assessment (such as pulse oximetry) and diagnostic testing (such as collecting sputum samples).

• If it is within your scope of practice in your province or territory, you may be asked to support a client who (1) has a tracheostomy, (2) may need suctioning, (3) is mechanically ventilated, or (4) has a chest tube.

## Heat Applications

Heat applications can be applied to almost any body part. They are often used for musculoskeletal injuries or conditions—for example, strains, low back pain, and arthritis (see [TABLE 40.1](#t0010)). Heat applications in hospitals and clinics include the use of blanket warmers, which are warming cabinets designed to heat bed linens for chilled clients who are recovering from anaesthetic, receiving chemotherapy, or are too ill or frail to raise their own body temperature. Like all heat applications, it is important to ensure the linens are not so hot as to cause burns, by adjusting the cabinet heating temperature and testing the linens by placing a corner of them against the inner aspect of your wrist.

When heat is applied to skin, blood vessels in the area dilate (i.e., they expand or open wider) (see [FIGURE 40.1, B](#f0010)), allowing more blood to flow through them. This extra blood brings more oxygen and nutrients to the tissues for healing and removes excess fluid and wastes from the area faster. The extra blood causes the overlying skin to become red and warm to the touch. When heat is used correctly, muscles in that area relax, and painful swelling is reduced.

### Complications

Burns result when heat is applied either for too long or at too high a temperature. Pain, excessive redness, and blisters are danger signs of burns. If you observe any of these signs, you should remove the application and report your observations immediately. If not treated right away, burns can lead to serious, life-threatening conditions.

Some clients are at greater risk than others for burns and other complications from heat applications (see the Think About Safety: Clients at High Risk for Complications From Heat or Cold Applications box). It is your responsibility to observe these clients closely and to protect them from injury. Follow the guidelines listed in [BOX 40.1](#b0010) when performing heat and cold applications.

### Moist and Dry Heat Applications

Moist heat application involves water coming in contact with the skin. Moist heat has certain advantages, including the following:

1. • Water conducts heat, so moist heat has a greater and faster effect than dry heat.
2. • Heat penetrates deeper with moist applications, so they have lower (cooler) temperature settings than dry heat applications and thus help prevent injury to the client.

Heating or Bean Bags

The use of heating bags (or bean bags) has become very popular because they are easy to use and inexpensive. They are often sold under the trade name of Magic Bag. They can even be made at home. These bags are warmed in the microwave, but the length of warming time depends on size, components, and the heating power of the client’s microwave. If the client’s care plan instructs you to apply them, you should
always do the following:

1. • Heat the bag in the microwave only for the length of time specified in the client’s care plan.
2. • Never leave the heating bag unattended while it is heating in the microwave. The contents can spark or even catch on fire (similar to microwave popcorn)! Always remove the bag immediately if you begin to smell something burning.
3. • After heating, shake the bag in order to mix the beans inside, as they usually heat unevenly.
4. • Never place these bags directly on skin. They should always be wrapped in a pad or towel to avoid injuring the client. Check with your supervisor if you are unsure of the type of cover you should use.

Heating Pads, Electric Blankets, and Hot-Water Bottles

Some home care clients may have electric heating pads or hot-water bottles that apply dry heat. These items create serious fire and burn risks,
so support workers are usually not permitted to apply electric heating pads or hot-water bottles.
 If a client asks you to apply an electric heating pad or hot-water bottle, explain that your employer’s policy does not allow you to do this. Find another way to provide warmth to the client—such as giving a blanket or a sweater—and tell your supervisor about the client’s request. If the client insists on using a heating pad or hot-water bottle while you are on duty, the client may need to sign a legal waiver (sometimes called a “Negotiated Risk Agreement”) with your supervisor before you can apply it.

### Warm Soaks

A warm soak is a warm, moist application that involves immersing a body part into heated water to promote circulation and muscle relaxation. The soak lasts 15 minutes. Warm soaks can be used for smaller body parts, such as a hand, lower arm, foot, or lower leg ([FIGURE 40.3](#f0020)), or they can be used for larger body areas. A tub is used to soak larger parts (arm, leg, or torso). Maintain the client’s comfort and body alignment during the warm soak.

For any type of warm soak, remember to check the water temperature before soaking the body part(s). If the water is too hot, the skin can be burned as soon as it touches the water. If a water thermometer is not available, test the water by dipping your inner wrist in the water. If the water feels too warm, add some cold water and mix it in well. Change the water, as necessary, to correct the temperature. Never add hot water while the body part is in the basin because you can burn the client. Instead, have the client remove the body part from the basin, assisting as necessary. Then you can safely add more hot water. Test the temperature again before assisting the client to put the body part back into the soak.

The sitz bath is also considered to be a warm soak. It involves immersing the perineal and rectal areas in warm or hot water (sitz in German means “seat”). Sitz baths, usually lasting for about 15 minutes, are used after pelvic or rectal trauma or surgery,

Some agencies use bidets instead of sitz baths. With bidets, the perineal and rectal areas are not immersed, but warm water gently flows over the area. Bidets have many of the beneficial effects of sitz baths. A sitz tub is a built-in fixture with a deep seat. The client sits in a seat filled with water ([FIGURE 40.4](#f0025)), with the feet resting flat on the floor. Do not use an ordinary bathtub for a sitz bath, as immersing the legs and feet decreases the effectiveness of the procedure.

The disposable plastic sitz bath fits onto the toilet seat ([FIGURE 40.5](#f0030)). Plastic tubing runs from the bowl of the sitz bath to a water bag, and a plastic clamp closes the tubing. The sitz bath is filled with water at an appropriate temperature. (Check with your supervisor to determine a safe temperature.) The water bag is filled with warmer water. As the water in the sitz bath cools, the tubing is unclamped to allow the warmer water from the bag to flow into the sitz bath.

As blood flow to other body parts is reduced and blood flow to the perineal and rectal areas increases, the client may feel chilled. Cover the client’s shoulders and knees for warmth. Reduced blood flow may also make the client feel faint, dizzy, weak, or drowsy. Check the client every 5 minutes for these signs. Stay in the room if it is not safe to leave the client alone. Allow as much privacy as possible. If it is safe to leave, place the call bell within the client’s reach, or remain within easy hearing distance.

## Cold Applications

Cold applications are often used to treat sprains, fractures, and fever ([BOX 40.2](#b0015)). They reduce pain, prevent swelling, and decrease circulation and bleeding. Cold applications also cool the body when fever is present.

![Close-up of warm pack wrapped around client's forearm, secured with Velcro straps.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000407/main.assets/f40-08-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 40.8 Warm pack secured with Velcro.

Cold has the opposite effect of heat on body functions. When cold is applied to the skin, blood vessels constrict (see [FIGURE 40.1, C](#f0010), on page 960), resulting in decreased blood flow to the area and less oxygen and fewer nutrients being carried to the tissues. Cold applications are useful immediately after an injury. The decreased circulation reduces the amount of bleeding, bruising, and swelling, and cold has a numbing effect on the skin, which helps reduce or relieve pain in the affected part.

### Complications

Cold applications should never be used on old injuries, as they can actually cause tissue damage, since the tissue over the old injury may be less sensitive to the cold. This tissue damage is called frostbite—a medical condition in which damage is caused to skin and other tissues due to extreme cold. Signs of frostbite include blisters; pale, white, or grey skin; cyanosis; shivering; numbness; pain; and burning.

Complications of cold applications include pain, burns, blisters, and cyanosis. Burns and blisters tend to occur from applications being in direct contact with skin. It is
your responsibility
 to check clients every 5 minutes, or more frequently for those who are at high risk for complications (see the Think About Safety: Clients at High Risk From Heat or Cold Applications box, on p. 964), and to follow the guidelines listed in [BOX 40.1](#b0010) on page 963 in order to prevent injuries from cold applications. Be sure to peek under the application to inspect the skin.

BOX 40.2 Focus on First Aid

Remember RICE for recent injuries.

If someone you know or are caring for has suffered a recent injury, you should remember the acronym that you probably learned in your first-aid class: “RICE.”

* R: Rest
   the limb. Do not try to use it.
* I:Ice.
   Apply ice packs to reduce swelling and decrease pain.
* C: Compression.
   Wrap a compression bandage, designed to provide pressure to a particular area (also known as a tensor bandage), around the affected part, ensuring that it is not too tight. Signs that it is too tight include tingling and numbness, followed by increased pain and poor circulation (loss of colour) of the skin in the extremities of the affected body part.
* E: Elevate
   the limb to reduce possible swelling.

Remember that the client should be assessed by a health care provider as soon as possible to determine if a fracture has occurred!

In general, cold applications should be applied and removed in 15-minute intervals (15 minutes on and 15 minutes off). Timely removal of the cold application helps prevent tissue damage resulting from overconstricted blood vessels.

### Moist and Dry Cold Applications

Cold applications can be moist or dry:

1. • Dry cold—ice bag, ice collar, or ice glove
2. • Moist cold—cold compress
3. • Moist or dry—cold packs

Moist cold applications penetrate deeper than dry ones, so the temperatures of moist applications should not be as low as those of dry applications. You need to check the skin frequently (at least every 5 minutes, or more often, as directed by your supervisor).

### Ice Bags, Ice Collars, Ice Gloves, and Dry Cold Packs

Ice bags, ice collars, and ice gloves are dry cold applications. The devices are filled with crushed ice.

Both reusable and single-use (disposable) cold packs are available commercially. To activate the cold, follow the manufacturer’s instructions. You may need to strike, knead, or squeeze the pack. After use, reusable cold packs are cleaned and then kept in the freezer.

Devices with an outer covering can be placed directly on skin. Devices that come without a cover should be wrapped in a towel, pillowcase, or dishcloth. When the covering becomes moist, remove it and apply a dry one (see Focus on Home Care: Ice Packs).

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Before you perform these procedures, it is
your responsibility
 to make sure of the following: (1) your provincial or territorial laws and employer’s policies allow you to perform the procedure, (2) the procedure is in your job description, (3) you have the necessary training, (4) you are familiar with the equipment, and (5) you have reviewed the procedure with a nurse.

• Heat and cold have opposite effects on body functions. Whereas heat increases blood flow by dilating blood vessels in the area, cold slows blood flow by constricting blood vessels. You must thoroughly understand the purposes, effects, and complications of both heat and cold applications.

• Heat and cold applications can cause severe injuries, including a decrease in the ability of that body part to function well.

• Because of the risks, heat and cold applications should be used only if they are in the client’s care plan. Support workers must not apply heat or cold unless delegated to do so by a nurse.

• Inspect the skin beneath the application every 5 minutes or according to the care plan. Heat or cold applications are not applied for longer than 15 minutes at a time.

• Not every client will report discomfort, so watch for signs of discomfort, such as restlessness, agitation, or other changes in behaviour.

• Both heat and cold applications can be either moist or dry. Water conducts heat and cold, so moist therapies have a greater and faster effect than dry therapies.

• Clients at greatest risk for complications from heat or cold applications include (1) clients with thin, delicate, or fragile skin; (2) clients who have decreased sensation; (3) clients with dementia or confusion; and (4) clients with metal implants.

The postpartum—after (post) childbirth (partum)—period starts with the birth of the baby and ends 6 weeks later. During this time, the birth parent begins to adjust physically and emotionally to the effects of childbirth, and the uterus and the rest of the client’s body begin to return to their normal (prepregnant) state. Because of the many changes experienced during the postpartum period, the client needs to rest and recover. Birth parents need to be encouraged to rest or nap when the baby is sleeping and take time for themselves and their partner. During this time, the client also has important nutritional needs (see [Chapter 27](../B9780323709392000274/B9780323709392000274.xhtml)).

### Lochia

In the first few weeks to months after giving birth, the birth parent’s hormone levels will change dramatically, and the uterus will begin to contract to its prepregnant size. Blood and other substances will be expelled from the uterus; this vaginal discharge, called lochia (from the Greek word lochos, which means “childbirth”), is made up of blood, mucus, and placental tissue.

Lochia will typically increase with breastfeeding (nursing) and activity, but the amount of flow should return to normal after the client rests. The client wears clean sanitary pads to absorb the discharge, which eventually decreases in amount.

Lochia generally has an odour similar to that of normal menstrual discharge. Any offensive odour indicates a possible infection and should be reported to a health care provider. The client should shower daily and change sanitary pads frequently (at least every 4 hours). An easy way to ensure the pad is changed frequently is to advise the client to change their sanitary pad each time they urinate, practise hand hygiene before and after changing a sanitary pad (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)), and check lochia for signs of infection (such as an offensive odour and greenish colour). As the support worker assisting the client, you should check for fever and pelvic pain, which are also signs of infection.

Lochia typically continues for 4 to 6 weeks after childbirth and progresses through three stages. These
stages ([FIGURE 41.1](#f0010)) describe the changes in the colour of the postpartum flow:

  —the first discharge, red because of the large amount of blood it contains. It typically lasts no longer than 3 to 5 days after childbirth.
* 2.
  Lochia serosa (or “watery” lochia)
  —thinned discharge that has turned brownish or pink. It contains blood, water, red blood cells, white blood cells, and cervical mucus, which is a viscous discharge secreted by the glands in the cervix. This stage continues until around the 10th day after delivery.
* 3.
  Lochia alba (or “white” lochia)
  —discharge that has turned whitish or yellowish-white. It typically lasts from the second through the third to sixth week after delivery. It contains fewer red blood cells and is made up mainly of leukocytes, epithelial cells, cholesterol, fat, and mucus.

It is important to note that the birth parent should not use tampons during the postpartum period, as the risk of toxic shock syndrome (TSS) is very great, especially if the birth parent had an episiotomy or vaginal tear. TSS is a rare, often life-threatening illness that develops suddenly after an infection. It can quickly affect several different organ systems, including the liver, lungs, and kidneys.

### Perineal Care and Episiotomies

An episiotomy is an incision (otomy) in the perineum performed to increase the size of the vaginal opening for delivery of the baby or to decrease the risk of perineal tearing. The physician or midwife performs this procedure, and the incision is sutured after the delivery. After the episiotomy, the perineal area may become swollen, sore, and tender. The episiotomy prevents perineal tearing, which will cause severe pain, swelling, or more severe problems in the birth parent’s perineal area. Unfortunately, in spite of every precaution, perineal tearing does occur during childbirth in some clients, and repair and suturing are then needed. As a result, clients who have had perineal tearing and
subsequent repair may be quite bruised and sore and may require a longer healing time.

Some birthing clients do not require an episiotomy, especially if (1) they have had previous vaginal deliveries; (2) they experience a very long labour, allowing the perineum to have an opportunity to stretch sufficiently; or (3) the client’s doctor or birthing midwife thinks it is unnecessary.

Complications can develop after an episiotomy or vaginal tearing and suturing, so it is very important that the client practise strict perineal care and proper hand hygiene (see [Chapter 17](../B9780323709392000171/B9780323709392000171.xhtml)) while they have lochia. They should be instructed to clean the perineum by using a squirt bottle filled with warm water, and then wiping away—from the front to the back—all remaining drainage with a clean, wet cloth (a baby wipe will be useful), change their sanitary pad, and practise hand hygiene each time they urinate. Complications of an episiotomy include infection and wound separation (dehiscence). Tell your supervisor if the client complains of pain in the perineum.

The physician may order pain relief medications and cold packs to the perineum for the client’s comfort. Physicians may also order sitz baths to promote comfort and hygiene (see [Chapter 40](../B9780323709392000407/B9780323709392000407.xhtml)). A warm bath also may relieve perineal pain and soreness. Make sure that the tub is disinfected before each use, and have clean washcloths and towels available for the client.

The client will be advised by their doctor, nurse practitioner, or midwife about the safe time to resume sexual activity, which is usually 6 weeks after delivery.

### Care of Caesarean Section Incisions

Some clients may have had a Caesarean section. A Caesarean section (C-section) is a surgical incision into the abdomen and uterine wall through which the baby is delivered. A C-section is done in the following situations:

1. • The baby must be delivered quickly to save the baby’s or the birth parent’s life.
2. • The baby is too large to pass through the birth canal or is in a position that would make vaginal delivery difficult or dangerous to either the baby or the birth parent.
3. • The birth parent has a vaginal infection that could be transmitted to the baby.
4. • A vaginal delivery is expected to cause difficulties for the baby or the birth parent. In consultation between the birth parent and their doctor or midwife, a C-section delivery may have been deemed the best course of action.
5. • The client’s doctor recommends a C-section because of a previous C-section.

The birth parent requires recovery time, because not only have they just given birth, they also had a major surgery (C-section). In the first few weeks after surgery, the client may feel weak and tired. The physician may order the client to avoid any lifting or housework during at least the first week of recovery. As a support worker, you may be required to help with housekeeping tasks and child care, or you may have to bring the baby to the client for feedings.

The C-section incision also needs to heal (see [Chapter 22](../B9780323709392000225/B9780323709392000225.xhtml)). Tell your supervisor or nurse in charge if the client complains of pain around the incision site. Also, tell your supervisor or nurse in charge if you observe bleeding, redness, swelling, or drainage from the incision.

### Breast Care

Lactation—the process of producing and secreting milk from the breasts—usually begins around the third day after childbirth. Occasionally, the new birth parent’s breasts may become overfilled with milk. Engorged breasts are swollen, hard, and painful, but once breastfeeding is established or the milk dries up (if the client chooses not to breastfeed), engorgement decreases within 1 or 2 days. Cold packs applied to the breasts or warm showers promote comfort. A well-fitting nursing bra worn day and night supports the breasts and increases comfort during engorgement. Follow the care plan to promote the client’s comfort.

Although uncommon, some birth parents are unable to breastfeed for a variety of reasons. Clients experiencing this difficulty may be very upset by this and will need to be supported.

Sometimes, clients feel a tender lump in a breast, which usually is a symptom of a plugged duct. Milk drains into ducts that open onto the nipple (see [FIGURE 32.34](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml), on page 748). When the milk does not drain properly through the duct, it builds up within
the breast. An untreated plugged duct can cause a breast infection. Tell your supervisor if you suspect the client has a plugged duct. Treatment usually involves:

### Postpartum Blues, Depression, and Psychosis

The postpartum period is also a time of emotional changes. Lack of sleep, more responsibilities, and their new role may affect the new birth parent’s moods. These and other issues such as isolation, disappointment, anxiety, poor body image, and lack of support from a partner or spouse, as well as the changes in hormone levels, may contribute to postpartum blues (“baby blues”), which are feelings of sadness or mild depression during the first 2 weeks after childbirth.

Common symptoms of postpartum blues include the following:

1. • Insomnia
2. • Mood changes
3. • Weepiness
4. • Fatigue
5. • Headaches
6. • Poor concentration
7. • Feelings of sadness, anger, or anxiety
8. • Agitation or irritability
9. • Changes in appetite
10. • Feeling withdrawn or unconnected
11. • Lack of pleasure or interest in most or all activities
12. • Loss of concentration

Health Canada estimates that up to 80% of Canadians who give birth experience postpartum blues.[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur473) Symptoms usually begin in the first days after delivery and disappear, even without treatment, after 1 to 2 weeks. Your encouragement and emotional support during this time may be very helpful to the client.

A smaller but still significant number of Canadian women suffer postpartum depression—about 10 to 15%.[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur474) Postpartum depression is major depression that begins any time within the first year after childbirth (see [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml) for a discussion of major depression). Usually, it begins within 2 weeks to 6 months after childbirth. Professional care is needed as soon as possible, as postpartum depression can worsen over time.

Postpartum psychosis is a severe form of postpartum depression. It is relatively rare, affecting about 1
woman per 1 000.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur475) The birth parent with postpartum psychosis loses touch with reality and has delusions, hallucinations, or suicidal thoughts (see [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml)). The client could harm or neglect the child, so they must not be left alone with the infant or other children.
Tell your supervisor or the nurse in charge immediately if the birth parent shows any of the signs or symptoms listed in
 [BOX 41.3](#b0020).

A client diagnosed with postpartum depression may be ordered medications and may need help with home management or child care so that they have time to rest. As the support worker assisting the client, your understanding and emotional support are essential at this time.

## Caring for Infants

Since infants are helpless and cannot care for or protect themselves and must depend on others for their basic needs, as a support worker, you must ensure their safety (see [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)) as well as meet their physical and emotional needs while they are in your care. Because infants have a delicate immune system, it is important for everyone to remember to practise proper hand hygiene (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)) each time infants are touched or picked up.

Newborns and infants change and grow quickly, and their physical and emotional needs will also change as they grow. Review the normal growth and development patterns of newborns and infants (see [Chapter 31](../B9780323709392000316/B9780323709392000316.xhtml)). Follow the care plan to meet the infant’s needs.

### Holding the Infant

It is important to pay particular attention to hygienic measures when handling infants. You should always practise proper hand hygiene before and after handling the infant. You need to wear a clean top (or gown) for each baby that you hold. If you are caring for a client and her baby in a home care setting, you should, if feasible, change into your clean uniform when you arrive at the client’s house. If changing is not feasible, you should protect your clean uniform by wearing a covering garment over it until you are in the client’s home and are about to pick up the infant.

Most infants are comforted when they are held and cuddled, and it helps them feel loved and secure. Always handle a baby with gentle, smooth movements; avoid sudden or jerking movements, as these may startle or upset the baby.

Laying the Infant Down to Sleep

When laying the baby down to sleep, safety precautions must be taken to prevent sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS)—the sudden, unexplained death, usually during sleep, of an apparently healthy infant under 1 year of age.

When laying the baby down to sleep, remember the following:

1. •
   Always lay babies on their backs for sleep.
    Babies who sleep on their stomachs or sides have an increased risk of SIDS. However, some babies have medical conditions that require them to sleep on their stomachs, so it is important to check with your supervisor and the care plan. Babies can lie on their stomachs when they are awake and being supervised.
2. •
   Do not lay the baby on soft bedding products. These include fluffy, plush products such as sheepskin pillows, quilts, comforters, memory foam mattresses or mattress pads, or soft toys. Remove these items from the crib. Soft products might cause large amounts of carbon dioxide to pool around the baby’s head, which may result in SIDS. The soft bedding also can cover the baby’s nose and mouth and cause suffocation.
3. •

### Cradle Cap, Diaper Rash, and Thrush

Many babies get “cradle cap,” diaper rash, or thrush. None of these issues is life-threatening, but each may cause discomfort to the baby.

#### Cradle Cap

Cradle cap (seborrheic dermatitis) is a skin condition in which yellowish, scaly, or crusty patches, made up largely of oil and dead skin cells, appear on the scalp. The condition is most common in infants, but it may be seen occasionally in children up to the age of 5 years. These patches appear most often on the scalp and may even extend onto the forehead. They may also appear in the skin fold behind the baby’s ears, on the ears, or in the diaper area. The most typical location is over the soft spot (anterior fontanelle) on the top of the baby’s head.

Cradle cap is quite common and not difficult to treat. Mild cases usually clear up when the baby is shampooed daily with regular baby soap applied on a wet, rough facecloth wrapped around your hand. Remember to always rinse the soap completely from the baby’s skin. Check the baby’s care plan or check with your supervisor for details on how to provide care for cradle cap on an infant. One common treatment is to soften the crusts first by massaging a small amount of baby oil into the baby’s scalp and letting it remain there overnight.

#### Diaper Rash

Diaper rash is a very common condition that most babies will develop at one time or another. Diaper rashes may be caused by medications (such as antibiotics), moisture, urine, or irritating chemicals in diapers (cloth or disposable). When you observe a rash on the baby you are caring for, make a note of its appearance, location, and other typical symptoms.

There are different types of rashes that have different causes. Always remember to practise proper hand hygiene before and after changing the baby ([BOX 41.5](#b0030)). If it is stated in the baby’s care plan, you may have to wear gloves for changing the baby’s diaper.
Always report a rash that is new, a rash that seems to be spreading in spite of treatment, or one that is present for longer than a day.
 Common types of rashes include the following:

1. •
   Simple diaper rash.
    Simple diaper rashes are red, slightly rough, and scaly and may appear over the whole area touched by the diaper. The skin may be irritated by chemicals used in laundering cloth diapers—detergent, bleach, whitener, water softener, or soap. Plastic or rubber pants worn over cloth diapers sometimes affect the skin. The skin may also react to the chemicals used in the manufacture of disposable diapers as well as in commercial baby wipes.
2. •
   Ammonia rash.
    Ammonia rash is a form of diaper rash caused by urine. The skin is literally burned by the ammonia that is formed when urine is decomposed by normal bacteria on the skin. Not surprisingly, ammonia rash is worse after the baby has been asleep for a long period without a diaper change. You can identify it by the noticeable ammonia smell when you change the diaper. The baby’s skin should be washed with mild soap and warm water and then dried thoroughly. Be sure to dry in between the baby’s skin folds.
3. •
   Other types.
    Besides these two basic types of diaper rashes, a variety of other rashes may appear in the diaper area, including those caused by an allergy to a food or a drug, a skin infection, or a contagious disease such as chicken pox or measles.

#### Thrush

Thrush is a common yeast infection that resembles cottage cheese or milk curds and is found on the sides, roof, and sometimes the tongue of a baby’s mouth
([FIGURE 41.4](#f0025)). Unlike milk residue, which leaves a white coating on the baby’s tongue that easily comes off, the white patches associated with thrush do not come off easily. If one of these patches is accidentally scraped away, you will find underneath a raw, red area that may bleed. Thrush is not life-threatening, but it can be very sore and would prevent the baby from feeding, which would lead to dehydration.

BOX 41.5 Treating Simple Diaper Rash or Ammonia Rash

1. • Always practise proper hand hygiene (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)).
2. • Keep the baby as dry as possible; change diapers frequently, even if they are only slightly wet; and avoid any airtight coverings.
3. • If using cloth diapers, use double diapers during the daytime and triple diapers at night.
4. • Wash the diaper area with plain water each time you change the baby. Be sure to remove any old creams that might remain on the baby’s skin. Dry the baby’s skin thoroughly, taking care to dry the baby’s skin folds well. If instructed in the baby’s care plan, apply a protective cream or ointment (such as zinc oxide, petroleum jelly, or an ointment combining zinc oxide, cod liver oil, petrolatum, and lanolin). Use only one type of ointment at a time unless the client’s care plan has instructed you to use more than one.
5. • Do not dust the baby’s skin with cornstarch, a practice that was once commonly recommended; it has been found to encourage the growth of fungi.
6. • Try a different brand of laundry soap on cloth diapers. Do not use fabric softener with every wash because the baby may be sensitive to a buildup of it.
7. • Give cloth diapers a try if you have been using disposables, or switch brands. Try disposables if you have been using cloth diapers.
8. • Cut down on the use of commercial baby wipes, powders, and oils for the baby, and ensure that whatever products you use are mild and nonallergenic.
9. • To further promote healing, place a pad under the baby and let the baby lie undiapered, whenever possible, to expose the baby’s skin to air.

You may first suspect thrush if the baby starts crying while breastfeeding or sucking on a pacifier or a bottle. It is most common in babies age 2 months and younger but can appear in older babies, too.
If the baby develops a fever, your supervisor should be notified immediately, as the baby may have another type of infection.

##### Cause

Yeast is present in the human body as the normal flora of the digestive system, but when the balance is disturbed, an infection sets in. Sometimes, hormonal changes or the use of antibiotics (in the birth parent or the baby) during the period of breastfeeding can cause this imbalance.

Some babies, such as low-birth-weight babies, premature babies, babies born to birth parents with diabetes, or babies with some type of congenital (present at birth) disorder are more susceptible to infections than others are. Some babies are infected by their birth parent during delivery through the birth canal.

##### Treatment

If you suspect the baby has thrush, you should immediately report it to your supervisor. Some doctors may prescribe medicated drops that the parent
must place in the baby’s mouth before feeding. If the client is breastfeeding the baby, the client may be prescribed an antifungal cream to be applied on the nipples so that client and the baby will not re-infect each other. It may take a week for the thrush to clear up after medication has been started. It is important that everyone who has contact with the baby practise proper hand hygiene. Soothers (also called pacifiers) may trap the yeast particles and re-infect the baby, so they should be replaced with either new soothers or ones that have been properly sterilized.

### Signs and Symptoms of Potentially Serious Illness

Infants can become ill quickly.
 Signs and symptoms may be sudden, so you must be very alert to note them. Your observations are important for the infant’s safety and well-being. Tell your supervisor when the sign or symptom began. If the infant has any of the signs or symptoms listed in [BOX 41.6](#b0035), tell your supervisor immediately. Your supervisor will tell you which care method to use.

You may need to take an infant’s or child’s temperature and count respirations (see [Chapter 24](../B9780323709392000249/B9780323709392000249.xhtml)). Axillary (between skin folds in the armpit) temperatures are taken on infants, and tympanic (external ear canal) or axillary temperatures are taken on children younger than 5 years. Apical (over the apex of the heart) pulses are taken on both infants and young children.

Whether the baby is being bottle-fed or breastfed, without sufficient feeding, the baby can become dehydrated. ### Helping Parents Bottle-Feed

Formula milk is given to infants who are not breastfed. It provides the essential nutrients the infant needs. Formula comes in three forms: The ready-to-feed formula is poured directly from the can into the baby bottle ([FIGURE 41.6](#f0035)); water is added to powdered and concentrated formulas. The formula container has directions about the amount of formula and amount of water to be added.

Infants under 4 months of age are especially at risk for a severe, even life-threatening diarrheal infection if the bottles and other items have not been sterilized. It is important that all items that come into contact with the infant’s formula be sterilized. Preboiled, cooled tap water should be mixed with the formula. All mixing containers, spoons, funnels, forceps, nipples, and bottles that are used in formula preparation or feeding must be sterilized (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml), p. 399, for tips on how to sterilize baby bottles and equipment). Boil the tap water that is to be mixed with the concentrated formula or formula powder for at least 2 minutes to destroy microbes. Use tap water from the cold tap. Check with the supervisor before using hot tap water, well water, bottled water, or water that has been filtered or treated. Follow the care plan and contact your supervisor if you have any questions.

Bottles are prepared one at a time or in batches for the whole day. Bottles not needed immediately are capped ([FIGURE 41.7](#f0040)) and stored in the refrigerator. The prepared bottles should be used within 24 hours. After 24 hours, the formula must be discarded.

#### Cleaning the Equipment

Baby bottles, caps, and nipples must be thoroughly cleaned. Reusable bottle-feeding equipment is carefully washed in hot, soapy water or in a dishwasher. Complete rinsing is needed to remove all of the soap. Some clients use plastic nursers, which require plastic liners that are used once and then discarded.

#### Bottle-Feeding the Infant

Bottle-fed babies usually want to be fed every 3 to 4 hours. The care plan or the parent will tell you how much formula a baby needs at each feeding. Babies usually take as much formula as they need; however, a newborn should not take in more than 60–90 mL with each feeding because of the small size of the baby’s stomach. When full, the baby stops sucking and turns away from the bottle.

### Burping the Infant

Babies swallow some air during feeding, whether they are breastfed or bottle-fed. However, bottle-fed babies swallow more air. Air in the stomach and intestines will cause the baby discomfort and cramping, which can lead to fussiness and vomiting. Burping helps get rid of the swallowed air. Most babies need to be burped after they have finished half the formula, after which they may want to continue feeding. They should be burped again at the end of the feeding.

There are three ways to position the baby for burping ([FIGURE 41.13](#f0070)). One way is to hold the baby over your shoulder. Place a clean diaper or towel over your shoulder to protect your clothing in case the baby “spits up.” The second way is to support the baby in a sitting position on your lap. If the baby is younger than 3 months, support the head and neck by cupping your hand under the baby’s chin. Hold a towel or diaper in front of the baby. Gently pat the baby’s back or rub it in circular motions until the baby burps, which may take up to 5 minutes. The third way is to sit, then lay the baby on their stomach across your knees and pat their back.

### Diapering

Babies usually urinate several times a day. A baby who is only breastfed usually has stools that are bright yellow, are sweet smelling, and have the consistency of scrambled eggs. Breastfed babies usually have bowel movements after feedings. If you introduce anything other than breast milk into a baby’s diet—for example, formula or solids—then the stools will change, usually to become more like an adult’s (more brown, solid, and foul-smelling). Until they are fed solid foods, babies usually have stools that are soft and unformed. Bottle-fed babies may have three bowel movements a day.

Hard, formed stools indicate that the baby has constipation, and watery stools indicate diarrhea, which is very serious in babies, as their fluid balance can be upset quickly (see [Chapter 27](../B9780323709392000274/B9780323709392000274.xhtml)). Inform your supervisor immediately if you suspect a baby has constipation or diarrhea.

Diapers should be changed when they get wet or soiled, usually after a feeding. Parents may use cloth or disposable diapers. Cloth diapers may be fastened with diaper pins or Velcro strips. They are covered with plastic pants to protect against leaks. When changing a soiled cloth diaper, empty the stool into the toilet if possible. Place used diapers and plastic pants in the diaper pail to be laundered later. They should be washed in hot water with a laundry detergent made especially for baby clothes. Putting them
through the wash cycle a second time without detergent helps remove all soap. They should be dried thoroughly and folded for reuse.

### Care of the Umbilical Cord

The umbilical cord connects the pregnant client and the fetus (unborn baby) and carries blood, oxygen, and nutrients from the birth parent to the fetus ([FIGURE 41.17](#f0090)). The umbilical cord is not needed after birth, so shortly after delivery, the physician or midwife clamps and cuts the cord. The stump of cord that is left on the baby dries up and falls off in 7 to 14 days. Slight bleeding may occur when the cord comes off. Do not pull the cord off—even if it looks ready to fall off.

The cord stump provides an area for the growth of microbes, so you need to ensure that it is clean and dry at all times. Cord care is done with each sponge bath and is continued for 1 to 2 days after the cord comes off. The care plan and your supervisor will advise you on cord care, which usually consists of the following[9](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur479):

1. • Keep the cord dry.
2. • Wash your hands before and after contact with the umbilical area.
3. • Keep the cord clean using mild soap and water. Gently wipe around the base of the cord with a clean, small facecloth or a gauze square moistened with warm water ([FIGURE 41.18](#f0095)). (Note: Alcohol swabs are not recommended.)
4. • Keep the top of the diaper below the cord to prevent the diaper from irritating the cord and to prevent the cord from being contaminated by urine.
5. ![Diagram shows lateral view of mother's body with a cross-section of abdomen depicting baby in head-down position connected to the mother with an umbilical cord.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000419/main.assets/f41-17-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 41.17 The umbilical cord connects the birth parent and the fetus.
6. • Repeat cord care daily and any time the cord gets wet or soiled.
7. • Report any signs of infection, such as redness, odour, or drainage from the cord.
8. • Give sponge baths to the baby until the cord falls off, after which the baby can be given a tub bath. In some provinces, it is now acceptable practice to immerse newborns into a shallow tub of water, providing the cord does not get soaked.

   It is important to check with your supervisor what the practice is for your province and whether giving a newborn a tub bath is included in your scope of practice.

### Caring for Uncircumcised and Circumcised Babies[10](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur480)

Circumcision is the surgical removal of foreskin from the penis. Not all infants with male genitalia are circumcised, as the parents decide whether or not they want the procedure done. The procedure promotes hygiene and is thought to prevent certain cancers. Circumcision is usually done in the hospital before the baby goes home. In the Jewish faith, the procedure is mandatory and is conducted as a religious ceremony by a mohel (one who performs circumcision on a Jewish male as a religious rite).

The uncircumcised penis can easily be kept clean by gently washing the genital area while bathing. Do not apply antiseptics of any kind. In uncircumcised baby boys, the foreskin
usually does not retract for several years and should never be forced
. Whether circumcised or not, the penis should be thoroughly cleaned at each diaper change, especially after the baby has a bowel movement. Use mild soap and water or commercial wipes.

After circumcision, a gauze square covered in petroleum jelly may be applied on the baby’s penis. In some provinces, it is common practice to wait until this gauze falls off, whereas in other provinces, the gauze square is removed (it may need to be soaked off) and replaced at each diaper change. It is important to check with your supervisor or the baby’s care plan as to what is appropriate for the baby. The penis will look red, swollen, and sore for the first couple of days after the surgery, but the circumcision does not interfere with urination. The area should be completely healed in 10 to 14 days. Report observations of bleeding, odour, or drainage.

![Hand of a person applying petroleum jelly using an ear bud on the circumcised penis.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000419/main.assets/f41-19-9780323711654.jpg)

If the baby has been circumcised, apply the diaper loosely to prevent the diaper from irritating the penis. Some physicians recommend carefully applying a teaspoonful of petroleum jelly to the penis to protect it from urine and stool. It also prevents the penis from sticking to the diaper. Use a cotton swab to carefully apply the petroleum jelly ([FIGURE 41.19](#f0100)). Your supervisor will tell you if other measures are needed.

### Bathing the Infant

It should be part of the baby’s daily routine. Some parents like to bathe their babies in the morning, and some in the evening. Follow the family’s routine.

Planning and preparation are an important part of the bath routine. You cannot leave the baby alone because you forgot something and have to go and get it. Gather all necessary equipment, supplies, and the baby’s clothes before you start the bath, and place everything you need within your reach.

### Nail Care

The baby’s fingernails and toenails should be kept short; otherwise, the baby could scratch themselves and others. However, if the care plan or supervisor instructs you to cut the nails, extreme care should be taken not to cut them too short, since the nail tips are highly vascular and could bleed a lot if accidentally cut. Some doctors suggest gently tearing or ripping the nails to keep them short. In some cultures, birth parents are taught to bite off their baby’s nails to shorten them. If you do choose to cut them, nails are best cut when the baby is sleeping so that the baby does not squirm or fuss during the procedure. Use nail clippers or file nails with an emery board. If using nail clippers, clip nails in a gentle curve as for an adult.

### Weighing Infants

Infants are weighed at birth since the birth weight is the baseline for measuring the infant’s growth. Your supervisor will tell you when to weigh the baby. ## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Support workers sometimes give care to new birth parents and infants, usually in a home care setting. Providing this service leaves the birth parent more time and energy for themselves and their family.

• Respect the family’s routines, schedules, and ways of doing things. As much as possible, follow the parent’s standards and preferences. As with all of the duties that you perform, follow your clients’ care plan and know the limits of your scope of practice.

• The postpartum period starts with the birth of the baby and ends 6 weeks later.

• As a support worker, you need to know how to care for a new birth parent and baby in the following areas: (1) lochia, (2) perineal care, (3) abdominal incision care, (4) breast care, (5) holding the infant, (6) comforting the infant, (7) assisting with breastfeeding, (8) preparing infant formula, (9) feeding the baby, (10) diapering the baby, (11) caring for the umbilical cord, (12) caring for a circumcised or uncircumcised baby’s penis, (13) bathing the baby, and (14) weighing the baby.

• As a support worker, you must be able to identify abnormal findings during the care of birth parents and infants and should know who to report these findings to.
