    Key Points

    • Regardless of where you work, you should be familiar with the scope of practice for support workers that are identified by the educational standards in your province or territory and by the policies of your employer, and you should always stay within this role. If you are asked to perform a delegated task, you are responsible for following the correct steps to ensure client safety (Chapter 1). Agreeing to perform tasks that are beyond your support worker role can risk legal action.

    • The current trend within the Canadian health care system is to decrease hospital costs and to increase its community-based services.

    • With the increase of home- and community-based programs, there is an ever-growing demand for support workers.

    • Community-based services include the health care and support services provided outside of a facility and in a community setting such as (1) school boards, (2) community health centres, (3) doctors’ offices, (4) home care agencies, (5) day programs, and (6) live-in facilities.

    • You may work directly for a client or the client’s family. Such clients select and supervise their own support workers. Clients may pay support workers from their own resources, or their province may provide funding assistance to them.

    • Facility-based services include the health care and support services provided within facilities, such as hospitals or long-term care facilities.

    • There are positive aspects as well as challenges associated with any type of service, regardless of the setting.

    Critical Thinking in Practice

    As a recently graduated support worker, you must consider many things when determining which workplace setting would be most suitable for you. For example, some elements that you may wish to consider include the following:

    • Is the philosophy of care of the agency similar to your own?

    • Will you be able to arrive at your workplace punctually?

    • Does this agency have a set job description for what you would be expected to do?

    After reading this chapter, can you identify any other aspects that are important for you to consider when deciding on an employment setting?

    The term ethics refers to the rules of conduct that guide us when deciding what is right and what is wrong (immoral). These rules come from society’s expectation of how people should conduct themselves. Morals are the fundamental principles of behaviour each person believes to be right or wrong for themselves without concern for legalities or customs. Support workers must be aware that people’s culture and personal circumstances may influence their ethical beliefs, morals, and values, and all of these influence their actions. Values are what an individual considers to be worthwhile or desirable qualities in a person. A belief is a conviction that something is true.

    Ethics and morals are often incorrectly used to mean the same thing, and some people think of morals as focusing specifically on standards of behaviour related to sexual activity. Morals and ethics play a part in our everyday lives, and we apply them when making both big and small decisions. Whether we realize it or not, they have a great impact on our personal and professional relationships. As a support worker, when you have to make difficult choices or decisions, you will rely on your ethical beliefs, morals, and values to guide your conduct.

    The Influence of Culture on Beliefs, Values, Morals, and Ethics

    Most people would probably agree that it would be wrong, or unethical, to kill another person. However, what if you were a soldier, fighting to protect your country from a foreign invader? If you were to learn that, in the past, a client had to kill or injure other people in self-defence, would you consider the client’s act unethical?

    As Canadians, we are obligated to obey the laws that govern us (addressed in Chapter 5). As support workers, we should also understand that our clients’ values, beliefs, morals, ethics, and behaviours may have been influenced by their cultures and their life’s circumstances (also refer to Chapter 7).     BOX 4.1 A Sample Code of Ethics for Support Workers

    1. • Support workers provide high-quality personal care and support services. They work within their scope of practice. They promptly report to their supervisors any concerns and observations about clients’ health and well-being. They perform only those tasks for which they have received the necessary education. They know and follow employer policies as well as federal and provincial or territorial laws. They work within the parameters of national and provincial or territorial laws at all times.
    2. • The support worker needs to be aware of the policies and procedures for each area. If the support worker is employed for more than one client or agency, the required skills may vary. It should never be assumed that the skills allowed at one agency can be automatically transferred to another agency. When unsure, the support worker should always ask for direction from their supervisor or regulated health care professional.
    3. • Support workers provide compassionate care to all clients. They promote the client’s physical, emotional, intellectual, social, and spiritual well-being. They encourage clients to maintain as much independence as possible during times of normal health and in situations of illness, injury, or disability or while dying. They respect and promote the family’s roles and relationships.
    4. • Support workers value the dignity and worth of all clients. They advocate for their client whenever necessary. They strive to treat all clients in an honest, fair, and just manner. They do not discriminate against a client on the basis of age, race or ethnicity, colour, religion, sexual orientation, or culture.
    5. • Support workers respect their clients’ choices about how they receive or participate in their care. They respect and promote the client’s wishes and use discretion to avoid causing distress or embarrassment to a person (see the Supporting Mrs. Glass: Recognizing the Need for Discretion box).
    6. • Support workers respect their clients’ right to privacy and confidentiality. Information about the client learned while providing care is not shared with anyone outside the health care team.
    7. • Support workers do not misuse their position of trust. They do not accept gifts or tips from their clients. They do not buy property from their clients, nor do they sell products to them. They do not try to impose their own religious or other beliefs on their clients. Their behaviour toward clients and co-workers is professional at all times (see the Supporting Mr. Creishilo: Maintaining a Professional Relationship box).
    8. • Support workers are reliable. They arrive at work on time and complete all assignments. They are patient and courteous with clients. They notify their supervisor if they are going to be late or are unable to work, or if they are working in the community or in a home care setting, they inform their client. Their conduct is professional and based on ethical principles.
    9. • Support workers promote and maintain their clients’ safety. They report mistakes and unsafe situations immediately to their supervisors. They consider their clients’ safety when performing all tasks and activities.

    The four basic principles of health care ethics are as follows:

    1. • Autonomy—respecting the client’s right to make choices for themselves
    2. • Justice—being fair
    3. • Beneficence—doing good
    4. • Nonmaleficence—doing no harm

    Understanding the principles of health care ethics will help you think through situations and behave ethically toward your clients and co-workers.

    Autonomy

    Autonomy (also called self-determination) means having the freedom and choice to make decisions that affect one’s life. As long as a person is mentally competent, they have the right to make decisions about lifestyle and medical care and services. This concept is critical to health care ethics. Laws have been established to protect the client’s right to autonomy (see Chapter 5). For example, physicians, facilities, and agencies must, by law, ensure that clients give informed consent before any procedure is performed on them. Clients, therefore, decide what kind of treatment they want or do not want.

    BOX 4.2 Ethical Dilemmas

    Scenario 1

    You are assigned to work for a family with a 2-year-old boy and newborn twins. You are responsible for helping with care of the 2-year-old. The mother mentions that since the birth of the twins, the toddler has been throwing temper tantrums around lunchtime. She wants to stop this problem behaviour and tells you that if the toddler throws a tantrum or misbehaves, he is to be sent to his room for 15 minutes or longer if he has not settled down by the end of that time.

    Discussion

    Consider how applying the main principles of health care may cause conflict. To ensure the child’s safety, discuss this with the supervisor.

    • Autonomy : The mother has the right to make parenting decisions.
    • Justice: Would it be fair to the child to leave him alone in his room for so long?
    • Beneficence: Will the disciplining help the child improve his behaviour? Can other actions improve his behaviour?
    • Nonmaleficence: Could leaving the child in his room cause harm?

    Scenario 2

    Miki works in a long-term care facility. Mr. Petrova is a resident on her resident care unit. Miki smells alcohol on Mr. Petrova’s breath following his son’s visit. She comments on this to Mr. Petrova, and he tells her that his son had brought him a bottle of liquor. Residents keeping alcohol in their rooms is against facility rules. Mr. Petrova says that alcohol eases his pain and asks Miki to promise not to tell anyone about the liquor.

    Discussion

    Telling her supervisor about the liquor would disregard Mr. Petrova’s wishes and his autonomy. However, not telling her supervisor could harm Mr. Petrova. For example, alcohol may interfere with his medications or cause adverse effects. Not telling could also harm Miki. She could lose her job for knowing a resident has broken facility rules and not reporting it.

    Miki explains to Mr. Petrova that she is ethically and legally obligated to tell her supervisor about the bottle of liquor in his room. She explains her reasons, but Mr. Petrova still gets upset. Miki, however, knows that she has followed the ethical principle of nonmaleficence. Also, she has avoided betraying Mr. Petrova’s trust by refusing to make a promise she cannot keep.

    Scenario 3

    John’s home care client is Mrs. Jessop, whose care plan states that she is not allowed sugary foods because she has uncontrolled diabetes; however, she often snacks on sugary foods when she is by herself. Today is Mrs. Jessop’s birthday, and her neighbour brings her a cake. Mrs. Jessop tells John that every year on her birthday she chooses to go off her diet. She asks him to cut her a piece of cake. Serving Mrs. Jessop a piece of cake would respect her autonomy. However, the cake could harm Mrs. Jessop’s physical health.

    Discussion

    John decides not to serve the cake to Mrs. Jessop. He knows that she can serve herself or her neighbour can serve it to her. John says that he should perhaps call Mrs. Jessop’s case manager about the situation, but Mrs. Jessop does not want John to make the call. She goes ahead and eats the cake. John observes her carefully for any ill effects but notices no change in her condition. Later, he telephones his supervisor to report the incident. His written notes also describe what happened exactly.

    John upholds the principle of autonomy by allowing Mrs. Jessop to decide to eat the cake. He also upholds the principle of nonmaleficence by not serving her the cake and by advising that they consult her case manager before she eats a piece of cake. Because he has reported what happened, he has also followed the principle of beneficence.

    Scenario 4

    Tomas is a support worker in a long-term care facility. One afternoon, he observes that Mrs. O’Brian seems upset. He asks her if anything is wrong, and she starts to cry. She tells Tomas that her 17-year-old grandson has been arrested for drunk driving. She asks Tomas not to tell anyone because the family wants to keep the matter private. She also asks Tomas not to tell the nursing staff that she is upset.

    Keeping the information confidential and telling no one about her emotional state would respect Mrs. O’Brian’s privacy. However, Tomas is expected to report his observations about residents’ emotional health. Not doing so could cause Mrs. O’Brian harm since she may suffer from the effects of stress.

    Discussion

    Tomas decides to tell the charge nurse that Mrs. O’Brian is upset about a private family matter. First, though, Tomas explains to Mrs. O’Brian that he is required to report his observations of his clients’ emotional health. He also assures her that anything he reports will be kept confidential by the health care team.

    Tomas’s solution respects the ethical principles of autonomy and justice. It also upholds the principle of nonmaleficence. By reporting that Mrs. O’Brian is upset, Tomas ensures that someone with authority will take responsibility for Mrs. O’Brian’s emotional health.

    Scenario 5

    P.J. is a support worker caring for Mrs. Osillo, an older woman who has a “G-tube” for feeding. As P.J.’s supervisor explained to him, this tube goes directly into Mrs. Osillo’s stomach through an opening made in her abdomen, and all of her foods and nutrients are given through it. She cannot have anything to drink, as she has severe problems swallowing and any food or fluids would go into her lungs, resulting in respiratory problems and pneumonia. Mrs. Osillo has already been admitted to the hospital for pneumonia twice this year.

    Mrs. Osillo complains that she feels thirsty all the time and begs P.J. to give her something to drink.

    Discussion

    P.J. cannot give Mrs. Osillo anything to drink because doing so would violate the principle of nonmaleficence. Anything that is taken orally can cause severe problems for Mrs. Osillo. P.J. decides that he should discuss this situation with the supervisor so that together they can come up with a solution that respects Mrs. Osillo’s right to autonomy and that is in keeping with the principle of beneficence.

    After a discussion with the supervisor and Mrs. Osillo’s doctor, P.J. gives Mrs. Osillo ice chips to suck on several times a day. In between the ice chips, Mrs. Osillo is given mouthwashes to gargle and spit out and an oral spray, which keeps her oral membranes moist. Mrs. Osillo says that these solutions have helped get rid of the dry-mouth feeling that she had been experiencing all the time.

    As a support worker, you must always respect your clients’ choices and preferences. The client has autonomy even in the case of routine tasks; for example, if a client asks you to use his own blue sheets to make his bed, it is important that you do so. Using other sheets shows a lack of respect for the client’s choice. Even if a client wants a certain hairstyle that you think is unattractive, you must respect her choice. It would be unethical to ignore her preference and style her hair according to your own taste.

    Respecting a client’s autonomy becomes more complicated if you think their decisions are unsafe. The client has the right to make choices and to take risks. For example, an older client may refuse to use his cane in spite of knowing that he is at risk of falling. After explaining why he should use the cane, you must accept his decision, whatever it may be. Always consult with your supervisor if you have concerns about the client’s safety.

    Respecting your clients’ autonomy also means that you do not judge their choices or lifestyles. While you base your judgements and opinions on your own values and standards, your clients may have values and standards that are different from yours. For example:

    1. • A daughter decides that her mother needs care in a long-term care facility. In your culture, children usually take care of their aging parents at home. You do not understand why the daughter will not care for her mother at home.
    2. • A client has multiple tattoos and body piercings. You disapprove of tattooing and body piercing.
    3. • A client mentions to you that he has decided not to seek treatment for his cancer. You believe he should try everything possible to save his life.

    As a caregiver you need to set aside your biases and avoid judging your clients and their decisions on the basis of your values or standards. Do not give advice to clients, and never express your disapproval or opinions about their choices, preferences, politics, religion, or lifestyle.

    Justice

    The principle of justice means that all people should be treated in a fair and equal manner. Justice is an ideal that is central to Canada’s universal health care system—all Canadians, regardless of their ability to pay, receive equal access to the same medical services. Unfortunately, this ideal is not a reality for all people across Canada. Clients who can afford it can buy services that are not readily available to everyone. Examples of such services include private clinics, medical tests and procedures (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], hip replacement), and elective procedures (e.g., plastic surgery).

    As a support worker, you can uphold the principle of justice by showing concern for all clients, regardless of their conditions or temperaments. For example, some people are easier to work with than others, so you may be tempted to spend less time with a client who is demanding and ungrateful. Or you may wish to avoid a client whose lifestyle is very different from your own. However, doing so would be unjust and unethical. Every client deserves your attention and care equally.

    Treating people justly also means that you do not betray their trust. Clients trust that you will handle their possessions with care, respect their privacy, perform your services competently and skillfully, and keep all conversations and health information confidential. Do not snoop in clients’ homes, pry into their personal lives, or gossip with your friends or co-workers about them, either in person or electronically. Share information about the client only with your supervisor and the health care team. Never speak about a client where others may overhear you—for example, in public areas such as dining rooms, lounges, locker rooms, and elevators. Confidentiality is a basic right. It is so important that laws have been passed to protect it (see Chapter 5).

    Beneficence

    Beneficence means doing or promoting good. The principle of beneficence is central to your work. Support work is about promoting wellness, helping people in their daily lives, and supporting them during difficult times.

    To apply the principle of beneficence in your work life, always consider meeting the client’s needs to be your most important function. That means the client’s needs come before those of their family. Consider the following: Mr. Mijovick lives with his son and daughter-in-law in their home and receives home care services. Marcia is assigned to give Mr. Mijovick a bed bath. His son, however, insists that Marcia not bathe Mr. Mijovick and instead finish early that day because he is expecting a visitor. When deciding what to do, Marcia focuses on Mr. Mijovick’s needs, not on his son’s. She calls her supervisor to report the situation and seek guidance.

    The concept of beneficence and professionalism are closely related. To meet your clients’ needs, stay within the boundaries of a professional supportive relationship (see Chapter 9). Do not ask clients to do something that is in your interests rather than theirs. For example, if your child is selling chocolate bars to raise money for a school trip, do not ask your client to buy a chocolate bar. Avoid asking clients to do something for you, even if it aims to benefit others more than yourself or your family. For example, even if you are canvassing for United Way, do not ask your client to contribute to the campaign.

    When caring for a client, avoid focusing on yourself or burdening the client with your problems and worries. Never take advantage of a client’s compassion and generosity. If you tell a client your problems, they may try to help you. For example, if you tell a client you are having financial difficulties, the client may offer you money. Never ask for or accept money or loans from clients, regardless of how long you have been working with them. To do so is unethical.

    Clients can become very attached to their support workers. If family relationships are strained, the client may see the relationship with a support worker as a substitute for the relationship with a family member. Never take advantage of strained family relationships or forget that your relationship with your clients is professional. Do not take sides with a client against a family member. Never flirt, date, or accept invitations from a client or a client’s family member. When support workers become entangled in their clients’ affairs, serious consequences can result. For example, a support worker’s being named as a beneficiary in a client’s will could lead to legal problems for that worker and the employer.

    It may be necessary for you to advocate (see Chapter 1) for your client in order to do the most good for them. For example, you have observed that Mrs. Smith hates being showered but enjoys being bathed. It is important for you to share that observation with the health care team so that Mrs. Smith’s care plan indicates her preference.

    To do the most good for your clients, always give your best effort at work. Unless the person has unexpected problems or needs that require your attention, finish all your assigned tasks. Be careful, alert, and exact when following instructions. Also, be compassionate and empathetic. Self-discipline is essential, especially when working in home care. Avoid any temptation to use your work time for your own interests, including watching television, talking on the phone, and stopping for an extra cup of coffee.

    Nonmaleficence

    Nonmaleficence means doing no harm. Harm can be intentional (abuse) or unintentional (accidental injury or negligence). To avoid harming a client, perform only tasks that you have been trained to do. By recognizing the limits of your role and knowledge, you are protecting your clients from the risk for harm. Clients or family members may ask you to perform functions that could be dangerous if not performed correctly. Most often, such requests are made innocently. The client may forget that you are not qualified to do certain tasks. Although this confidence in you as a support worker is to be appreciated, it is not safe or wise to take on tasks that you are not trained to do, even if you have the best of intentions.

    Clients and their family members may also ask you for information about diagnoses or medical, surgical, or treatment plans. You must never reveal these details, whether or not you are asked. In doing so, you could give the wrong information and cause harm or distress. Not only is giving or discussing medical information outside your scope of practice, it is also unethical. Refer all such questions to your supervisor.

    To provide safe and effective care, you must keep your skills and knowledge current. Participate in educational programs offered by your employer. Consider enrolling in courses or workshops relevant to your work. Support work is continually changing. What you are trained to do this year may become outdated in a few years. The more knowledge and practice you get, the better and safer your skills will be.

    As a support worker providing care, you must keep clients as safe as possible. You can protect them from harm by practising infection control techniques (see Chapter 19) and by recognizing common safety hazards and knowing how to prevent accidents (see Chapter 20).

    Dealing With Ethical Dilemmas

    Codes of ethics provide only guidelines for ethical behaviour. They do not have the answers or rules for every situation. Occasionally, you may come across a situation that involves a conflict between two opposing moral choices, and choosing one would result in going against the other. When confronted with such an ethical dilemma, you need to know how to determine the right thing to do.

    When making an ethical decision, carefully consider the four principles of health care ethics. Collect as much information about the situation as possible. Consider all possible options to resolve the dilemma. Ask yourself these questions about each option:

    1. • Does the option respect the client’s wishes and preferences?
    2. • Does the option treat the client justly and fairly?
    3. • Does the option provide the client with a short-term benefit or a long-term benefit?
    4. • Could the option cause harm or increase the client’s risk for harm?
    5. • What are all of the possible consequences to the client, family, or others with this option?

    Answers to these questions may contradict each other. For example, one option may benefit the client but go against their wishes. Another option may reflect the client’s preferences but increase the risk for harm. Because you must protect the client from harm and also avoid serious legal problems for yourself and your employer, if any one option could harm the client, you must involve your supervisor in finding the solution.

    Understanding Rights

    A right is something to which a person is justly entitled. Some rights, sometimes called moral rights, are based on a sense of fairness or ethics. For example, when you and a classmate arrange to study together, you have the right to expect that the classmate will show up and be prepared to work. In another example, if you discussed a personal matter with a friend, you have the right to expect that your friend will not repeat this information to others. These rights are not based on written laws. They are based on moral principles: Commitments should be honoured, and secrets should be kept.

    Rights that are formally recognized in law are legal rights based on rules and principles outlined in the law and enforced by society. For example, various laws give you the rights to vote, to receive medical care, to own property, and to receive fair treatment if accused of a crime. Laws reflect the values of the society that created them. Canadians enjoy rights and freedoms that enable a life of equality and dignity.

    Basic Human Rights in Canada

    The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms is federal legislation that applies to all Canadians regardless of where they live. As part of the Canadian Constitution, the Charter applies at the federal level, so all provincial and territorial laws must be consistent with its rules. The Charter lists the basic rights and freedoms to which all Canadians are entitled. They include the following:

    1. • Freedom of conscience and religion
    2. • Freedom of thought, belief, opinion, and expression
    3. • Freedom of peaceful assembly and association (usually these freedoms are associated with the right to form a union or engage in a strike)
    4. • The right to vote
    5. • The right to enter, stay in, or leave Canada
    6. • The right to life, liberty, and security
    7. • The right to equality before and under the law, without discrimination based on race, ethnic origin, colour, religion, gender, age, or mental or physical disability

    The Right to Be Treated With Dignity and Respect

    The right to dignity is both an ethical principle and a legal obligation throughout Canada. It is also a guiding principle of caregiving, as emphasized throughout this textbook under the acronym DIPPS (Dignity, Independence, Individualized care, Preferences, Privacy, and Safety).

    Most health care legislation aims to protect and promote the client’s dignity. For example, British Columbia’s Community Care and Assisted Living Act states that facilities must be operated “in a manner that will maintain the spirit, dignity, and individuality of the client being cared for.”6 Ontario’s Long-Term Care Act states that the client has the right to be dealt with “in a courteous and respectful manner . . . that respects the client’s dignity” and in a manner that “recognizes the client’s individuality and that is sensitive to and responds to the client’s needs and preferences, including preferences based on ethnic, spiritual, linguistic, familial and cultural factors.”5,7

    Respecting the client’s dignity is a basic and important part of support work. For most people, dignity and independence go together. To respect your clients’ dignity, encourage them to be independent, allowing them to do as much for themselves as possible (FIGURE 5.2). For example, if a frail older man can put on his shoes, let him do so. It may save you time to put his shoes on for him. However, letting him do it himself helps him maintain some independence.

    Be careful not to make any assumptions about a client’s abilities, interests, and limitations. By making assumptions, you may discourage them from doing tasks and activities that they can do. Observe what your client is capable of doing, and check the care plan. A client who is dependent in one area is not necessarily dependent in all areas. For example, Mrs. Mukherjee needs help getting out of a chair. However, she can feed herself. Mr. Simpson needs help shaving, but he can comb his hair and brush his teeth. Mrs. MacDonald can easily walk by herself, but because of Alzheimer’s disease, she may wander off and needs to be supported in a secure environment.

    The Right to Privacy and Confidentiality

    People using health care services have the right to personal privacy. They have the right to receive care in private and in a way that does not expose their bodies unnecessarily. Only staff members involved in the client’s care should see, handle, or examine the client’s body.

    Information about the client’s care, treatment, and condition is confidential (see Chapter 1). All provinces and territories have legislation that protects the privacy and confidentiality of clients’ health information. This legislation is usually called a privacy act. Privacy acts provide guidelines to facilities and agencies on how to collect, use, and disclose personal health information. Most agencies require that all staff (and usually volunteers and students) sign an oath of confidentiality, a pledge that promises that the signer will respect and guard personal and private information about a client, family, or agency (see Chapter 9). When in doubt about whether or not to discuss something that you saw in regard to the client, you should always follow your employer’s policies.

    The Right to Give or Withhold Informed Consent

    All people have the right to decide for themselves whether or not they agree to medical treatment, health care, or personal care services. Such agreement is called consent. All provinces and territories have legislation that describes when and how consent is to be obtained.

    For consent to be valid, it must be informed consent, which is based on having received accurate and complete information.8 This information is provided to the client by the facility, agency, or physician.8 Consent is informed when the client clearly understands the following:8

    1. • The reason for the treatment or service
    2. • What will be done
    3. • How it will be done
    4. • Who will be doing it
    5. • The expected outcomes
    6. • Potential risks and side effects of the treatment
    7. • Other treatment options
    8. • The likely consequences of not having the treatment

    When a client enters a facility or hires an agency, they sign a form giving general consent to treatment (see “Advance Directive,” below). Special consent forms, however, are required for surgery and other complex and invasive procedures. The support worker is never responsible for obtaining written consent or giving medical information .     Advance Directive

    An advance directive (also known as an advance care directive) is a legal document that allows clients to convey their decisions about their own end-of-life care, whether they live at home or in a live-in facility. This document is signed ahead of time, often when a client is admitted to an extended care facility, and is usually completed as part of advance care planning in consultation with the client, next of kin (usually the substitute decision maker for health care—called the power of attorney or proxy in some provinces), and the agency’s director of care or administrator.9 The substitute decision maker for personal care may—but does not have to—set out the person’s wishes regarding care and treatment.10 Advance directive documents (and regulations about them) vary from province to province throughout the country.

    Advance directives provide a way for clients to communicate their wishes to their families, friends, and health care providers and are intended to avoid confusion later on, when the clients are perhaps less cognitively aware of their surroundings.9

    4. • Organ or tissue donation

    Living Will

    The term living will originated in the United States and has no legal status in Canada. In Canada, it is usually known as an advance directive. A living will is a document that lets the reader know a person’s preferences about care intended to sustain life and reflects a person’s wishes at the time that it is created and signed. Some people who are still living in their own home may want their family and friends to know whether or not they want care that is intended to sustain their life, and some may write these thoughts down in their living will.

    Like an advance directive, a living will is also not legally enforceable (see the Supporting Mme. LeBrun: Is a Living Will Enforceable? box) but is used to encourage family members to make decisions that respect the client’s wishes.

    Substitute Decision Makers for Personal Care or for Property

    Clients under the legal age (usually 18 years) and clients who are unable to make informed decisions for themselves or their property need another person to provide consent on their behalf. The generally accepted legal term for this person in Canada is substitute decision maker. In most provinces, people refer to the substitute decision maker as having power of attorney. (See Chapter 47 for more information about powers of attorney.)

    Substitute Decision Maker for Personal Care

    People with certain mental illnesses, confusion, dementia, or intellectual disabilities may not be able to give informed consent. In some situations, for example, an unconscious client cannot give consent for a procedure. Such situations require a substitute decision maker for health care. Depending on the province, the title proxy or power of attorney for personal care may be used instead.

Usually, the substitute decision maker is a spouse, daughter, son, or legal representative. As with consent given by the client, consent given by a substitute decision maker must be informed consent.

    The Right to Autonomy

    Autonomy, or self-determination, means having the freedom and choice to make decisions that affect one’s life (see Chapter 4) or end-of-life care (see Chapter 47). People using health care services have a right to autonomy when making decisions about their care and lifestyle. Clients have the right to be involved in issues concerning their admission, discharge, or transfer to or from a facility (see the Focus on Long-Term Care: Autonomy box). They also have the right to participate fully in assessing and planning their own care and treatment, whether receiving care in a live-in facility or at home.

    Understanding Legal Issues

    Client rights are based on laws. Like all health care team members, you, as the support worker, must act in a legally appropriate manner. If you break the law or violate someone’s rights, you are legally responsible (liable) for your actions and could be fined, sued, or even imprisoned.

    You must obey both criminal laws and civil laws. Criminal laws are concerned with offences against the public and against society in general. A violation of criminal law is called a crime—for example, theft, murder, rape, or abuse—and a person found guilty of a crime is fined or sent to prison.

    Civil laws deal with relationships between people. For example, laws relating to business disputes, divorce, or adoption are civil laws. A tort is a wrongful act committed by an individual against another person or the person’s property. A person who commits such an act can be sued by the injured person. Torts may be intentional or unintentional. An example of an unintentional tort is negligence. Examples of intentional torts are assault, battery, false imprisonment, invasion of privacy, and defamation of character.

Negligence is an unintentional wrong because the person at fault did not intend to cause harm. They either failed to do what a reasonable and careful person would have done or did what a reasonable and careful person would not have done.     Assault and Battery

    Assault and battery may result in both civil and criminal charges. Assault is identified in Canada’s Criminal Code and can be defined as intentionally attempting or threatening to touch a client’s body without the client’s consent, causing the client to fear bodily harm. Threatening to “tie down” an uncooperative client is an example of assault. Battery (which is part of tort law) is the actual intentional touching of a client’s body without the client’s consent. Battery can be committed without an intent to injure; just touching a client without their permission is battery. Force-feeding a client is an example of battery. Another is giving a treatment (such as a blood transfusion) to a person who has refused the treatment, even if the health care provider thinks the treatment will “help” the person.

    As a support worker you are not required to obtain written consent before you perform a task or procedure. However, you must always be aware of the client’s wishes. Also, a client who has signed a consent form has the right to withdraw their consent at any time. Always explain the procedure and what you are going to do, and make sure the client agrees to it. Consent may be verbal (“yes” or “okay”) or physical (a gesture such as a nod, turning over for a back rub, or holding out an arm for a blood pressure measurement). If the client objects to or declines your services, respect their wishes and stop the procedure or task, and immediately inform your supervisor since the client’s decision may affect their well-being.

    False Imprisonment

    False imprisonment is the unlawful restraint of or restrictions on a person’s freedom of movement. For example, preventing a client from leaving a facility is false imprisonment. So is the unnecessary use of restraints (discussed in Chapter 20).

    Invasion of Privacy

    Every client has the right not to have their name, photograph, private affairs, health information, or any personal information made public without having given consent. Violating this right is an invasion of privacy, a term recognized in tort law. Your employer may require you to sign an oath of confidentiality, binding you to keep all dealings with clients and your employer confidential (see page 65). This document may refer to the provincial or territorial privacy act that protects the privacy of individuals. Signing the document obliges you not to reveal information about clients obtained in the course of your work.

    Freedom of Information

    Canada is on the rapidly growing list of countries that have freedom of information and data protection legislation.11 Federal laws have been passed to govern access to information and privacy, the two main laws being the Access to Information Act and the Privacy Act. In Canada, the Access to Information Act allows citizens to demand records from federal bodies. This law is enforced by the Information Commissioner of Canada.

    The complementary Privacy Act was introduced in 1985. The Privacy Act’s purpose is to (1) extend the present laws of Canada that protect the privacy of individuals with respect to personal information about themselves that is held by a federal government institution and (2) to provide individuals with the right of access to that information. Complaints about possible violations of the Privacy Act may be reported to the Privacy Commissioner of Canada.

    The provinces and territories of Canada also have legislation governing access to government information; some acts also include privacy legislation. For example, the Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act applies to Ontario’s provincial ministries and agencies, boards, and most commissions, as well as community colleges and district health councils. In Quebec, the Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act governs access to documents held by public bodies and the protection of personal information.

    Defamation of Character

    Defamation is injuring the name and reputation of a person by making false statements to a third person. Libel is a criminal offense when it is knowingly making false statements that harm a person’s reputation in print, in writing, or through pictures or drawings. In civil law, slander is making harmful false statements orally. As a support worker, you can protect yourself by never making false statements about a client, co-worker, or any other person. Examples of defamation include the following:

    1. • Implying or suggesting that a client has a sexually transmitted infection
    2. • Saying that a client is insane or mentally ill
    3. • Implying or suggesting that a client is corrupt or dishonest

    Your Legal Rights

    Federal, provincial, and territorial legislation ensures that Canadian workers receive fair wages and work in a fair and safe environment. Laws have been enacted to protect workers’ rights and clarify their requirements and duties. These laws have different names across the country and vary in their details. In general, however, all provinces and territories have legislation that addresses human rights, occupational health and safety, employment, labour relations, workers’ compensation, long-term care services, and community services.

    Human Rights Legislation

    Human rights codes protect workers’ basic human rights. This legislation states that employers must treat all workers equally and not discriminate on the basis of a worker’s ethnic background, colour, gender, sexual orientation, religion, age, or disability. Employers and employment agencies also cannot discriminate at the request of a client. Human rights legislation declares that workers have the right to be free from harassment (also called workplace bullying) in the workplace by the employer, the client, or a fellow worker (see Chapter 12). Harassment means troubling, tormenting, offending, or worrying a person through one’s behaviour or comments.

    Occupational Health and Safety Legislation

    All provinces and territories have occupational health and safety (OH&S) legislation. This legislation outlines the rights and responsibilities of workers, employers, and supervisors in creating and maintaining a safe work environment. OH&S legislation, however, is not enforceable in a home care environment and does not protect support workers in home care.

    Employers must “take every precaution reasonable in the circumstances for the protection of a worker.”12 Workers have a right to receive (and employers must provide) proper education, instruction, and supervision to ensure their safety. Employers who do not fulfill these duties may be fined. Workers have the right to refuse to work if the work poses a danger to themselves or others. In some provinces and territories, however, health care workers cannot refuse to work if by not working they endanger a client’s health or safety.13

    OH&S legislation also details how hazardous materials used in the workplace are to be identified and managed. WHMIS (Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System) is a national plan developed to provide information on the safe use and potential health risks of hazardous materials (see Chapter 20).

    Employment Standards Legislation

    Employment standards legislation states the minimum employment standards acceptable within the workplace. This legislation covers basic rules about issues such as minimum wage, how wages are paid, how many hours of work per day and per week are acceptable, what is fair overtime pay, how many holidays and vacation days should be given, and what situations qualify a worker for a leave of absence.

    Labour Relations Legislation

    Provinces and territories have legislation that addresses how employers and employees can resolve workplace issues. According to these laws, all employees have a basic right to form or join a trade union of their choice and to participate in lawful union activities. These unions can negotiate wages and other issues with the employer on behalf of all union members. Labour relations legislation sets out the rules for these negotiations (also called collective bargaining), identifies what obligations must be fulfilled before a legal strike can take place, and identifies unfair labour and employee conduct.

    Workers’ Compensation Legislation

    The provinces and territories have workers’ compensation legislation covering how workers are financially compensated for accidental injuries on the job. Generally, an employee is considered to be on the job from the time of reporting to work until the end of the shift. If travel is work related, accidents that happen while travelling may also be covered by workers’ compensation. This legislation also discusses worker and employer rights when an injury occurs.

    Long-Term Care Facilities Legislation

    All long-term care facilities are regulated by provincial and territorial legislation. These laws address the basic rights of clients and describe requirements for the operation of the facility. Licensing and placement requirements, funding structures, accountability systems, guidelines about creating and maintaining health care records, and the level of training required of the staff are all listed as well.

    Community Services Legislation

    Community services legislation sets out the rules and procedures for accessing and providing community services, including support work. It defines the different types of community services and details how the services are to be provided.

    Key Points

    • As a support worker, your conduct at work is determined by (1) your code of ethics, (2) your employer’s policies, and (3) federal and provincial or territorial laws.

    • The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms lists the basic rights and freedoms to which all Canadians are entitled. All provincial and territorial laws must be consistent with its rules.

    • All people have the right to be treated with respect and dignity, as reinforced throughout this book by the acronym DIPPS (Dignity, Independence, Individualized care, Preferences, Privacy, and Safety).

    • All people have a right to decide whether or not to agree to medical treatment or health care or personal care services—called consent. This right extends further in that people have a right to complete, accurate, and relevant information about the situation and the potential implications for consenting or refusing to consent—called informed consent.

    • Wills are legal documents drawn up by people stating how they want their property distributed upon their death. A living will is a written statement that tells the person’s wishes in regard to accepting or refusing medical care in the event that the person is unable to express such a decision and reflects a person’s wishes at the time that it is created and signed. Living wills are not legally enforceable. Advance directives are legal documents that allow clients to communicate their decisions about their own end-of-life care.

    • A substitute decision maker, or power of attorney, is a person appointed to make decisions for a client who is unable to make informed decisions about themselves or their property. A substitute decision maker for health care (also known as a proxy in some provinces) makes informed health care decisions if the client is unable to do so. A substitute decision maker for property (known as an estate trustee) makes decisions regarding the client’s belongings if the client is unable to do so.

    • Criminal laws are concerned with offences against the public and against society in general (e.g., rape, murder). Civil laws deal with relationships between people. A tort is a wrongful act committed by one person against another person or their property.

    • Negligence occurs when a person or their property is harmed because of another’s failure to act carefully or competently.

    • An assault is an intentional attempt or threat to touch a person’s body without consent, causing the person to fear bodily harm. Battery is the actual touching of a person’s body without their consent.

    • All clients have a right to privacy, including electronic privacy and confidentiality. Violating this right is an invasion of privacy and is punishable by law.

    • As a worker, you have legal rights to receive fair wages and to work in a safe and fair environment.

    A support worker’s job is to help clients achieve or maintain optimal health. But what exactly is “optimal” health? This chapter examines the concepts and experiences of health and wellness as well as illness and disability and will also explain what optimal health is. A support worker providing supporting care to a client who lives with an illness or disability might be tempted to focus on the medical condition rather than on the whole person. However, by trying to understand what the person may be experiencing, support workers can provide better, more compassionate care and support.

    All caregivers need to understand how health practices, lifestyle, and social status can influence a person’s health. As a support worker, you must focus your care and support on improving (or maximizing) a person’s health potential, whatever that potential may be.

    Health and Wellness

    Past Definitions of Health

    The definition of the term health has changed over the years. At the end of the 1800s, health was defined by what it was not: Health was the state of not being sick. At that time, the leading causes of death were diseases that spread from one person to another. Pneumonia, tuberculosis, and influenza, for example, were frequent killers. Anyone lucky enough to avoid being infected during an outbreak was considered healthy. During the first half of the twentieth century, vaccinations, antibiotics, health education, and cleaner living conditions reduced the spread of diseases. People were living longer and getting sick less often. But were they healthy?

    Modern Definitions of Health

    During the latter part of the twentieth century, people started to realize that health is more than just the absence of disease. As they recognized that health is affected by factors such as lifestyle and environment, discussions of health began to focus more on the person rather than on the absence or presence of disease. A whole person has physical, emotional, social, cognitive, and spiritual dimensions. Psychosocial health is well-being in the social, emotional, intellectual, and spiritual dimensions of one’s life. A holistic approach to health care takes into account a client’s psychosocial well-being and health as well as their physical health. Few people enjoy perfect psychosocial health throughout their lives.

    Holism focuses on all these dimensions of health. Each dimension relates to and depends on the others. Current views on health give importance to holistic health—a state of well-being in all dimensions of one’s life (FIGURE 6.1). Providing holistic health care means caring for all dimensions of the person, not just the physical aspect.

    FIGURE 6.1 A whole person has physical, emotional, social, intellectual or cognitive, and spiritual dimensions. Optimal (or holistic) health is a state of achievable well-being in all dimensions.

    A widely accepted definition of health is that of the World Health Organization (WHO): “Health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.” In more recent years, the WHO has also recognized that health includes the ability to lead a “socially and economically productive life.”1

    Dimensions of Health

    The five dimensions of health—physical, emotional, social, spiritual, and cognitive—all must be considered in a discussion of whole health. These elements are discussed below.

    Physical Health

    Influenced by genetics and lifestyle, physical health is achieved when the body is strong, fit, and free from disease. The following factors contribute to the physical health of all people, including caregivers:

    1. • Following a nutritious diet according to Canada’s Dietary Guidelines (see Chapter 27)
    2. • Exercising regularly
    3. • Living in a smoke-free environment
    4. • Drinking alcohol moderately or not at all
    5. • Having a good night’s sleep
    6. • Maintaining optimal body weight according to body mass index
    7. • Following safety practices, such as using seat belts and wearing bike helmets
    8. • Seeking medical attention when needed

    Support workers have an important role in maintaining clients’ physical health. For instance, they help ensure a clean, safe, and comfortable environment; may prepare nutritious meals; and may assist clients with physical activity.

    Emotional Health

    Emotional health is not merely the absence of negative emotions but the ability to function well in and adapt appropriately to circumstances, whatever they may be. When people feel good about themselves, they are emotionally healthy. They have strong self-esteem, self-control, and self-awareness. They are able to give and receive from others without worrying about being hurt or rejected. In contrast, emotionally unhealthy people may be depressed or have intense feelings of insecurity. When upset, they may feel overwhelmed and become aggressive.

    Emotional health varies throughout one’s life. For example, Mr. Szabo was a confident and happy person and enjoyed many social relationships. However, at the age of 60, a series of tragedies destroyed his emotional health: His daughter died in a car accident, his grandson died from a drug overdose, and Mr. Szabo himself suffered a heart attack. While recovering from the heart attack, he experienced major depression. This example shows that even emotionally strong individuals cannot always withstand misfortune and suffering without experiencing depression or a blow to their resilience.

    Support workers work with emotionally healthy as well as emotionally unhealthy individuals. The behaviours of some of these clients may be surprising. For instance, a client who is usually cheerful may sometimes be irritable. Avoid judging them. Instead, learn to read your clients’ emotions so that you can respond in a caring manner.

    Social Health

    Social health is achieved through stable and satisfying relationships. Socially healthy people treat others with respect, warmth, and openness. They like and trust others. People with poor social health may show little regard for others and may use others for their own gain.

    Few people enjoy strong social health all through life. Feelings of isolation and loneliness are common among older persons and people who have lost their partners, friends, or other social relationships. New immigrants are also at risk for poor social health. Being in an unfamiliar place and not speaking the local language can be very socially isolating.

    Most people have friends and acquaintances who help them meet their needs. These needs may be practical, such as a ride to work. Others may be deeper, such as the need for:

    1. • Companionship and a feeling of belonging
    2. • Comfort, emotional support, and encouragement
    3. • Reassurance of one’s self-worth
    4. • Help, guidance, and advice

    A social support system is an informal group of people who help each other or others outside the group. Research has shown that social support systems help improve overall health. Indeed, social support may be as important to wellness as a nutritious diet, a smoke-free environment, and regular exercise. Social support systems can provide food, housing, financial aid, and emotional support during crises. They can make it possible for ill and frail people to continue to stay in their homes rather than move to a live-in facility (FIGURE 6.2). Social supports may also help people living with dementia stay in their homes longer. Support workers may be key members of a client’s social support system. As a support worker, you provide practical support, such as help with activities of daily living (ADLs) and home management, and emotional support by practising compassionate care.

    Spiritual Health

    Spiritual health is achieved through belief in a purpose greater than the self. It may or may not involve being a member of a formal religion or even believing in a higher being. People who are spiritually healthy tend to have a clear understanding of what they believe to be right and wrong, and their behaviours often reflect their beliefs. They feel their life has meaning. They are generally more concerned about personal fulfillment than about material things. Compassion, honesty, humility, forgiveness, and charity are elements of spiritual health.

    Influences on Our Health Beyond Our Immediate Control: The Social Determinants of Health

    Canada is one of the healthiest countries in the world. However, some Canadians are healthier than others and have more opportunities to lead a healthy life. The social determinants of health can have an important influence on health. For example, Canadians with higher incomes are often healthier than those with lower incomes.

    Differences in the health status of individuals and groups are called health inequalities. These differences can be due to a person’s genes and the choices they make (for example, whether they exercise or drink alcohol).2 A person might follow good holistic health practices and encourage everyone in the family to do so as well, but may still become very ill if they get a genetic (inherited) health condition. Similarly, in spite of holistic health practices, people (or even an entire community) could become very ill if they live close to a toxic waste dump or live several hundred kilometres away from the closest hospital and are unable to access health care when they need it.

To ensure the health of all Canadians, we need to improve not only the quality of health care that is available to Canadians but also their living conditions.     Achieving Optimal Health

    Optimal health, or wellness, is the achievement of the best health possible in all five dimensions of one’s life. It is the perfect balance of body, mind, and spirit. A holistic approach to health care takes into account not only a client’s physical health but also their psychological well-being and health. This approach, including Erikson’s stages of psychosocial development and Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, is discussed further in Chapter 31.

    Although many people try to achieve wellness, not all people actually experience it, as it is difficult to be healthy in all areas of life all the time. At some point, everyone experiences ill health in one or more dimensions. Seeking wellness is a lifelong process. It involves continually making choices that improve quality of life. It also involves becoming one’s best self in all areas of life, despite limitations. Therefore, even people with diseases or disabilities can have a high level of wellness.

    Interestingly, some individuals with excellent physical health may not achieve wellness. For example, Soo Hee is an athlete and in excellent physical shape. She eats well and trains daily. However, she does not make time for friends or family and suffers from loneliness and a lack of meaningful relationships. So she is not content with her life. To achieve wellness, she needs to improve the emotional and social dimensions of her life. Compare her with Josef, who has diabetes but manages his disease well, feels good about himself, and has strong relationships and an active mind. He feels he has a meaningful, productive life. Despite his illness, he has achieved a high level of wellness.

    Health can be expressed as being on a continuum (FIGURE 6.5). On one end is optimal (complete) health or wellness, and on the other end is extreme ill health. A person’s place on the continuum shifts, depending on factors such as life circumstances and how the person can adjust to them. Remember that health is not constant throughout life. Everyone experiences physical illness and emotional stress during their lives, and, therefore, most people can be said to have only average health.

    Culture and Health, Wellness, Illness, and Disability

    Throughout history, some cultures have treated their old and sick members as important persons in need of the utmost care and respect; in other cultures, by contrast, people who could not contribute to society were segregated from those who could. In some cases, those who were chronically ill, mentally challenged, or otherwise deemed “unacceptable” were vulnerable to extermination. However, here in Canada, one of our fundamental principles is to accept all people regardless of their differences. Chapter 7 discusses in detail how to respect diversity among clients.

    Personal Factors That Can Influence Health

    We all make choices that affect our health in one way or another. Some people eat the wrong foods or drink too much alcohol. Others may take drugs, engage in unsafe sex, or drink and drive. A person’s attitude toward health is important as it influences the choices that person makes. In this context, attitude can be defined as a person’s beliefs, values, or opinions toward engaging in healthy behaviours.

    A number of personal factors can influence our health, but the five main factors are (1) personal lifestyle choices, (2) stress, (3) personal beliefs about health care, (4) social relationships and belonging, and (5) a sense of control. These are described below.

    1. Personal Lifestyle Choices

    Some people make the wrong health choices for themselves or their family because they lack the knowledge or education to make the right choices. For example, some parents who are not aware of the importance of eating nutritious foods might demonstrate making poor nutritional choices to their children. These children then grow up with their parents’ habits and lead their own children to make the same poor choices. This example illustrates the importance of teaching healthy lifestyle choices in Canadian schools.

    As a health care worker, you should have healthy habits yourself in regard to drinking alcohol or using recreational drugs. For example, more than two alcoholic drinks a day is harmful for most people. Women who have more than 10 drinks a week have higher rates of cancer and other health problems compared with women who drink less. Men who have more than 15 drinks a week also have higher rates of alcohol-related health problems.8

    2. Stress

    We all must deal with stress in our lives. However, it is the manner in which we deal with our stresses that influences our health in the long term. For example, some people deal with their stresses by drinking excessive amounts of alcohol or by smoking cigarettes (unhealthy behaviours), whereas others choose to go jogging or to meditate (healthy behaviours).

    3. Personal Beliefs About Health Care

    Personal choices and beliefs can influence whether or not a person will seek medical treatment, take prescribed medications or herbal or nonmedicinal supplements, or even accept care from someone who is not a family member. Personal choices and beliefs can also influence when and how a person will accept care. For example, some people may view North American medicines as poisons and therefore distrust any advice from Western doctors, nurses, or support workers. (See “Culture,” one of the determinants of health, on page 85, as well as Chapter 7.)

    Whereas mainstream psychology generally focuses on only one part of the person, such as the person’s thinking, feelings, or behaviour, First Nations people include such values as balance, interconnectedness, nature, and spirituality into their view of the delicate balance of health, healing, and spirituality. They believe that interconnectedness is an important part of healing and that balance is important because illness occurs when a person lives in an unbalanced way. First Nations healing practices consider the individual in the context of the family, community, culture, and all of creation. Nature and spirituality, which play a prominent role in First Nations healing practices, have historically been almost nonexistent in mainstream therapies. However, with an ever-increasing understanding of the need for a balanced lifestyle to achieve health, mainstream health practitioners are now focusing on healing by incorporating the physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual aspects as important parts of the whole self.9

    4. Social Relationships and Belonging

    It is important to most of us to be loved and accepted by others. Some people will make unhealthy lifestyle choices in order to be accepted by others. For example, a teen may engage in sexual activity before she is emotionally ready because she does not want to “lose” her boyfriend. In another example, even though he knows he has a gambling addiction, a man may go to a casino after work because his friends are going.

    5. Sense of Control

    Some people make unhealthy lifestyle choices because they feel powerless to make the correct ones. For example, a teen may start smoking cigarettes because his friends pressure him to smoke. When educating about healthy behaviours, many health care workers teach people that they should make their own decisions and realize that they do not have to be influenced by the choices of others. This option is called personal empowerment and is an important step toward making healthy lifestyle choices.

    Disease and Illness Prevention Strategies

    The aim of primary prevention strategies is to prevent disease by reducing risk factors that cause disease. For example, we may come to know that many children living in a particular community are developing a certain type of cancer, but until we make the effort to prevent the root cause of the problem—and not just treat the cancers—that community will continue to have serious health issues.

    Illness and Disability

    Illness is the loss of physical or mental health, whereas disability is the loss of physical or mental function. Illness or disability may limit a person’s ability to communicate, move, or perform ADLs without assistance. Although the symptoms of acute illnesses can be severe, acute illnesses (such as influenza) and disabilities (such as a broken arm) last for a relatively short period. On the other hand, a persistent, or chronic, illness (such as acquired immune deficiency syndrome [AIDS]) or disability (such as paraplegia) is ongoing, slow or gradual in onset, and may or may not grow worse over time. Because a chronic illness cannot be cured, the focus of care is on preventing the complications of the illness. Both acute and chronic illnesses are discussed further in Chapter 33.

    It is important to remember that clients living with illnesses and disabilities are whole persons with many dimensions. They are more than their medical conditions. As a support worker, while you help them achieve their best physical health possible, you must also consider their emotional, social, cognitive, and spiritual health (see the Supporting Mrs. Davidson: Maintaining Cognitive Function box).

    Supporting Mrs. Davidson: Maintaining Cognitive Function

    Salman is a support worker at a long-term care facility. He works on the floor for residents with physical problems such as arthritis and osteoporosis. Mrs. Davidson, age 92, is a resident on Salman’s floor. Mrs. Davidson loves to read. She reads the newspaper every morning and listens to an audiobook in the afternoon. One day, Mrs. Davidson tells Salman that she misses talking about books with other people. Salman knows some other residents who also like to read and listen to stories. He wonders if there is a way to bring these clients together as a group.

    Salman suggests to his supervisor that the facility start a book club. He explains that discussing books will help the residents maintain their cognitive function. Belonging to a book club will also help them develop friendships. Salman’s supervisor likes his idea and speaks to the recreation director about it. By the end of the month, a weekly book club meeting is up and running, with Mrs. Davidson as its leader. The book club becomes so popular that within a year the members form two separate clubs—one for fiction and another for nonfiction.

    Supporting Clients with Illness and Disability

    Illness and disability usually affect all aspects of a client’s life. For example, Mr. Spinelli recently suffered severe vision impairment. As a result, his cognitive health has suffered because he can no longer read or pursue his other hobbies; his social health is affected because he can no longer travel to meet friends; his emotional health suffers because he is frustrated and depressed; and his spiritual life is affected because he is angry at God and no longer wants to attend church services.

    No two clients will experience illness and disability in the same way. Some severely ill clients remain cheerful and calm throughout their illness; some who are not seriously ill complain constantly or grow easily sad or frustrated. Many clients living with disabilities are not ill, and they do not consider themselves to be ill. Clients who are born with disabilities have never known life any other way, and many clients who get disabilities later in life learn to adjust to their situations and need no further medical care.

    Although clients’ experiences vary, health care providers have identified some common reactions to a newly diagnosed illness or a recently acquired disability (BOX 6.2). Factors affecting a client’s experience of illness and disability include the following:

    1. • The nature of the illness or condition
    2. • The client’s age
    3. • The client’s level of physical fitness
    4. • The amount and degree of pain and discomfort the client experiences
    5. • The prognosis (the expected course of recovery based on the usual outcome of the illness)
    6. • The client’s emotional, social, cognitive, and spiritual health
    7. • The client’s personality and ability to cope with difficulties
    8. • The client’s cultural background, which may influence how they view the illness, seek treatment, and interact with caregivers and health care workers (see Chapter 7)
    9. • The presence of emotional, social, and financial support

    Change and Loss Associated With Illness and Disability

    Clients with a serious illness or a recently acquired disability must cope with change and loss. The following are just a few of the many changes these clients face.

    Change in Routine

    During illness, daily routines almost always change. Time previously spent at work or with friends and family is often now filled with doctors’ appointments, tests, and treatments. For many clients, simple ADLs suddenly become challenges. Such routine matters as getting to the bathroom, making meals, eating, and controlling pain now become serious issues.

    Change in Work Life

    Many clients with serious illnesses or disabilities quit their jobs or limit their workloads. Clients who used to feel rewarded and fulfilled by their work may suddenly feel worthless when they can no longer do it (see the Supporting Mr. Vitale: The Effect of Serious Illness on Self-Esteem box). The loss of work may also result in financial problems and loss of social interactions.

    Change in Family Life

    Serious illness or disability almost always disrupts family life. When one family member is ill, often the lives of everyone in that person’s home change greatly. Every family member must make adjustments to the new situation and take on new roles. For example, Mrs. Kim survived a severe stroke. She can no longer be the sole provider and caregiver for her teenage children. Her role has changed to that of a person who needs care. While she recovers, her children must take care of her, with help from professional caregivers. The children may have to give up after-school activities or time with their friends.

    Changes and new roles often create stress. Sometimes the stress on family members is so severe that their own health suffers as a result (see Chapter 11).

    Change in Sexual Function

    Disability and illness often affect sexual function. The client may feel unfit for closeness and love and may lose interest in sex. Or the adverse effects of medications or the illness itself may render a client physically unable to have sex. Reproductive surgery, heart disease, stroke, spinal cord injuries, and nervous disorders are some of the conditions that can affect sexual function in people of all genders. Changes in sexual function can significantly affect clients and their partners. Fear, anger, worry, and depression are common but normal and expected reactions. Time, understanding, and a caring partner will be helpful to the client. Professional counselling may help the client and their partner adjust to the situation.

    BOX 6.2 Common Reactions to Illness and Disability

    1. • Fear and anxiety. When even minor illnesses can cause anxiety, it is understandable that clients with serious illnesses have many fears and anxieties—the effects of their illness on their family; how they will manage their daily responsibilities; financial issues; their family’s future; and death. Some clients worry about any change in their role within the family unit. Clients with disabilities, disfigurements, or speech or memory impairments may worry about embarrassing themselves in front of others. Clients with mental health disorders often experience severe anxiety. Some of the fears of clients may make sense to you as a support worker; others may not. To the person experiencing the fear, however, it is real. Some clients will communicate their fear and anxiety to you, but many will not and may prefer to keep their concerns to themselves.
    2. • Sadness and grief. Clients facing any kind of loss are usually sad. Those with serious illnesses and disabilities often deal with many kinds of losses—loss of position, loss of independence, loss of confidence. For some, there is loss of their dreams for the future. These losses can cause intense grief. These clients are grieving and need to mourn. Observe and listen to your clients so that you can understand their needs. Some clients may not want to talk about their feelings, whereas others may find it helpful to talk to an understanding, caring person.
    3. • Depression. Fear, anxiety, sadness, and grief can cause anyone to feel depressed. This manifestation is called reactive depression (see Chapter 37). However, clients who are coping with serious illnesses, progressive disabilities, or, in the case of some older clients, the losses of lifelong friends and family members, are at risk for more serious depression called clinical depression. Clients who are depressed are often tired, anxious, and uninterested in life. They may avoid contact with other people. Clients who are severely depressed may be suicidal. Observe closely for any changes in a client’s mood, energy levels, and behaviour. Report these changes to your supervisor immediately (see Chapter 37).
    4. • Denial. Denial is a refusal to recognize and admit the truth. Clients who are afraid that they might be seriously ill may downplay or deny symptoms. Even clients who know that they are seriously ill may deny their situation. For example, a teenager with diabetes may refuse to take her insulin or may demand foods that she should avoid. A middle-aged man with heart disease may continue to shovel his driveway in spite of his doctor’s strict order not to do such heavy work. Their way of coping may be to deny that they have serious health problems. If you think a client is denying their condition, be understanding and show a positive attitude. If the person’s denial may cause harm, let your supervisor know.
    5. • Anger. Some clients may be angry because they resent their limitations, their illnesses, and their inability to have control over their lives. They may direct their anger toward their physician, family, friends, or caregivers. Some clients may direct their anger toward their support worker. If you become the target of a client’s anger, you should remain calm, patient, and gentle with the client. Avoid becoming angry yourself. Reacting negatively will only make the situation worse. Try to understand the client’s needs and problems. Imagine what life must be like for him. However, you do not have to accept abuse. See Chapters 12 and 37 to learn what to do when faced with an angry client.

    Supporting Mr. Vitale: The Effect of Serious Illness on Self-Esteem

    On Wednesday, Tony Vitale felt on top of the world. He got up at 6:00 a.m., as usual, and jogged for half an hour. Over breakfast, he reviewed the speech he was to give at his company’s annual meeting. He was looking forward to announcing that profits were up. At the age of 46, he had achieved his life’s goal of becoming the chief executive officer (CEO) of a major corporation.

    Mr. Vitale never gave his speech. As he stepped up to the podium, he let out a short gasp and collapsed to the floor. When he awoke, 10 hours later, he did not recognize his wife or his two children. He could not speak or understand anything that was said. He had suffered a severe stroke.

    Within 4 months, it became clear that Mr. Vitale would never recover sufficiently to be able to return to his job. Although his memory eventually returned, his speech remained difficult for others to understand. He also had difficulty understanding others. The news that his position had been filled by a new CEO overwhelmed him with sadness and anger. Throughout his adult life, Mr. Vitale’s job had given him the recognition, prestige, and status that he desired. Without it, he felt useless and depressed. He slowly began to be consumed by anger. He was angry at his body for “betraying” him and at his caregivers for not being able to “make him better.” He shouted at his family each time they came to visit him. He was angry that they were able to survive without him.

    During 8 months of therapy and rehabilitation, Mr. Vitale made impressive progress. Although he found long sentences difficult to comprehend, other people could now understand much of what he said. With the support of his family and a caring health care team, his depression was gradually reduced. He began to realize that he still had something to offer to the world. This realization also helped lift the anger that had pressed him down. He understood that his family loved him, and he began to feel pride that they were independent enough to survive without his financial support. He discovered that he enjoyed painting. He spent time volunteering with people who had sustained a brain injury. His newfound self-esteem came from the knowledge that he was making a useful contribution.

    Loss of Independence

    Independence is the state of being able to do things for oneself. Losing one’s independence can be very hard. It is particularly distressing when the onset of the illness or disability is sudden and there is little or no hope for recovery. As the support worker, you must try at all times to enable your clients to be as independent as they can be.

    Loss of Dignity

    Independence and dignity are closely related. For some clients, loss of independence can lead to loss of dignity, particularly when they need help with personal care. It can be extremely difficult for some clients to have to depend on others to perform private functions, including bodily functions. Always be sensitive to your clients’ need for dignity.

    Change in Self-Image

    Self-image is the individual’s view of themselves. Changes to a person’s body caused by illness may affect self-image. Clients who have lost body parts or have scars from surgery or accidents may feel that they are unattractive or even repulsive. Others with conditions that negatively affect the way they look, move, walk, or speak may feel very self-conscious.

    As a support worker, you can help clients with illnesses and disabilities by understanding how their condition affects various aspects of their lives. Do not make assumptions. Do not judge the person’s behaviour or compare one client’s reaction to illness with another’s. Do everything you can to communicate warmth, acceptance, and respect to clients. Always keep in mind the goal of support work (DIPPS—see Chapter 1). (See the Providing Compassionate Care: Caring for Clients With Illnesses or Disabilities box.)

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Caring for Clients With Illnesses or Disabilities

    1. • Dignity. Needing help with bathing, toileting, and other ADLs can be extremely embarrassing and can affect a client’s dignity. Never expose a client’s body unnecessarily. Be aware of your facial expressions and gestures while you are providing care, as they may reveal that you are disturbed by the client’s disfigurement or body odours. Such a reaction will cause feelings of shame in the client.
    2. • Independence. Encourage clients to participate in their care. Tell them what you are about to do, and ask if they can help. Clients may be able to carry out some of the steps in a procedure themselves. Also, let clients make decisions for themselves if they are able. For example, clients who are paralyzed may not be able to dress themselves, but they can decide what to wear.
    3. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit each client’s personal choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. For example, if a client wishes to pray before a meal, arrangements need to be made to accommodate this in a respectful manner.
    4. • Preferences. Ask clients how they want tasks done. You may have to ask for specific information. For example, ask them what is important to them, what they enjoy doing, what they are able to do, what they find easy, and what they find difficult.
    5. • Privacy. Clients may, in some situations, feel that their privacy has been violated. They may need to adjust to having caregivers around. Never snoop and look at your client’s belongings when you are in a client’s room or house. The following actions promote privacy: knocking before entering, drawing curtains and blinds, closing doors and windows, covering the client during personal care activities, and keeping client information confidential. Clients living with illnesses or disabilities still have sexual needs, including the need for touching, caressing, and embracing. Allow privacy for clients to fulfill their sexual needs.
    6. • Safety. All clients need to feel safe from harm. Clients with illnesses or disabilities have special safety needs. Check with your supervisor and the care plan for specific safety measures for each client. Look at clients’ rooms from their viewpoint and ask yourself if there is a safe passage to the bathroom or if any items could be in the way and cause falls or injuries. When you recognize signs and symptoms of tiredness or overexertion, allow the client time for rest. If you are not sure that the client’s safety needs are being met, talk to the client and to your supervisor. Follow the safety measures described in Chapter 20.

    Attitudes of Others Toward Illness and Disability

    Some people are uncomfortable or fearful when they encounter people with illnesses or disabilities. Other people may stare or avoid eye contact. They may treat the person with an illness or disability differently from the way they treat people who are well and able-bodied.

    For example, Ms. Leblanc needed to use a wheelchair after an injury to her spinal cord. She said that it was very hard getting used to the way some people treated her. She recounted, “the first time my husband and I went out to dinner after the accident, the waiter asked my husband what I wanted for dinner. To the waiter, I was invisible. Since then I have met many people who ignore me or treat me like a child. I’ve learned to live with it, but it still hurts.”

    Some clients experience stigma and discrimination because of their illness or disability. Stigma is social shame associated with a particular circumstance, quality, or person that causes one to feel shame or embarrassment. One person described her experience with stigma as follows: “My arthritis was so bad that I wasn’t able to type any longer. Because of that, I was fired from my job, and now, my former co-workers treat me differently. I can just feel it. I haven’t been able to face any of them either because I am so ashamed.” A person who is stigmatized by others is thought of as being different in a negative way. Discrimination is the unfair treatment of people on the basis of such aspects as physical characteristics, health history, or group membership. Some clients with AIDS, mental health disorders, or substance abuse disorders are likely to face discrimination. Sometimes, they are blamed for their conditions and they and their families are deprived of much-needed social support. Such rejection can lead to isolation, loneliness, and depression, as well as feelings of self-blame and guilt.

    The language that we use can also convey negative or demeaning images of people, especially to those living with disabilities (BOX 6.3). It is important to use language that is inclusive, respectful, and accurate at all times.

    BOX 6.3 Suggested Guidelines for Language to Promote Positive Images of People Living With Disabilities

    General Guidelines

    It is important to remember that words have a precise meaning and are not interchangeable. The following guidelines suggest appropriate terminology to use when speaking or referring to people with disabilities.

    1. • A disability is a functional limitation or restriction of an individual’s ability to perform an activity. The word disabled is an adjective, not a noun. People are not conditions. It is therefore preferable not to use the term the disabled but rather people with disabilities.
    2. • Avoid categorizing people with disabilities as either super-achievers or tragic figures. Choose words that are nonjudgemental, nonemotional, and are accurate descriptions. Avoid using brave, courageous, inspirational, or other similar words to describe a person with a disability. Remember that the majority of people with disabilities have similar aspirations to those of the rest of the population, and that words and images should reflect their inclusion in society, except where social isolation is the focal point.
    3. • Avoid references that cause discomfort, guilt, pity, or insult. Expressions like “suffers from,” “stricken with,” “afflicted by,” “patient,” “disease,” or “sick” suggest constant pain and a sense of hopelessness. While this may be the case for some individuals, a disability is a condition that does not necessarily cause pain or require medical attention.
    4. • Avoid using words such as burden, incompetent, or defective, which suggest that people with disabilities are inferior and should be excluded from activities generally available to people without disabilities.

    People living with disabilities are comfortable with the terminology used to describe daily living activities. People who use wheelchairs go for “walks,” people with visual impairments “see” what you mean, and so on. A disability may just mean that some things are done in a different manner, but that doesn’t mean the words used to describe the activity must be different.

    Remember that, although some disabilities are not visible, it does not mean they are less real. Individuals with invisible disabilities such as epilepsy, hemophilia, and mental health and learning or developmental disabilities also encounter barriers and negative attitudes.

    Focus on the issue rather than the disability. If the disability is not relevant to the context, it is not necessary to report it.

    Chapter review

    Key points

    • A whole person has physical, emotional, social, cognitive, and spiritual dimensions.

    • Holism involves considering all dimensions of health (physical, emotional, social, cognitive, and spiritual); optimal (complete) health is a state of achievable maximum well-being in all of these dimensions.

    • Health lies on a continuum with optimal health or wellness on one end and extreme ill health on the other end. A person’s place on the continuum shifts depending on factors such as life circumstances and how the person can adjust to them.

    • Personal factors that can influence the dimensions of health are (1) personal life skills, (2) stress, (3) personal beliefs about health care, (4) social relationships and belonging, and (5) a sense of control.

    • The influences on health that are beyond a person’s immediate control are called determinants of health. The 12 determinants of health focus on the “bigger picture” of where we live—that is, our environment—and the way it affects our health.

    • Illness is the loss of physical or mental health, whereas disability is the loss of physical or mental function.

    • Illness or disability usually affects all aspects of a client’s life, and no two clients will experience illness or disability in the same way.

    This chapter addresses one of the most important values that all support workers—and all caregivers, for that matter—must possess: respect for diversity. Diversity is the state of different individuals and cultures coexisting. During your career as a support worker, you will care for and work with people whose lifestyles, beliefs, customs, and rituals are different from your own. It is necessary that you are always respectful of a person’s age, ethnicity, gender, occupation, sexual orientation, and lifestyle. Respect for diversity is a theme that will be repeated throughout this textbook and will be reinforced throughout your career as a support worker.

    Canada has a very diverse population, and Canadians are known for embracing this diversity and celebrating it. Unlike the American melting pot, which illustrates how all peoples and cultures are unified and blended within that country, Canada is known as a cultural mosaic, or a cultural salad, which illustrates a country of many distinct pieces. This reality was enacted into federal laws. In 1971, Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau declared that Canada would adopt a multicultural policy, recognizing and respecting the diversity in languages, customs, religions, and practices of its people. Multiculturalism was recognized by Section 27 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982) and became law in 1988 in the Canadian Multiculturalism Act. This law declared equal rights to all Canadian citizens, ensured Indigenous rights, and identified both French and English as the official languages of Canada, although other languages can be used. As a result, all Canadians are protected by law from discrimination based on the person’s sex, gender, ethnicity, age, sexual orientation, language, religion, origin, caste or class, income or property, beliefs or opinions, and health or disability.1

    Your client (or a co-worker) may be a third-generation Canadian, an Indigenous Canadian, or a new immigrant. Another client (or co-worker) may be in a same-sex relationship, and yet another may voice very different political or religious beliefs from yours. Each of us has a unique background and culture, and we all have a right to our own personal viewpoints. As a support worker, you must possess good listening skills and be both understanding and accepting of other people’s differences and perspectives. You should also be aware of and respectful toward each client’s cultural background in order to provide the best care possible. Your supportive care should never be less respectful for one client than it is for any other client, for any reason. Lastly, when giving support care, you should never try to change the client to fit in with your care; rather, you need to adapt your care and support to fit the client.

    Diversity: Ethnicity and Culture

    Two terms are often confused when discussing diversity—ethnicity and culture. Because many people find the word race insulting or misleading, this chapter will avoid this term and use ethnicity instead. While the authors agree that it is not a seamless replacement, it is the one that is usually used.

    In nearly any part of Canada, you will notice people with different skin colours and facial features that show that they come from different ethnicities and backgrounds. This variety is an example of the ever-increasing diversity of our country. Racism results when people have feelings of intolerance of or prejudice toward a person or group of people because of their racial or ethnic backgrounds. Ethnocentrism, the belief that one’s own culture or ethnic group is better or more important than others’, may be less obvious than racism, but neither racism nor ethnocentrism is in keeping with the spirit and intention of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

    Ethnicity

    Ethnicity refers to the sharing of a common history, language, geography, national origin, religion, or identity. Examples of ethnic groups include the Irish, the Inuit, and the Chinese. An ethnic group is not necessarily a nationality. For example, you may have been born in Canada, so your nationality is Canadian, but you may consider your ethnic identity to be Ukrainian because your family came from Ukraine and you still speak its language or practise many Ukrainian ethnic customs. Canada can be proud that it has many ethnic groups within it.

    Culture

    Culture makes a society distinctive. Culture refers to the characteristics of a group of people—the language, values, beliefs, habits, ways of life, implied rules for behaviour, music, and traditions—that are shared or perhaps even passed on from one generation to the next. Culture may be influenced by age, ethnicity, gender, occupation, sexual orientation, or lifestyle, or a combination of these factors.

    Cultural characteristics, which are learned by living within a group, influence a person’s attitudes and behaviours. Examples of cultural groups include high school students (numerous subgroups exist within this main group), farmers, and snowboarders. Everyone is part of a culture. Some people belong to more than one culture at the same time. For example, you might be a college student, belong to a religious organization, have parents with whom you speak another language, and go jogging with a set of friends regularly. Each of these groups of people that you associate with can have its own culture.

    Ethnicity is an important influence on a person’s culture, but it is not the only influence. A person might have come from China as a young child, spoken Chinese at home, and loved Chinese food and customs. Having grown up in Canada, however, she might be very different from her parents, who came to this country as adults. Her ethnicity has influenced her, but so has being schooled in Canada, having outside interests, and making friends from different social, cultural, and religious backgrounds. Because her experiences are different from her parents’ experiences, she has been subject to cultural influences that are different from theirs.

    Some people have chosen to assimilate their styles of dress, eating habits, or other behaviours when with people from another culture. Examples of assimilation are a Muslim woman who no longer wears a hijab, or a First Nations person who changes eating habits (see the Supporting Amy Longfeather: How Cultural Habits Can Influence Health box).

    Prejudice and Discrimination

    Unfortunately, prejudice and discrimination do still exist throughout the world. Prejudice is an attitude toward or opinion of a person based on their membership in a group. It is formed from the word prejudge, which implies that value assumptions regarding a person are formed before even meeting or knowing that person. Similarly, a bias occurs when a person is unable to impartially judge the issues at hand because of a preformed point of view.

    Acting on one’s prejudices results in discrimination, the unfair treatment of people on the basis of their physical characteristics, health history, or group membership. An example of discrimination is using the assumption that “All people who are ______ are lazy” to justify why a certain person is not hired.

    Types of Prejudice

    In an ethnically and culturally diverse society such as Canada’s, prejudices and discrimination should not be tolerated. As a support worker, you will be caring for and supporting all types of people and must be prepared to give all your clients the same high quality of care, regardless of their differences.

    Some people stereotype ethnic or cultural groups by assuming they are “all alike” or by believing that everyone in that group acts or behaves in a certain way. A stereotype is an overly simple or exaggerated view of a group of people. An example of a stereotype is “Women are shorter than men.” While many women may be shorter than some men, this general statement is certainly not true.

    Ageism refers to feelings of intolerance of or prejudice toward others because of their age, based on negative attitudes, stereotypes, or impatience (BOX 7.1). North American society has long fostered a desire to look young and “act young,” and older people who are frail or live with cognitive decline are often seen as less worthy of attention, less valuable to society, and less employable. In fact, even some academic health care programs focus only on the characteristics and health needs of young adults, instead of emphasizing the types and consequences of age-related bodily changes. As a result, some health care workers overlook serious symptoms in older persons, such as signs of fever, medication interactions, or urinary tract infections.

    Sexism is feelings of intolerance or prejudice toward a person or group of people because of their gender. Many examples of sexism exist today. For example, some people still believe that men make better politicians than women. In some workplaces, men are paid more than women who perform the same job. In some countries, couples who are trying to conceive a baby seek medical assistance to ensure a baby of a preferred gender.

    Some people exhibit homophobia, or negative attitudes toward or an intolerance of homosexuality or people who identify as part of the LGBTQ2 community, consisting of people who are lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, transsexual, queer, questioning their sexuality, or two-spirited. Transgender means that a person’s sense of personal identity and gender does not correspond to that person’s sex at birth or does not otherwise conform to conventional notions of sex and gender (see the Supporting Jimmie: Respecting Diversity in Transgender Clients box). Cisgender describes a person whose sense of personal identity corresponds to the sex and gender assigned to them at birth or corresponds with their birth sex. Although discrimination against people in the LGBTQ2 community is illegal, such discrimination exists. Of the children or youth in Canada who identify as part of the LGBTQ2 community, more than half have reported being verbally harassed about their perceived gender or sexual orientation, and over 20% have been physically harassed or assaulted.2

    Cultural Conflict

    Cultural conflict occurs when a person tries to dictate to another person what their culture should be. Unfortunately, we often see examples of cultural conflict even in our multicultural society. For example, people may be forced by others not to wear anything that reveals religious affiliation in certain environments. Or a caregiver might serve unfamiliar foods to a client because those foods are common in the caregiver’s ethnic background, not the client’s.

    An individual living within different cultures at the same time can also have feelings of cultural conflict. A child raised in a very modest, religious family may experience cultural conflict when he grows up and moves out of the house to go to school. This person may struggle with trying to live by the family’s rules, as expected by his parents, while trying to enjoy some newly found freedoms within his peer group. Refer to the Case Study: Cultural Conflict box for an example of cultural conflict experienced by an individual.

    BOX 7.1 Ageism and Age Discrimination (Fact Sheet)

    The term ageism refers to two concepts: a socially constructed way of thinking about older persons based on negative attitudes and stereotypes about aging, and a tendency to structure society on the basis of an assumption that everyone is young, thereby failing to respond appropriately to the real needs of older persons.

    Ageism is often a cause of individual acts of age discrimination and also discrimination that is more systemic in nature, such as in the design and implementation of services, programs, and facilities. Age discrimination involves treating persons in an unequal fashion due to age in a way that is contrary to human rights law. The Ontario Human Rights Code prohibits age discrimination in employment, housing accommodation, goods, services and facilities, contracts, and membership in trade and vocational associations.

    Age discrimination is often not taken as seriously as other forms of discrimination. However, it can have the same economic, social, and psychological impact as any other form of discrimination.

    To combat ageism it is necessary to raise public awareness about its existence and to dispel common stereotypes and misperceptions about aging. Aging is a highly individual experience, and it is not possible to generalize about the skills and abilities of an older person based on age, any more than it is possible to make assumptions about someone based on any other aspect of their identity. Human rights principles require people to be treated as individuals and assessed on their own merits, instead of on the basis of assumptions, and to be given the same opportunities and benefits as everyone else, regardless of age. It is important to recognize that older persons make significant contributions to our society and that we must not limit their potential.

    At the same time, ageism can be combated through inclusive planning and design which reflects the circumstances of persons of all ages to the greatest extent possible. The Supreme Court of Canada has recently made it clear that it is no longer acceptable to structure systems in a way that assumes that everyone is young and then try to accommodate those who do not fit this assumption. Rather, the age diversity that exists in society should be reflected in design stages for policies, programs, services, facilities and so forth so that physical, attitudinal, and systemic barriers are not created. Where barriers already exist, those responsible should identify them and take steps to remove them.

    Finally, it is important to remember that the experience of ageism and age discrimination may differ based on other components of a person’s identity. For example, certain groups of older persons may experience unique barriers because of their age, combined with their gender, disability, sexual orientation, ethnicity, colour, ethnicity, religion, culture, and language.

    The Effect of Culture

    A person’s culture affects how they deal with daily situations and problems. It is not possible to completely understand the beliefs and practices of all cultures. However, it is important to realize that culture affects a person’s beliefs and behaviours toward such issues as:

    1. • Family and social organization
    2. • Religion and worship
    3. • Health care practices and reactions to illness
    4. • Communication

    The Effect of Culture on the Family

    In your career as a support worker, you will meet different kinds of families. Culture affects family structure, as well as the roles and responsibilities of various family members during times of illness. For example, in some cultures, adult children (especially daughters) are expected to care for their older parents.

    Types of Families

    In Western culture, the most common family structure is the traditional nuclear family, which consists of two partners and a child (or children) who live under one roof and are committed to mutually raising the child or children. The family arrangement in today’s Canada is very different, though, from how it looked in previous generations. Now there is an increasing number of single-parent (also called lone-parent) families, in which the adult head of the household does not have a partner who shares the home. Some families are blended families—that is, the family consists of a couple with two or more children, at least one of whom is the natural child of one or both members of the couple and at least one of whom is the stepchild of one of the partners. Another common family structure, the same-sex family, is one in which both partners living together in a loving, intimate relationship are of the same gender and who may or may not have children.

    Western culture emphasizes self-reliance and independence. Children are usually encouraged to be self-sufficient, and most young adults leave the family home and live independently of their parents and siblings. Care of family members outside the nuclear family—such as that of grandparents, aunts, or uncles—is often entrusted to others outside the family.

    In some cultures, such as Asian, South Asian, and Indigenous cultures, extended families (a couple, their children and parents, siblings, and siblings’ children) commonly inhabit one household (FIGURE 7.2). In extended families, the needs of the entire family are more important than individual needs. Older persons and anyone who is unwell are often taken care of by family members. For example, in Vietnam and China, all family members are involved in the care of a member who has an illness.5 Family members bathe, feed, and comfort the person with the illness. People from these and other such cultures continue this custom even in Canada, and those affected by illness are often surrounded by family during this time.

    Sometimes children rebel against the culture of their parents. Children of first-generation immigrants often reject the roles and behaviours expected of them in favour of those of the new culture, causing great stress for the parents and family.     The Effect of Culture on Religion

    In most cultures, religion is an extremely important influence. Religion relates to spiritual beliefs, needs, and practices and may in part determine people’s daily living habits, behaviours, relationships with others, diet, times of worship, and attitudes about birth and birth control, medicine, healing, and death.

    Many people rely on religion or spirituality for support and comfort during illness. They may want to pray and observe certain religious or spiritual practices and may find it helpful to have a visit from a spiritual advisor or religious leader. If a client asks to see such a person, promptly report the request to your supervisor. Make sure the client’s room is tidy for the visit. Ensure privacy during the visit.

    Religions, including Christianity (Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant faiths), Judaism, Buddhism, Islam, Hinduism, Sikhism, and the Baha’i faith, among others, are practised by various groups within Canada. You will likely care for clients who have religious beliefs that are different from yours, and some clients may not follow any religion. Trying to convert your clients to your own belief system is called spiritual abuse. You must always respect the client’s beliefs, practices, and religious symbols and items (such as a rosary, yarmulke, prayer rug, icon, or religious medal). Religious items should be treated with the greatest of respect and never touched or moved unless you are given permission to do so by the client.

    It is important that support workers be aware that all clients have a right to spiritual care. Refusing to allow spiritual care for clients is another kind of spiritual abuse. In most facilities, the registered staff or spiritual counsellor or chaplain is responsible for ensuring that the client is visited by the religious representative if the client requests one.

    The Effect of Culture on Perceptions of Health Care and Illness

    Culture greatly affects how people view health care and illness and how they cope with the symptoms and stresses of being ill. Some cultures have certain beliefs about the causes of illnesses. In Western culture, the general belief is that disease and illness are caused by biological or environmental factors. Illness and disease can often be prevented, and people can be cared for or cured with scientifically proven methods. Some other cultures believe that illness is caused by supernatural forces, an imbalance with nature, or disharmony among mind, body, and spirit. People from these cultures may use charms, rituals, alternative medicines, or traditional or folk medicine that may include ancient remedies and rituals, passed down through generations. Some folk remedies involve use of herbs or a traditional healer, or shaman.

    Folk remedies may help the person, or they may not have any effect on the person’s health. If the practice does not harm the client and promotes their emotional well-being, the nurse or case manager would probably include it in the care plan. Some folk remedies, however, may interfere with the client’s medical treatment. For example, some herbal medicines may interact with prescription drugs and produce harmful results. Often, clients try alternative therapies or cultural health care practices without telling their physician, nurse, or case manager.6 The health care team must be aware of all health care practices to make sure they are not harmful to the client. It is important to tell your supervisor if your client tells you that they are using alternative or folk remedies or if you observe a client using alternative or folk remedies.

    The Effect of Culture on Communication

    Communicating across cultures presents many challenges. As a support worker, you must communicate with clients and their families in such a way that your message is clear and, in turn, the client understands your intended meaning. All communication should always adhere to the five principles of compassionate care (DIPPS: dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, and safety), which is the goal of support work.

    The Use of Certain Words

    Words and phrases may have different meanings to people from different cultures, even if they speak the same language. Some words or expressions that are used in one country may have a vulgar and insulting meaning in another country. It is important to be aware of that and to avoid terms that could be misinterpreted.

    It is also important to understand that words and phrases can have a painful historical context to some people. For example, in Canada, many Indigenous people have been affected by residential school abuses. Some words and phrases can bring back painful memories about these experiences (see the Respecting Diversity: Terms and Phrases to Avoid When Communicating With Indigenous Peoples box).

    The Use of Translators and Interpreters

    Some of your clients may speak languages or dialects different from yours. Other clients may be deaf or hard of hearing and thus unable to hear you (Chapter 34). As a result, with some clients, you will need to work with an interpreter. Interpreters may be family members who are able to describe what the client is saying or trying to say to you and then translate back to the client what you would like to say to (or ask of) the client. In other cases, the interpreter may be a staff member who happens to speak the same language (or a similar language) as the client. Occasionally, a paid translator must be called in to interpret what a client is saying, especially if a legal consent is necessary. However, in certain situations, you may not have an interpreter handy when you need to communicate with clients who do not speak your language, and you must find a way to communicate with and understand the client (see the Respecting Diversity: Communicating With Clients Who Speak a Language Different From Yours box).

    Body Language

    Speaking the same language is only part of communicating. Information and messages are also sent using nonverbal cues, such as the use of touch, space, eye contact, and even silence (see Chapter 9). Body language, an important part of nonverbal communication, is expressed through gestures, postures, and facial expressions, which can reveal a person’s physical, mental, or emotional state. A person’s cultural background can influence the body language they use, as well as how they interpret another person’s overall nonverbal communication (see Chapter 9). Support workers need to be aware, however, that nonverbal cues may mean different things to people of different cultures.

    Terms and Phrases to Avoid When Communicating With Indigenous Peoples

    • 1. Pre-history. Avoid use of the term pre-history because it implies that the history of Indigenous peoples began with the arrival of Europeans. In reality, each individual Indigenous culture has its own creation story that predates the arrival of Europeans in what is now known as Canada.
    • A related caution is to avoid the concept that the Americas were “discovered.” When Christopher Columbus “arrived” in the Americas, there were an estimated 100 million Indigenous peoples living there at the time. A better way to divide the timeline is to use the terms precontact and postcontact.
    • 2. Canada’s Indigenous peoples. This phrase implies that Canada owns Indigenous peoples. The appropriate phrasing is “Indigenous peoples in Canada.” This also applies to provinces and cities—for example, “Vancouver’s Indigenous population” should be changed to “the Indigenous population in Vancouver.”
    • 3. Equality. Indigenous peoples are not treated equally because they exist under a separate set of laws defined by the Indian Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. I-5).7 The purpose of the Indian Act was to assimilate Indigenous people, but in practice it segregated and marginalized them, restricted their ability to be part of the local economy, forced their children to attend separate schools, outlawed their traditions and culture, and restricted their ability to mingle with mainstream Euro-settlers.
    • There is no equality in the standard of living for those on reserve. More often than not, those who live on reserve have to contend with poor housing and unsafe water, both of which contribute to poorer health, and lower levels of education, which lead to lower rates of employment and higher rates of incarceration.
    • Also, until 2008, there was no equality under the Canadian Human Rights Act (CHRA), which most Canadians take for granted as applying to all citizens. Prior to 2008, the CHRA denied Indigenous people full access to human rights protection because it did not apply to those who were under the jurisdiction of the Indian Act. Prior to 1960, Indigenous people were denied the right to vote unless they gave up their Indian status and Treaty Rights.
    • When Indigenous people hear the terms equality or equal, they hear that they must give up their constitutionally protected rights, or they hear that they can be equal only if they give up their human rights to be who they are as a people.
    • 4. We’re here to help. The full phrase “we’re from the government and we’re here to help” was an all-too-familiar refrain used by Indian agents, working on behalf of the federal government, to describe laws and policies of the Indian Act designed to “help” Indigenous people become something other than who they are. Because of the existence, for over 140 years, of the Indian Act and its “help,” Indigenous communities have struggled with issues such as poorer health, inadequate and unsafe housing, unsafe drinking water, lower education achievement, lower employment rates, and higher incarceration and higher suicide rates. Instead, you could say, “I have lots to share, but I know that I also have lots to learn.”

    Personal Space

    If someone stands too close to you, you would probably feel uncomfortable or anxious because your personal space is being invaded. The same holds true for your clients. Personal space is the area immediately around one’s body. Everyone has personal-space preferences, and it is not always dictated by culture. Some people prefer more personal space than others do because of their life experiences (see the Supporting Donald Lundy: Respecting a Client’s Sense of Personal Space box on page 108).

    The exact distance requirements vary among individuals and situations. However, people in the same cultural group tend to have similar personal-space requirements.10 In Western cultures, most people prefer to stand at a distance of about 90 cm (3 feet) to speak. People in other cultures may prefer to stand closer or farther away when interacting with others. When you are providing care, it is important not to invade your client’s personal space. If the client steps back from you, does not face you directly, or pulls their chair away from you, they may be sending a message that you are too close.11

    Eye Contact

    Eye contact has different meanings within different cultures. In Western cultures, eye contact is usually a sign of positive self-concept, openness, interest in others, attention, and honesty, and it also communicates warmth. Lack of eye contact can communicate rudeness, guilt, dishonesty, shyness, or embarrassment. People from some other cultures, however, may not be comfortable with direct eye contact. In some Asian and Indigenous cultures, for example, eye contact is considered disrespectful and an invasion of privacy.11 In the Indian culture, eye contact with people of a higher or lower social and economic class is usually avoided.11

    Facial Expressions

    Some facial expressions are universal. Expressions of pain, surprise, embarrassment, and happiness are similar around the world, but some cultures are more expressive than others. It may be hard to judge what others are feeling only on the basis of their facial expressions. For example, many Italian and Spanish people tend to use facial expressions and gestures often to communicate happiness, pain, or displeasure. In contrast, many Irish, English, and northern European people use fewer facial expressions, especially with strangers.10 In some cultures, certain facial expressions may, in fact, suggest the opposite of what the person is really feeling. For example, in some Asian cultures, people may smile to hide negative emotions.11

    Silence

    Even the use of silence varies among cultural groups. In some cultures, such as the English and Arabic cultures, silence is usually used for privacy.12 Among Russian, French, and Spanish cultures, silence usually indicates agreement between parties.12 In some Asian cultures, silence is often used as a sign of respect, particularly in interactions with an older person.12 In some Indigenous cultures, silence is considered a virtue: Speaking is reserved only for matters of extreme importance.13 Among most Indigenous, Chinese, and Japanese people, silence is used as a communication aid. For example, if the person is speaking and suddenly stops, their silence may be intended to allow the listener to think about what has just been said before the speaker continues.14

    Providing Culturally Sensitive Care and Support

    Providing culturally sensitive care is important in support work. Remember that clients are unique individuals and respond to cultural influences in unique ways. Do not stereotype a person on the basis of ethnicity, religion, or any other factor.

    You cannot apply the cultural behaviours of a given culture to all members of the group. Individuals may not follow every belief and practice of their culture and religion. Each person is unique.

    Stress

    Stress is a normal part of life that everyone experiences at one time or another. Stress is the emotional, behavioural, or physical response to an event or situation. People face various types of stress in their lives, some positive and some negative. Eustress is a type of stress that is healthy and gives one a feeling of fulfillment or other positive feelings. Acute stress is short in duration, whereas chronic stress is ongoing.

    Stress affects the whole person. It can have positive or negative effects in all dimensions—the physical, emotional, social, intellectual (cognitive), and spiritual (TABLE 8.1). Chronic stress can lead to burnout—a state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion. A person experiencing burnout feels discouraged, negative, and powerless. We discuss the topic of burnout in more detail on page 119. For some individuals with underlying mental health issues, severe or prolonged stress may trigger the onset of mental health symptoms, especially if the client experiences changes in eating or sleeping, increases use of drugs or alcohol, or feels impulses to engage in reckless behaviour (also see Chapter 37).

    Sources of Stress

    Stress can be caused by various factors, called stressors. The more frequently the stressor occurs and the longer it lasts, the more likely it is to affect the person’s health. Stressors that last for a few minutes to a few hours—for example, daily irritations—usually create only mild stress. Stressors that last for months or years, such as persistent illness, disability, or family relationship problems, can cause severe stress.

    Some common stressors are discussed below.

    Changes

    Whether it is positive or negative, change is always a source of stress. Regardless of age, many people find change difficult, while others seem to embrace it without exhibiting signs of stress. For example, most older persons have lived through numerous changes and personal difficulties. Many of them are still able to adapt well to new changes in health status or to the loss of partners and friends. Meanwhile, some younger adults can be very negatively affected by life changes and personal loss. Common life changes include the following:

    Stress

    Infants and children also react to stressors and may show the same signs of stress as adults do. For example, a child under stress might have stomach aches, irritability, changes in appetite, or changes in sleep patterns.

    Defence Mechanisms in Times of Stress

    Most people use defence mechanisms at one time or another, especially when they are under stress. Defence mechanisms are used to delay—or avoid altogether—uncomfortable feelings such as anxiety, guilt, stress, or embarrassment. In this way, they relieve stress by helping the person avoid facing a troubling reality (see Chapter 9). For example, a client in a facility is upset that her daughter is not able to visit her frequently. She blames the city bus system since she believes that if the buses were more reliable, her daughter would visit more often. Even though she focuses her anger on the bus system, she really is disappointed with her daughter.

    When working with clients who are under stress, being able to recognize their defence mechanisms is useful. Understanding defence mechanisms gives you insight into what your clients may really be feeling. You can help them by being empathetic to their feelings, by trying to validate their feelings, and by providing compassionate care. Validation means to acknowledge, recognize, or confirm the client’s feelings (see Chapter 38).

    As a support worker, you must recognize the common responses to stress in your clients. Tell your supervisor when you notice a client showing signs of stress, such as loss of appetite or sleep, a change in behaviour or mood, or increased use of alcohol or other substances. Remember, your role is to observe and report. It is not within your scope of practice to diagnose clients by using defence mechanism (or diagnostic) terms; instead, you need to describe the client’s behaviour when reporting it. It is important, however, to be familiar with these terms when they are used, as an understanding of them will help you when giving care to your client. For example, when a client complains of headache and nausea, do not assume that the client is suffering from stress, because these could also be symptoms of a physical condition. Rather, note and report your observations, since professionals such as nurses and social workers can help clients cope with stress.

    Managing Stress in Your Life

    Stress is common among health care workers, particularly in support work, which can be very demanding. You may feel physical stress from all the lifting, moving, and carrying that you are required to do every day. You may feel cognitive stress while trying to do many things at the same time. For example, a client asks for the bedpan every time you walk by the room, and another needs to be turned and repositioned every 15 minutes. You may feel emotional stress from working with clients who are sick, lonely, frail, or dying. You may also work with clients or family members who are angry or distressed. For example, a wife who is the primary caregiver for her ill husband is angry with her boss for insisting that she always stay late at work. She may direct her anger toward you, or you might feel upset for her. Depending on how you react, such situations can cause severe stress.

    Managing stress is essential, so do not ignore the signs of stress; watch for them. Letting stress build can result in burnout or illness. Not dealing properly and immediately with stress can cause some support workers to take out their frustrations on their clients or someone in their personal lives, leading to abuse or neglect, which cannot be allowed to happen.

    The following strategies can help in managing stress:

    1. • Develop self-awareness. Know what causes your stress. Think about when you felt under stress. What was the source? Does it occur often? After determining your stressors, you need to decide how to eliminate them, avoid them, or cope with them. Avoid people who cause you stress.
    2. • Take care of your needs. A healthy mind and body enable people to cope better with stress. Getting enough sleep is important. Mild irritations may seem like serious problems if you are sleep deprived. Exercising regularly and following a nutritious diet are also important. Do not ignore your social, cognitive, and spiritual needs. Often, the key to managing stress is finding ways to balance family, work, relaxation, and recreation. Keep track of how you spend your time. Which parts of your life get too much time? Which parts are neglected? How might you achieve a better balance? Make time for fun and relaxation.
    3. • Think positively. A positive attitude can help you manage stress. Focus on what you do well and what you can control. Remember that every person perceives stress differently. Put the stressor into perspective—try not to let a minor stressor become a major one. Try to change your perspective to see how a stressor can create a positive outcome. Keeping a sense of humour also helps reduce tension. Look at the big picture. Ask yourself how important this stressful situation will be in the long run. Will it matter in a month or a year? If the answer is no, focus your time and energy elsewhere.
    4. • Assert yourself. Nonassertive people say “yes” to things when they really want to say “no.” They take on tasks when they have no time for them. They also give in to the demands of others without considering their own needs. Never agree to do more than you can do safely (see Chapter 23).
    5. • Ask others for help and support. To avoid stress, you have to accept that you cannot do everything yourself. Assert yourself at work and in your home life. If you need help with an assignment, tell your supervisor. At home, let your family know that you need their help and support. Discuss sharing household duties with your roommates, siblings, spouse, or children. Let them know what you need and expect, and take the time to explain to them how to do the activities you usually do. Be encouraging, and avoid being critical if things are not done to your standard.
    6. • Practise calming exercises. As soon as you feel the first sign of stress, find a way to calm yourself (BOX 8.4). If necessary, walk away to calm down rather than engage yourself in a stressful situation. Some people, for example, do daily meditation to cope with stress (BOX 8.5). Others take a moment to change their thoughts into more positive ones to reduce their stress.
    7. • Practice healthy lifestyle habits. Adopting a lifestyle that includes healthy eating habits, getting enough sleep, avoiding smoking, minimizing alcohol intake, not abusing drugs, exercising regularly, and reducing caffeine and sugar intake can lead to better stress management.1
    8. • Learn to accept the things you cannot change. You cannot control the behaviour of other people. However, you can choose to change how you react to that behaviour.
    9. • Understand how change itself can be stressful. In the workplace, we are constantly being affected by changes such as budget cuts, changing technology, and staffing issues. These changes can cause stress to the people who must adapt to them. Try to identify if these changes are a cause of stress for you and discuss how you are feeling with your supervisor, or perhaps even a counsellor. Ask if they could suggest ways to adapt to change as a means of reducing stress.

    BOX 8.4 Calming Yourself When Feeling Stress

    1. • Close your eyes (if safe to do so), and take deep, slow breaths. Relax your stomach muscles. Breathe in through your nose and out through your mouth. Your stomach should rise about 3 cm (1 inch) as you breathe in. As you inhale, count slowly up to 4. As you exhale, count slowly back down to 1. Pause between breaths. Continue breathing slowly and rhythmically until you feel yourself relaxing.
    2. • If you feel your muscles tensing, relax them. People tend to clench their jaws and tighten their necks and backs when they are under stress. Relax your muscles, from your face down to your feet.
    3. • If possible, take a few minutes to yourself. Remove yourself from the stressful situation. However, never leave a client unless it is safe to do so.

    Job Burnout

    Burnout is common among health care workers because these helping professions can be very demanding physically, emotionally, and mentally (see Supporting Kathy: Support Workers Can Face Burnout Also). As discussed earlier, severe or prolonged stress can lead to burnout, which is a state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion caused by long-term exposure to demanding work situations. Like stress, burnout can have negative consequences for your health, such as insomnia, weight gain or loss, depression, anxiety, and other emotional difficulties (also see Chapters 9 and 37).

    The following are some of the signs of burnout:

    1. • Increasing criticism of others or sarcasm at work
    2. • Changed sleep habits
    3. • Changed appetite
    4. • Fatigue—for example, having trouble getting the day started

    Time Management

    Time management, which is essential to reducing stress, is important in support work. You can use time-management strategies in all aspects of your life. If you reduce your overall stress levels, stress at work will also decrease.

    To manage your time, you must identify your priorities. Doing so helps you stay focused on what is important to you. While you are at work, providing competent, compassionate care is a priority. To determine your priorities outside of work, ask yourself these questions:

    1. • What do I value most in life?
    2. • What gives me satisfaction?
    3. • What principles do I want to live my life by?

    You may identify a large number of priorities. Take some time to decide which ones are the most important. Assign a number to each, with 1 being the most important and 10 being the least important. Now you are ready to turn your priorities into goals.

    Setting SMART Goals

    Setting goals for yourself will help you manage time and stress. Your goals should give you direction and motivate you to take action. Start with your priority number 1, and work down the list to number 10. Do not set more than 10 goals, or you may lose your focus. Your goals should be SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, and Timely.

    1. • Specific. Goals must be clear. For example, “losing weight” is not a specific goal. The goal of “losing 5 kg by the end of March” is specific and gives direction and focus.
    2. • Measurable. Measurable goals tell you if you are making progress. The goal stated above is measurable in two ways: “5 kg” and “by the end of March.”
    3. • Achievable. Goals should be challenging yet achievable. When setting goals, consider how much time and effort you can put into them. A goal may need two or more parts to be achievable. For example, to lose the weight, you are going to need to investigate a diet plan or an exercise plan and determine how much time will be needed to put your plan into action.
    4. Respecting Diversity

    As a support worker, you must remember that some people may not value keeping strict adherence to time schedules. When working in the community, you could be scheduled to arrive in your client’s home for 9:00 a.m. but then find that your client is not ready for your appointment and would like you to wait and chat until she is ready. Talk to your supervisor if adhering to the schedule is an ongoing problem with your client. In facilities, late starts could create a challenge for both workers and clients when a routine has to be followed. 5. • Realistic. A realistic goal accounts for time, resources, and skills. For example, losing 5 kg in 3 months is realistic. It would not be realistic, however, if you were planning to take a vacation during this time. 6. • Timely. A target date for meeting goals increases commitment. Break goals into parts, and set schedules. As each part is achieved, you will gain confidence and be motivated to reach higher goals. For example, your goal can be broken down into losing 1.6 kilograms a month.

    Planning Your Life and Your Work

    Well-organized people have weekly and daily plans and include their personal and professional goals in their planning. Goals are easier to achieve when time is spent planning at the start of each week. For example, every Sunday, Raj, a busy support worker who has a wife and two children, plans his week. He is working the evening shift this week, which means his wife must pick up the children, make dinner, and take them to after-school activities. Since Raj’s wife is going to be out of town on Thursday, Raj makes a note to arrange for his sister to look after the children that day. One of Raj’s goals is to build a backyard hockey rink, as he promised his children. He decides to work on the rink each day before leaving for work. Raj reviews his work schedule for the week. His supervisor has asked him to coach a new support worker, which means he will be taking the new support worker on client visits. Since one of his clients may be uncomfortable with the presence of another support worker, his supervisor has asked him to call the client ahead to prepare him for the new worker. Raj makes a note to telephone this client on Monday.

    Daily planning and scheduling are important to meeting goals. Review your assignment sheet and the care plan for each client, and decide how you will approach each task. If you prepare ahead of time (the night before or just before your shift), you will not have to spend the valuable time you have with clients scheduling tasks. However, just because you have prepared does not mean that you will never have to change a schedule once it has been set. You must stay flexible and responsive to the needs of the client (see the Supporting Mrs. Paget: Daily Planning box).

    Use your planning and scheduling time to think about problems that might arise. Review the tasks on your list. Plan how much time each task will take. To improve scheduling, ask yourself these questions:

    1. • What are the client’s needs and priorities?
    2. • How much time will each task or activity require?
    3. • When will I do each task or activity?
    4. • Can I organize my time so that some of the tasks overlap?
    5. • Have I allowed time for the unexpected?
    6. • Is there anyone with whom I should coordinate these activities?

    Giving each task a time limit will help you stay focused and complete a task on time. See BOX 8.6 for ways to manage your time and stay organized. At the end of your workday, compare what you planned to do with what you actually did. Did you accomplish what you planned? If not, try to identify why. Did problems arise? Were there interruptions? Was it a poor schedule?

    3. • The amount of time to spend with each client
