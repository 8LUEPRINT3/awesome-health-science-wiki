    BOX 22.7 Guidelines for Applying Nonsterile Dressings

    1. • Make sure your province or territory allows you to perform the procedure.
    2. • Make sure the procedure is in your job description.
    3. • Apply dressings only under a nurse’s direction and supervision.
    4. • Review the procedure with the nurse.
    5. • Allow time for pain medications to take effect, as the client may experience discomfort during the dressing change. The nurse will give the medication and tell you how long to wait.
    6. • Provide for the client’s fluid and elimination needs before starting the procedure.
    7. • Collect needed equipment and supplies before you begin.
    8. • Be aware of your nonverbal communication. Wound odours, appearance, and drainage may be unpleasant, but take care not to communicate your thoughts and reactions to the client.
    9. • Follow Standard Practices. Wear personal protective equipment, as necessary. Never touch a wound with ungloved hands.
    10. • Remove dressings in a way that prevents the client from seeing the soiled side. The drainage and its odour may upset the client.
    11. • Do not force the client to look at the wound, as the appearance of the wound can affect body image and self-esteem. The nurse will address the client’s concerns about the wound.
    12. • Remove the tape by pulling it toward the wound. You can ensure that the tape does not stick to the skin by, again, folding it back on itself.
    13. • Remove the dressing gently. If the dressing sticks to the wound and surrounding skin, ask your supervisor if the wound can be dampened (for example, by applying sterile normal saline) to loosen the dressing from the wound.
    14. • Observe the wound, and report and record your observations, according to employer policy (see BOX 22.5 on page 497).

    Pre-Procedure

    • 1 Review the procedure with your supervisor.
      • Being familiar with the procedure before beginning will reduce your anxiety and, in turn, the client’s.
    • 2 Identify the client, according to employer policy.
      • This eliminates the possibility of mistaking one client for another.
    • 3 Explain the procedure to the client.
    • 4 Allow time for pain medication to take effect (injectable medication may begin to work within minutes, but oral pain medication may take at least 1 hour).
      • This will make the procedure more comfortable for the client.
    • 5 Provide for the client’s fluid and elimination needs.
      • This will make the client more comfortable during the procedure.
    • 6 Practise proper hand hygiene.
    • 7 Collect the following supplies:
      • □ Gloves
    • □ Personal protective equipment, as needed
    • □ Tape
    • □ Dressing, as directed by the care plan
    • □ Dressing tray, as directed by the care plan
    • □ Clean, small gauze squares to wipe wound area, as directed by the care plan
    • □ Normal saline to clean the wound, as directed by the care plan
    • □ Adhesive remover
    • □ Scissors
    • □ Leak-proof plastic bag
    • □ Bath blanket or clean sheet
    • By collecting all the necessary equipment beforehand, you will not have to needlessly leave your client’s side and can eliminate wasting time and energy in obtaining the forgotten equipment. You will also avoid tiring the client needlessly.
    • 8 Provide for privacy.
    • 9 Arrange items on your work area.
    • 10 Raise the bed to a comfortable working height. Follow the care plan for bed rail use.∗
      • Raising the bed will reduce your risk for back strain. Lowering the head of the bed will make it easier to slide the client up to a better position.

    Procedure

    • 11 Lower the bed rail near you if it is up.
    • 12 Help the client to a comfortable position.
    • 13 Cover the client with a bath blanket or clean sheet. Fan-fold the client’s top linen to the foot of the bed.
      • A fan-folded sheet is easier for the client to grasp when it is time to cover up.
    • 14 Expose the affected body part, leaving the rest of the client as covered as possible.
      • This respects the client’s need for privacy and prevents the client from being needlessly chilled.
    • 15 Make a cuff on the plastic bag. Place it within reach and in a place that avoids crossing over the wound with soiled wipes.
    • 16 Wear a gown and mask if necessary as directed by the care plan.
      • These measures decrease the risk for pathogen spread.
    • 17 Put on gloves.
    • 18 Remove tape by holding the skin down and gently pulling the tape toward the wound. Avoid lifting the tape to remove it, but rather peel away the tape closely to the client’s skin.
      • Pulling the tape toward the wound is more comfortable for the client than pulling away from it, as is peeling it closely to the client’s skin. These steps can also reduce the chance of reopening the wound if it has not yet healed.
    • 19 Remove adhesive from the skin. Wet a 10 × 10 cm (4 × 4 in.) gauze dressing with the adhesive remover. Clean away from the wound.
      • Adhesive left behind irritates the skin, so it should always be removed. Cleaning away from the wound decreases the chance of introducing pathogens or adhesive into the wound.
    • 20 Remove the gauze dressing. Start with the top dressing. Keep the soiled side of the dressing out of the client’s sight.
      • Keeping the soiled dressing out of sight reduces the client’s anxiety.
    • 21 Place the dressing in the leak-proof bag. It must not touch the outside of the bag.
      • The outside of the bag must remain clean and uncontaminated to reduce the risk for pathogen spread.
    • 22 Remove the dressing directly over the wound very gently.
      • The old dressing may stick to the wound. If you handle it roughly, you can reopen a wound that hasn’t yet healed.
    • 23 Observe the wound and drainage (see BOX 22.5, on page 497). Avoid showing the drainage to the client.
      • Seeing the drainage may be upsetting to the client.
    • 24 Remove gloves. Put them in the bag. Practise proper hand hygiene. (If used, raise the bed rail before leaving the bedside. Lower it when you return.)
    • 25 Open the dressing tray. Unfold the clean or sterile wrapper and ensure it stays open.
      • The new dressing tray is opened after the soiled dressing is removed, to reduce the risk of contaminating it.
    • 26 Pour the normal saline into the dressing tray, being careful not to splash it on the wrapper.
      • Splashing will contaminate the wrapper.
    • 27 Open the small gauze square package and, using the forceps supplied in the dressing tray, place five or six squares onto the dressing tray or on the clean wrapper, staying as close to the centre of the wrapper and the tray as possible.
      • The small gauze squares will be used to cleanse the wound. Old drainage and dried blood left behind on a wound can be a source of infection and should be removed.
    • The outer 2.5 cm of the wrapper is regarded as contaminated.
    • 28 Cut the length of tape needed.
    • 29 Put on clean gloves.
    • 30 Using either the forceps supplied in the dressing tray or your clean, gloved hand, pick up a small gauze square. Dip it into the normal saline solution.
    • 31 Wipe the wound in one long stroke down the middle of the wound from top to bottom. Discard the gauze square into the leak-proof bag. If you are using a cleaning cloth, use a fresh corner for each stroke you use. Do not touch the wound again with a soiled gauze square or cleaning cloth.
      • The wound is wiped in long strokes, and the gauze wipe is discarded after each wipe to prevent pathogen spread.
    • 32 Wipe down the middle of the wound again and then on either side of the wound.
      • Wiping is always done from the centre of the wound to the outside to prevent contaminating the wound.
    • 33 Apply dressing, as directed by the care plan.
    • 34 Secure the dressing in place using tape.
      • Tape prevents the dressing from falling off.
    • 35 Remove your gloves. Put them in the bag. Practise proper hand hygiene.
      • These steps decrease the risk for pathogen spread.

    • 36 Provide for safety and comfort.
    • 37 Cover the client and remove the bath blanket or clean sheet.
    • 38 Place the call bell within reach.∗
      • This provides an easy, safe way for the client to contact the staff if necessary.
    • 39 Return the bed to its lowest position. Follow the care plan for bed rail use.∗
    • 40 Remove privacy measures.
    • 41 Discard supplies into the leak-proof bag. Tie the bag closed. Dispose of the bag, according to employer policy.
    • 42 Clean your work surface, according to employer policy.
      • This reduces the risk for pathogen contamination of anything placed on that surface after the dressing change.
    • 43 Practise proper hand hygiene.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • Skin is the body’s first line of defence, protecting the body from invading pathogens.

    • A wound is a break in skin or mucous membranes and becomes a portal of entry for microbes and pathogens.

    • The support worker’s role in wound care depends on provincial or territorial laws, job description, and the client’s condition.

    • Types of wounds include (1) intentional, (2) unintentional, (3) open, (4) closed, (5) clean, and (6) contaminated. Wounds can also be described according to their depth, such as (1) partial-thickness or (2) full-thickness.

    • Skin tears occur due to friction, shearing, pulling, or direct pressure. They should always be reported to the supervisor.

    • Pressure injuries are caused by unrelieved pressure over a bony prominence. All support workers should know who is at risk for pressure injuries and should observe for them and report any found immediately to the supervisor.

    • Support workers should know ways to prevent pressure injuries.

    • Circulatory ulcers include (1) venous ulcers and (2) arterial ulcers. Their causes, appearances, and treatment differ from each other.

    • Complications of wounds include (1) hemorrhage, (2) infection, (3) dehiscence, and (4) evisceration.

    • Wound drainage types include (1) serous, (2) sanguineous, (3) serosanguineous, and (4) purulent.

    • Support workers should be familiar with the responsibilities of caring for clients with wounds.

    Every person is an individual who is shaped by a unique blend of genetics, environmental influences, and experiences. In Chapter 6, concepts such as health, wellness, illness, and disability were introduced. It must be emphasized that it is important to understand that a client’s culture and experiences, joys, sorrows, needs, ability to think and make decisions, and ability to interact with others will all have an impact on the client’s feelings of comfort and well-being (see Chapters 7, 8, and 11). Therefore, to provide good care, it is necessary to use a holistic approach to client care. Support workers can play a key role in promoting the comfort and well-being of clients by understanding their psychosocial needs and knowing ways to help them feel safe, comfortable, and relaxed.

    For example, Ms. Lalonde, 35, lives with a disorder that has caused paralysis. You help her to bathe and dress. You change her bed, do her laundry, and clean her house. You also position her in bed to make her more comfortable. She feels more relaxed and comfortable in fresh clothes and a clean environment. You also create an environment that is conducive to comfort, rest, and sleep.

    Promoting Client Comfort And Well-Being

    A comfortable client is not in any physical or emotional pain and feels content and at peace (see Chapter 21 for a description of the furniture that is found in a typical client’s facility room that helps to support the client’s safety). Age, illness, pain, and inactivity, as well as factors such as temperature, ventilation, odours, noise, lighting, and feeling safe, all affect a person’s comfort and feelings of well-being.

    1. • Temperature. Most people are comfortable when the room temperature is between 20°C and 23°C (68°F–74°F). Infants, older persons, and people with illnesses generally need higher room temperatures for comfort. Government legislation in some provinces dictates minimum comfortable temperatures in long-term care facilities. In home care settings, clients set the temperature they want. Some clients, however, may be concerned with the cost of heating. You can help these clients keep warm by providing them with extra clothing or blankets.
    2. • Ventilation. Stale room air affects comfort. Facilities have ventilation systems that ensure fresh air. In home care settings, you can open windows and doors and turn on fans, as the client desires. Protect clients from drafts by making sure they are dressed warmly, covered with blankets, and away from drafty areas.
    3. • Odours. Many bodily substances and fluids have unpleasant odours that can embarrass clients. Body, breath, and smoking odours may also offend some. Some clients can experience great discomfort from perfumes worn by a caregiver. Do not wear perfumes or any scented products when you are at work. Many foods, beverages, and smoking can also result in breath and body odours, so wash your hands and brush your teeth after you have eaten or smoked. If you do not have time to brush your teeth, use mouthwash or suck on a breath mint. Regardless of what you are expected to wear when providing care, your clothing must be clean and fresh for every shift. Never wear clothing (including underwear) that has been exposed to cooking odours from your home in the place where you provide client care. These odours can cling to fabrics and be offensive to others. Good hygiene, housekeeping practices, and ventilation help eliminate odours. To reduce odours in any workplace setting, do the following:

A client’s home can also be noisy, particularly when young children and teenagers live at home. When working in a home environment you can help control noise levels by talking quietly and handling equipment carefully.

    Pain And Its Impact On A Client’s Well-Being

    To experience discomfort or pain means to ache, hurt, or be sore. Discomfort and pain are subjective. Each of us will experience pain differently or to a different degree, which is influenced by our personal histories. Because you cannot see, hear, touch, or smell a client’s discomfort or pain, it is important to believe their complaints of discomfort . It is also important to pay attention to body language cues (see Chapter 9) from the client since a person’s body language can often indicate if they are uncomfortable. Body language can often reveal if the client is feeling any discomfort even if the client has not verbalized this or is unable to do so, because of their physical condition or the presence of dementia. Report client complaints and your observations to your supervisor.

    Pain is personal and differs for each client. What may be hurt to one client may be an ache to another. What one client calls sore, another may call burning. If a client complains of pain or discomfort, the client has pain or discomfort, and you must believe the client. Remember, you cannot see, hear, feel, or smell the pain. Pain may signal tissue damage.

    Pain is not just physical; clients also feel emotional, social, and spiritual pain. When a person is suffering, the whole self feels the pain. Clients in pain may be sad, impatient, irritable, or angry. As a support worker, you must be especially kind to and empathetic with your clients in pain.

    Some older people who have experienced pain for years may be accustomed to feeling pain and think of it as normal. Others may not report pain because they think it is a normal part of aging. Some clients live with impairments that may affect their ability to recognize pain (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease) or to report pain (e.g., aphasia).

    Types of Pain

    There are different types of pain:

    1. • Acute pain is felt suddenly from injury, disease, trauma, or surgery, when tissue is damaged; it usually lasts less than 6 months and decreases with healing.
    2. • Chronic pain (also known as persistent pain) lasts longer than 6 months. Pain is constant or occurs off and on. Arthritis and cancer are common causes of persistent pain.
    3. • Radiating pain is felt not just at the site of tissue damage but extends to nearby areas as well. Pain from a heart attack is often felt on the left side of the chest, left jaw, left shoulder, and left arm. However, women may experience a very different pain pattern during a heart attack (see Chapter 33). A diseased gallbladder can cause pain in the right upper abdomen, the back, and the right shoulder (FIGURE 23.1).
    4. • Referred pain is pain felt in a part of the body separate from the source of the pain (e.g., a kidney stone may produce pain in the groin).
    5. • Phantom limb pain is felt in a body part that is no longer there; it occurs as a result of the disruption of nerve endings in the stump. A client who has had a leg amputated may still feel leg pain.

    Factors Affecting Pain

    Pain does not affect all clients the same way. Many factors affect reactions to pain.

    1. • Past experience. A client may have had pain before. The severity of pain, its cause, how long it lasted, and whether relief occurred all affect the client’s current response to pain. Knowing what to expect can help or hinder a client in handling pain. Clients who have never experienced pain may be fearful because they do not know what to expect.
    2. • Anxiety. An anxious client feels troubled or threatened. Pain and anxiety are related—pain can cause anxiety, and anxiety can make the pain feel worse. Lessening anxiety, therefore, helps reduce pain. For example, the nurse explains to Mr. Schett that he will have pain after surgery and that he will receive medication for pain relief. When Mr. Schett feels pain after surgery, he knows what to expect—that medication will relieve it. This knowledge helps reduce his anxiety and, thus, the amount of pain he feels.

Ill and injured clients need more sleep than usual. Lack of rest and sleep affects how a client copes with pain. Pain seems worse when a client is tired or restless. 5. • Attention. The more a client thinks about the pain, the worse it can seem. Sometimes, pain is so severe that it is all a client thinks about. However, even mild pain can seem worse if a client dwells on it too much. Pain often seems worse at night when there are no distractions. 6. • The meaning of pain. Pain means different things to different people. Some see it as a sign of weakness or of a serious illness. Some clients ignore or deny their pain. Some clients may use their pain to avoid certain people or things, whereas some others use it to get attention. 7. • Support from others. Pain is easier to deal with when family and friends offer comfort and support. The presence of a friend or loved one can be very comforting to a person in pain. Clients who do not have caring family and friends must deal with their pain alone, a state that can increase fear, anxiety, and suffering. Be especially sensitive to clients who are suffering alone. 8. • Culture. Culture affects how a client responds to pain (see the Respecting Diversity: Cultural Aspects of Pain box). In some cultures, clients in pain show no reaction at all, whereas in other cultures, clients in pain display strong verbal and nonverbal reactions. 9. • Age. See the Focus on Older Persons: Pain Reactions box.

    Respecting Diversity

    Cultural Aspects of Pain

    Culturally acquired patterns of pain responses may also influence the neurophysiological and verbal responses to pain. A client’s expectations concerning pain may influence how much pain can be tolerated. Response to pain may be limited by language used to describe or report pain. The degree of pain expression does not necessarily correlate with pain intensity. Preferences for pain-coping strategies are usually determined by culture; thus, nontraditional interventions to manage pain need to be explored with the client. How people view and respond to pain may influence your choice of interventions.

    Implications for Support Workers

    1. • Be aware of perceived causal factors of pain (e.g., fate, lifestyle, punishment, witchcraft).
    2. • Emotional responses to pain (overt [obvious], stoic [bearing quietly]) vary among and within cultures.
    3. • Words used to express pain vary among cultures (hurt, ache, discomfort).
    4. • Personal and social meanings of pain and past pain experiences affect pain perception.
    5. • Definitions of pain change the perception of pain intensity.
    6. • Feelings about pain direct treatment.
    7. • The health care provider’s beliefs and expectations regarding pain expression sway pain-management strategies.
    8. • Therapeutic goals of pain management are influenced by cultural beliefs.

    Focus On Older Persons

    Pain Reactions

    Some older people have multiple health problems that cause pain, so they may think that a new pain is related to an existing health problem. Often, people who have experienced chronic pain do not report their pain because they are used to living with this pain. Other people who have chronic pain may think their pain is untreatable, so they do not report it. Sometimes, pre-existing chronic pain can mask any new types of pain the client may be experiencing. In other cases, clients may deny or ignore pain because of their fears that this pain may be a sign of an untreatable and advanced illness. Without relief from pain, the older person who is in pain experiences decreased appetite, changes in mentation (thought), or decreased mobility, which can lead to further health challenges.

    Older persons may also have conditions (e.g., dementia) that affect their pain perception, or they may be unable to reliably recognize and report that they are in pain. This situation places them at greater risk for undetected disease or injury. Pain alerts a client to illness or injury, so if pain is not felt, the client may not realize the presence of a problem and not seek health care.

    Some older people have disorders that affect their thinking and reasoning, and some cannot communicate verbally. The only indication of pain in such cases will be changes in behaviour, so report any changes in a client’s behaviour to your supervisor.

    Definitions

    Breathing

      1. Normal breathing is characterized by effortless, quiet, rhythmic (smooth) respirations.
      1. Occasional laboured breathing is characterized by episodic bursts of harsh, difficult, or wearing respirations.
      1. Short period of hyperventilation is characterized by intervals of rapid, deep breaths lasting a short period of time.
      1. Noisy, laboured breathing is characterized by negative-sounding respirations on inspiration or expiration. They may be loud, gurgling, or wheezing. They appear strenuous or wearing.
      1. Long period of hyperventilation is characterized by an excessive rate and depth of respirations lasting a considerable time.
      1. Cheyne-Stokes respirations are characterized by rhythmic waxing and waning of breathing from very deep to shallow respirations with periods of apnea (cessation of breathing).

    Negative Vocalizations

      1. None is characterized by speech or vocalization that has a neutral or pleasant quality.
      1. Occasional moan or groan is characterized by mournful or murmuring sounds, wail, or laments. Groaning is characterized by louder-than-usual, inarticulate involuntary sounds, often beginning and ending abruptly.
      1. Low-level speech with a negative or disapproving quality is characterized by muttering, mumbling, whining, grumbling, or swearing in a low volume with a complaining, sarcastic, or caustic tone.
      1. Repeated troubled calling out is characterized by phrases or words being used over and over in a tone that suggests anxiety, uneasiness, or distress.
      1. Loud moaning or groaning is characterized by mournful or murmuring sounds, wails, or laments in much louder than usual volume. Loud groaning is characterized by louder-than-usual inarticulate involuntary sounds, often beginning and ending abruptly.
      1. Crying is characterized by an utterance of emotion accompanied by tears. There may be sobbing or quiet weeping.

      1. Smiling or inexpressive: Smiling is characterized by upturned corners of the mouth, brightening of the eyes, and a look of pleasure or contentment. Inexpressive refers to a neutral, at ease, relaxed or blank look.
      1. Sad is characterized by an unhappy, lonesome, sorrowful, or dejected look. There may be tears in the eyes.
      1. Frightened is characterized by a look of fear, alarm, or heightened anxiety. Eyes appear wide open.
      1. Frown is characterized by a downward turn of the corners of the mouth. Increased facial wrinkling in the forehead and around the mouth may appear.
      1. Facial grimacing is characterized by a distorted, distressed look. The brow is more wrinkled, as is the area around the mouth. Eyes may be squeezed shut.

      1. Relaxed is characterized by a calm, restful, mellow appearance. The person seems to be taking it easy.
      1. Tense is characterized by a strained, apprehensive, or worried appearance. The jaw may be clenched. (Exclude any contractures.)
      1. Distressed pacing is characterized by activity that seems unsettled. There may be a fearful, worried, or disturbed element present. The rate may be faster or slower.

    Measures other than medications that control pain include distraction, relaxation, and guided imagery. Nurses and therapists teach clients these techniques, and as a support worker you may be trained to assist with some of them.

    1. • Distraction involves a change in a client’s focus of attention. Attention is directed away from the pain through distractions such as conversation, music, television, games, and needlepoint.

    Rest And Sleep

    To be rested means to be calm, at ease, relaxed, and free from anxiety and stress. Rest involves physical inactivity, but some people choose to do calming or relaxing activities while resting—for example, reading, listening to music, or watching television.

    A comfortable position and good body alignment are important for rest. A quiet setting and a clean, dry, and wrinkle-free bed promote rest as well. Some clients rest easier in a clean, neat, and uncluttered room. Many clients spend a great deal of time in bed, so it is important for a support worker to know the different types of beds and how to position them for the client’s comfort (see Chapter 21). Clients may sleep in very different ways. Some may not have slept in a bed for most of their lives, perhaps for cultural reasons. Some may want to sleep sitting in a chair for ease of breathing; for example, a client with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) would find it easier to breathe in the sitting position. The client’s personal preference is very important when promoting rest and sleep; for example, clients should choose the number of blankets they want on the bed.

    Basic needs must be met for clients to rest. Thirst, hunger, elimination needs, pain, discomfort, anxiety, and fear, as well as unmet needs for love and belonging, can affect rest. As a support worker you can promote rest by meeting clients’ needs. For clients living in a facility or living alone, visits or telephone calls from family and friends may promote relaxation. (See the Providing Compassionate Care: Helping Clients to Rest box).

    You will need to plan and organize care so that clients can rest without interruptions. Some clients feel refreshed after resting for 15 or 20 minutes, whereas others need more time. Health care routines usually allow time for an afternoon rest.

    Clients with illnesses or injuries need to rest often. Some need to rest during or after receiving care. For example, a bath tires Mr. Rajan, so to gather the energy to dress, he must rest in a chair. Some clients need up to a few hours to complete oral hygiene, bathing, grooming, and dressing. Others need to rest after meals. It is important not to rush clients. Allow rest periods, as needed.

    The physician may order bed rest for a client (see Chapter 15 for a discussion on bed rest, its complications, and guidance on preventing complications). Sleep is a basic physical need. It saves the body energy, lets the mind and body rest, and allows body functions to slow down. During sleep, vital-sign levels fall and tissue heals and repairs itself. Sleep lowers stress, tension, and anxiety. After sleep, a person usually feels refreshed, more energetic, and mentally alert. During sleep, the normal adult passes through four to six cycles of nonrapid eye movement (NREM) and rapid eye movement (REM) phases—four levels of NREM and one level of REM in each cycle. Children and infants sleep more deeply, whereas older persons sleep more lightly.

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Helping Clients to Rest

    1. • Dignity. Protecting a client’s dignity can promote rest. Some clients may find hospital gowns embarrassing and may rest better wearing their own gowns or pyjamas. Many clients feel better about themselves when they are clean and well groomed. Help clients with their personal hygiene and grooming before rest.
    2. • Independence. Many clients follow rituals or routines before resting—for example, going to the bathroom, brushing their teeth, having a snack or beverage, praying, locking doors, and making sure loved ones are safe at home. Some clients have a favourite blanket. Ask clients about their preferences, and help them follow their rituals and routines, when possible.
    3. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. Everybody’s reaction to pain and comfort is different. We should always believe any client who complains of pain.
    4. • Preferences. Allow clients to do as much as possible without assistance. Clients decide when and where they want to rest. Provide a restful environment according to their wishes.
    5. • Privacy. Lack of privacy can make rest impossible, so close the door and privacy curtains, if the client so desires.
    6. • Safety. The client’s safety needs must be met (see Chapter 20) to achieve a good rest. Clients trying to rest must feel that they are safe from falls or other injuries. In facilities, the call bell must be within reach. Understanding the reasons for their treatments and knowing how procedures are done can also help clients feel safe, so make sure you explain procedures before they are performed.

    The amount of sleep required varies for each age group and declines with age (TABLE 23.1). Clients may require more sleep when they are sick or recovering from illness or injury (see the Focus on Older Persons: Sleep box).

    Several factors affect the amount of sleep a person needs and the quality of sleep a person gets. Quality refers to how well a person sleeps. Does the person sleep soundly and feel refreshed in the morning? Or are they restless and wakeful during sleep?

    1. • Illness. Discomfort, pain, nausea, and coughing can affect sleep. Also, clients may be awakened frequently for treatment or medication.
    2. • Nutrition. Certain foods and drinks can affect sleep—for example, those containing caffeine (such as coffee, chocolate, tea, and colas). A protein found in milk, cheese, and beef can promote sleep.
    3. • Exercise. Exercise makes people tired and helps them sleep. However, it is also a stimulant. Exercising before bed may disrupt sleep. Allow at least 2 hours between exercise and bedtime.
    4. • Environment. Most people sleep better in their own beds and in familiar surroundings. Any change in the environment, as well as noise and light, can affect sleep. Promote a quiet environment with the amount of light preferred by the client.
    5. • Medications. Sleeping pills promote sleep. Medications for anxiety, depression, and pain can cause drowsiness and can also interfere with sleep. The person may not feel mentally alert or refreshed the next day.
    6. • Alcohol. Alcohol disrupts normal sleep patterns. The person may wake up and have difficulty falling asleep again.
    7. • Change and stress. Change disrupts sleep. Change can range from small variances in one’s routine, such as staying up late to watch a game on television, to stressful life events, such as a new job or a divorce.
    8. • Emotional problems. Fear, worry, anxiety, and depression affect sleep. People in emotional distress may have difficulty falling asleep, or they may wake up often and have problems falling asleep again.
    9. • Nocturia. Nocturia, the need to urinate during the night, disrupts sleep (see Chapter 29). With advancing age, bladder tone is reduced, increasing the need to void frequently.

    Sleep Disorders

    Sleep disorders are persistent problems that affect the amount and quality of sleep and can cause fatigue, irritability, poor judgement, and other problems. Signs and symptoms of sleep disorders are listed in BOX 23.5. Routines in many facilities do not accommodate residents’ individual sleep-and-wake cycles.     Insomnia is a persistent condition in which the person affected cannot go to sleep or stay asleep throughout the night. The person may also wake early and be unable to fall asleep again. Clients with illnesses or injuries often suffer from insomnia because they may be depressed or anxious, may be feeling pain and discomfort, or may be afraid of dying during sleep.

    Sleep Deprivation

    Sleepwalking

    Sleepwalkers walk about while they are sleeping, often for several minutes. They are not aware that they are sleepwalking and have no memory of doing so on awakening. Children sleepwalk more than adults do. Stress, fatigue, and some medications can cause sleepwalking. The risk of falling during sleepwalking is great. Clients with illnesses may trip or pull out tubes and catheters. Guide sleepwalking clients back to their beds. Awaken them gently, as they can startle easily.

    4. • Allow flexible bedtimes. Bedtime is whenever the client is ready to sleep.
    5. • Ensure a comfortable room temperature.
    6. • Help the client take a warm bath or shower.
    7. • Provide a bedtime snack, if needed.
    8. • Have the client avoid caffeine and alcohol.
    9. • Have the client void before going to bed. Make sure incontinent clients are clean and dry.
    10. • Follow the client’s bedtime rituals.
    11. • Make sure the client wears loose-fitting nightwear.
    12. • Provide for warmth (blankets, socks).
    13. • Make sure linens are clean, dry, and wrinkle-free.
    14. • Allow the client to read, listen to music, or watch television if they so desire.
    15. • Stay and talk with the client.
    16. • Reduce noise.
    17. • Darken the room: close shades, blinds, and curtains. Shut off or dim the lights in the room and hallway.
    18. • Position the client in good body alignment. Support body parts, as ordered.
    19. • Implement measures to relieve pain.
    20. • Give a back massage, if ordered.
    21. • Assist with relaxation exercises, as ordered.

    Your Role in Promoting Rest and Sleep

    If required, measures to promote sleep are included in the client’s care plan (BOX 23.7). Check the care plan to make sure you are giving correct care. Observe the client closely, and report any of the signs and symptoms of sleep deprivation listed in BOX 23.6.

    As mentioned earlier, many clients have rituals and routines before bedtime—such as having a bedtime snack, watching a television program, or reading a book. Some long-term care clients may like to check on friends and loved ones before going to bed. Whatever the routine, it is important to the client, and you must help them with it as much as possible.

    Sleep disturbances are common with some types of dementia. In clients with dementia, confusion and restlessness often increase at night, and night wandering is common. Night wandering in a safe and supervised setting can be helpful for some clients (see Chapter 38).

    The vital signs of body function are temperature, pulse, respirations, and blood pressure. Some people also consider basic assessment and observation of pain to be a vital sign (see Chapter 23). It may also include oxygen saturation levels (SpO2), which will require special training by your employer.

    Vital signs reflect the function of three body processes essential for life: (1) regulation of body temperature, (2) breathing, and (3) heart function. Measuring and recording vital signs, as well as height and weight, provide important information for the care-planning process. Your job description will tell you the vital signs that you are approved to perform on your client. Usually, support workers can measure and record temperature, pulse, and respirations, and, in some agencies or facilities, oxygen saturation levels (SpO2). It is beyond the caregiver’s scope of practice to offer any treatment (such as withholding or giving medication) based on a client’s vital sign reading. However, it is very important that you report any changes in your client’s vital signs to your supervisor immediately.

    Measuring Height And Weight

    Height and weight are measured sometimes daily, weekly, or monthly as part of a client’s care plan. Clients who must have their weight monitored may be cared for in a facility or in their home. Some clients may be weighed daily when there is concern about fluid retention (e.g., in congestive heart failure) and to monitor weight gain or loss. It is important to weigh the client at the same time of day for daily, weekly, or monthly measurements and to use the same scale. Before breakfast is the best time to weigh the client, as food and fluids add weight.

    The client should wear only a gown or pyjamas when being weighed, as clothes add weight. Shoes or slippers add to the weight and the height. Also, have the client void (urinate) before being weighed, as a full bladder also affects weight measurement. If a urine specimen is needed, it can be collected at this time.

    There are various types of scales: balance beam scales, chair scales, lift scales, wheelchair weigh scales, and digital scales (FIGURE 24.1). The most common weigh scale used in facilities is the weigh scale in the bathtub lift chair. Balance beam scales are used for clients who are able to stand, and chair and lift scales are used for those who are unable to stand. Wheelchair scales are used for clients in wheelchairs. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions and employer policy when using scales. You must ensure that you weigh your client when they are wearing the same clothing, at the same time of the day, and on the same scale (see the Measuring Height and Weight and Measuring Height: The Client in Bed procedures).

    Measuring And Reporting Vital Signs

    A person’s vital signs can vary within certain limits, and they can be affected by medications, pain, illness, activity, exercise, sleep, foods, fluids, smoking, and emotions (e.g., excitement, anger, fear, and anxiety).

    Vital signs are measured to detect changes in normal body function. They can indicate response to treatment and signal life-threatening events. Vital signs are part of the assessment process and are measured:

    1. • During physical examinations
    2. • When a client is admitted to a facility
    3. • Several times a day for hospital clients and clients in subacute care units
    4. • Before and after surgery
    5. • Before and after complex procedures or diagnostic tests
    6. • After certain care measures such as ambulation
    7. • After a fall or other injury
    8. • When medications that could affect the respiratory or circulatory system are taken
    9. • Whenever the client complains of pain, dizziness, light-headedness, shortness of breath, rapid heart rate, or not feeling well
    10. • As often as dictated by the client’s condition
    11. • As ordered by the doctor
    12. • As stated on the care plan (usually daily or weekly)
    13. • As instructed after the client has been given medication to relieve a fever

    Vital signs reveal even minor changes in a client’s condition. Accuracy is essential when you measure, record, and report vital signs. If you are unsure of the accuracy of your measurements, promptly ask your supervisor to take them again. Unless otherwise ordered, take vital signs with the client at rest and when the client is lying down or sitting. You should always compare any readings to the client’s baseline reading (a baseline reading refers to the first set of recorded vital signs obtained on the client). Immediately report:

    1. • Any vital sign that is changed from a previous measurement
    2. • Vital signs above the normal range
    3. • Vital signs below the normal range

    Body Temperature

    Body temperature is the amount of heat in the body. It is a balance between the amount of heat produced from the client’s metabolic rate and the amount of heat lost by the body. It remains within a normal, safe range when a person is healthy. Temperature normally changes slightly throughout the day and in response to different factors such as age, weather, exercise, pregnancy, the menstrual cycle, emotions, and stress. It is lower in the morning and higher in the afternoon and evening. Illness also affects body temperature, and temperatures above or below the normal range signal illness or health problems.

To convert Celsius to Fahrenheit, multiply the Celsius reading by 1.8 and then add 32.

Axillary temperature is often taken on infants, very young children, and older persons (see the Taking Temperature With an Electronic Thermometer procedure).

    Pulse

    Pulse is the beat of the heart felt at an artery as a wave of blood passes through the artery. The pulse is felt every time the heart beats (see Chapter 32 for information about the structure and function of the heart and blood vessels.)

    Sites for Taking the Pulse

    You can feel the pulse by placing your fingertips over certain sites on the body. The temporal, carotid, brachial, radial, femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis (pedal), and posterior tibial pulses are felt on both sides of the body (FIGURE 24.16). The pulse is easy to feel at these sites because the arteries are close to the body’s surface and lie over a bone.

    To take the pulse, support workers often use the radial site because it is easy to reach and find and because the pulse can be taken without disturbing or exposing the client. The carotid pulse (in adults and children) and the brachial pulse or apical pulse (in infants) are taken during emergencies.

    Rate and Rhythm of the Pulse

    Pulse rate is the number of heartbeats or pulses felt in 1 minute. If you are taking a pulse of a client you are unfamiliar with, always take the pulse for 1 full minute. The rate varies for each age group (TABLE 24.2) and is affected by many factors, including the following:

    2. • Exercise
    3. • Pain

    6. • Caffeine

    Respirations

    Respiration is the act of breathing air into (inhalation) and out of (exhalation) the lungs. Oxygen enters the lungs during inhalation, and carbon dioxide leaves the lungs during exhalation. Each respiration involves one inhalation and one exhalation. The chest rises during inhalation and falls during exhalation (see Chapter 32 for an overview of the respiratory system).

    The healthy adult has 12 to 20 respirations per minute. The respiratory rate is affected by the same factors that affect temperature and pulse. For example, infections, heart disease, and respiratory diseases usually increase the respiratory rate.

    Respirations are normally quiet, effortless, and regular. Both sides of the chest rise and fall in a uniform manner. If the chest barely moves during respiration, the breathing is described as shallow. Deep breathing occurs when the chest rises and falls significantly with every breath (see Chapter 39 and Chapter 47 for a description of abnormal respiratory patterns).

    Count respirations when the client is at rest. Position the client so that you can see the chest rise and fall. To a certain extent, a client can control the depth and rate of breathing. Clients tend to change breathing patterns when they know their respirations are being counted, so try to count respirations without making the client aware of what you are doing.

    Respirations are counted right after taking the pulse (see the Counting Respirations procedure). Keep your fingers over the pulse site and maintain the pressure so that the client believes you are still taking the pulse. To count respirations, watch the chest rise and fall. Count how many times the chest rises and falls in 30 seconds. Remember, the rise and fall of the chest together is counted as one respiration. Multiply the number by two for the number of respirations in 1 minute. If an abnormal pattern is noted, count the respirations for 1 minute.

    The period of heart muscle contraction is called systole, and the period of heart muscle relaxation is called diastole.

    Both the systolic and diastolic pressures are measured. The systolic pressure, which is the higher pressure, represents the amount of force needed to pump blood out of the heart into the arterial circulation. The diastolic pressure, which is the lower pressure, reflects the pressure in the arteries when the heart is at rest.

    Blood pressure is measured in millimetres (mm) of mercury (Hg). The systolic pressure is recorded over the diastolic pressure. The average adult has a systolic pressure of 120 mm Hg and a diastolic pressure of 80 mm Hg. This reading is written as 120/80 mm Hg.

    BOX 24.1 Factors Affecting Blood Pressure

    1. • Age. Blood pressure increases with age. It is lowest in infancy and childhood and highest in adulthood.
    2. • Gender. Women usually have lower blood pressures compared with men, but blood pressures tend to rise in women after menopause.
    3. • Blood volume.  Blood volume is the amount of blood in the system. Severe bleeding lowers the blood volume and thus the blood pressure. Giving intravenous (IV) fluids rapidly increases the blood volume, raising the blood pressure as a result.
    4. • Stress. Stress, including anxiety, fear, and other emotions, can increase blood pressure as the body responds.
    5. • Pain. Generally, pain increases blood pressure. However, severe pain can cause shock, which can seriously lower blood pressure (see Chapters 23 and 33).
    6. • Exercise. Physical activity increases blood pressure; therefore, blood pressure should not be measured immediately after exercise.
    7. • Weight. Blood pressure is higher in overweight people and is lowered by weight loss.
    8. • Ethnicity. Blood pressure tends to be higher among Canadians of South Asian, Indigenous, or African descent.
    9. • Diet. A high-sodium diet increases the amount of water in the body, and the extra fluid volume, in turn, increases blood pressure.
    10. • Medications. Medications can be given to raise or lower blood pressure. Other medications can cause high or low blood pressure as an adverse effect.
    11. • Position. In clients who are on blood pressure medication, blood pressure may be lower when lying down and higher when standing. Sudden position changes can cause sudden changes in blood pressure (orthostatic hypotension). When a person stands suddenly, a rapid drop in blood pressure can occur, causing dizziness and fainting (see Chapter 15).
    12. • Smoking. Smoking increases blood pressure. The nicotine in cigarettes causes blood vessels to narrow, making the heart work harder to pump blood through narrowed vessels.
    13. • Alcohol. Excessive alcohol intake can raise blood pressure.

    The role of the support worker in taking blood pressure can vary from employer to employer and province to province. Ensure that you are aware of your employer’s policy.

    Factors Affecting Blood Pressure

    Blood pressure can change from minute to minute and can be affected by a number of factors (BOX 24.1). Because blood pressure can vary so easily, normal blood pressure has been assigned a range of values:

    1. • Systolic pressure—normal range is between 100 and 140 mm Hg
    2. • Diastolic pressure—normal range is between 60 and 90 mm Hg

    Persistent measurements above the normal ranges of systolic and diastolic pressures are considered abnormal, and the condition is referred to as hypertension (see Chapter 33). According to the Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada, blood pressure that is consistently more than 140/90 mm Hg is considered high, but in clients with diabetes, 130/80 mm Hg is considered high. Normal blood pressure is below 120/80 mm Hg. In young and middle-aged adults, report any systolic pressure above 140 mm Hg at once. Also, report if the diastolic pressure is above 90 mm Hg. Likewise, systolic pressure below 90 mm Hg and diastolic pressure below 60 mm Hg must be reported, as low readings can be a condition referred to as hypotension. Low blood pressure may be normal in some clients, but hypotension may be a sign of a life-threatening problem (see the Focus on Children: Blood Pressure and Focus on Older Persons: Blood Pressure boxes.)

    1. • Do not take blood pressure on an arm with an intravenous (IV) infusion, a cast, or a dialysis access site. If a client has had breast surgery, do not take blood pressure on that side. Avoid taking blood pressure on an injured arm.
    2. • Allow the client to rest for 10 to 20 minutes before measuring blood pressure.
    3. • Measure blood pressure with the client sitting or lying down, unless the physician has ordered blood pressure to be measured with the client in the standing position.
    4. • Use the correct-sized blood pressure cuff for each client. For example, use a pediatric cuff for a very small arm.
    5. • Apply the cuff to the bare upper arm. Remove any clothing that can affect the measurement.
    6. • Make sure the cuff is snug, as loose cuffs can cause inaccurate readings.
    7. • Place the diaphragm of the stethoscope firmly over the brachial artery. The entire diaphragm must be in contact with the skin.
    8. • Make sure the room is quiet. Talking, TV, radio, and sounds from the hallway can affect an accurate measurement.
    9. • Ensure that the manometer is clearly visible at eye level.
    10. • Measure the systolic and diastolic pressures. Expect to hear the first blood pressure sound at the point where you last felt the radial or brachial pulse. The first sound is the systolic pressure, and the point where the sound disappears is the diastolic pressure (see FIGURE 24.23).
    11. • Take the blood pressure again if you are not sure of an accurate measurement. Wait 30 to 60 seconds before repeating the measurement.
    12. • Notify your supervisor at once if you cannot “hear” the blood pressure.
    13. • Know the normal blood pressure ranges for the client. Immediately report any blood pressure measurement above or below the client’s normal range.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • Vital signs are measured to detect changes in normal body functions. They can indicate response to treatment and signal life-threatening events.

    • Vital signs reveal even minor changes in a client’s condition. Accuracy is essential when you measure, record, and report vital signs.

    • Body temperature normally changes slightly throughout the day. Temperatures above or below your client’s normal reading can indicate illness or other health problems.

    • A variety of thermometers are available. Agencies will determine the type to use for each client.

    • Pulse rate is the number of heartbeats heard in 1 minute. It varies with age and can be affected by a number of factors. The force of the pulse relates to its strength.

    • To take the pulse, support workers often use the radial site. Any irregular pulse should be counted for 1 full minute to obtain the most accurate reading.

    • Support workers may use a pulse oximeter to measure both a client’s pulse rate and the oxygen level in the blood.

    • Respirations are normally quiet, effortless, and regular. Normal respiratory rates vary with age. Abnormal rates can indicate illness or health issues in all ages.

    • Blood pressure is the amount of force exerted by the blood against the walls of an artery. Blood pressure can change from minute to minute and can be affected by a number of factors.

    • The period of heart contraction is called systole, and the period of heart muscle relaxation is called diastole. Measuring blood pressure is a delegated act in many areas. Ensure that you know your scope of practice and your employer’s policies.

    Combining Word Elements

    Medical terms are formed by combining word elements. A root can be combined with prefixes, other roots, or suffixes. The prefix dys (difficult) can be combined with the root pnea (breathing) to form the term dyspnea, meaning “difficulty in breathing.” Similarly, the root mast (breast) combined with the suffix ectomy (excision or removal) forms the term mastectomy, which means “removal of a breast.” Combining a prefix, root, and suffix is another way to form medical terms. Endocarditis (meaning “inflammation of the inner part of the heart”) consists of the prefix endo (inner), the root card (heart), and the suffix itis (inflammation).

    More complex combinations of prefixes, roots, and suffixes can be created:

    1. • Two prefixes, a root, and a suffix
    2. • A prefix, two roots, and a suffix
    3. • Two roots and a suffix

    The important things to remember are that (1) prefixes always come before roots and (2) suffixes always come after roots. You can practise forming medical terms by combining the word elements listed in this chapter.

    Abdominal Regions

    The abdomen is divided into regions (FIGURE 25.1) to help describe the location of body structures, pain, or discomfort. The regions are as follows:

    1. • Right upper quadrant (RUQ)
    2. • Left upper quadrant (LUQ)
    3. • Right lower quadrant (RLQ)
    4. • Left lower quadrant (LLQ)

    Directional Terms

    Certain terms describe the position of one body part in relation to another. These terms indicate the direction of the body part when a person is standing and facing forward. The following directional terms come from some of the prefixes listed earlier in this chapter:

    1. • Anterior (ventral)—located at or toward the front of the body or body part
    2. • Distal—the part farthest from the centre or from the point of attachment; for example, the foot is distal to the knee
    3. • Lateral—the farthest away from the midline of the body
    4. • Medial—the closest to the midline of the body
    5. • Posterior (dorsal)—the back surface of the body—often used to indicate the position of one structure to another
    6. • Proximal—nearest to the trunk of the body or the point of origin

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • Medical terms are formed by combining prefixes, roots, and suffixes, which come mostly from Greek and Latin. Understanding how medical words are formed will help you understand them better.

    • The root contains the basic meaning of the word; a prefix comes before the root; a suffix comes after a root. Prefixes and suffixes always change the basic meaning of a word and are never used alone.

    • The abdominal regions are the right upper quadrant, the left upper quadrant, the right lower quadrant, and the left lower quadrant.

    • The terms that describe the position of one body part in relation to another include anterior (ventral), distal, lateral, medial, posterior (dorsal), and proximal.

    • Each agency, facility, or province or territory will have a list of acceptable abbreviations. As a support worker, you must ask your employer for the list of abbreviations you are expected to use when charting and never use any abbreviations not on the list.
