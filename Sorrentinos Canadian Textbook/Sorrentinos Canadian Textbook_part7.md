## Safety Measures at Home and in the Workplace

Safety is something we take for granted until someone we know or love is injured. We need to be aware of safety hazards in our homes as well as in our workplaces, wherever that may be. As a support worker, you have the responsibility of ensuring your clients’ and co-workers’ safety as well as your own. Doing so begins with the recognition of common safety hazards (see the Supporting Mrs. Gerhlbardt: Creating a Safer Environment box) and reporting any lapses in safety measures immediately to the proper authority, usually your supervisor.

### Preventing Falls and Injuries

Falls are the most common cause of accidental injuries in all settings, with children and older people being at greatest risk. While you should always supervise all of your clients, you should pay particular attention to children, clients in wheelchairs, clients with a history of injury, sedated clients, older clients, and cognitively impaired clients, as they are at greatest risk for injuries related to falls. Falls may result in death, serious injuries, or changes in the older person’s quality of life. For example, after a fall, an older person may avoid leaving the house because they fear falling again, but staying in the house may lead to feelings of isolation, anxiety, and depression. It may also lead to increased dependence on others.[6](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur206)

Falls remain the leading cause of injury-related hospitalizations among Canadian older persons; between 20 and 30% of older people fall each year, and absolute numbers are on the rise.[6](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur206) Falls can lead to negative mental health outcomes, such as fear of falling, loss of autonomy and greater isolation, confusion, immobilization, and depression. In addition to the negative physical and mental health consequences of falling, there are significant associated financial costs, estimated at $2 billion annually, a value 3.7 times greater than that for younger adults.[6](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur206)

Clients who live with impairments that affect their abilities to think, remember, communicate, or concentrate, such as brain injuries, mental health disorders, dementia, and other conditions that cause confusion and disorientation, have an increased risk for falling.

Many facilities have fall prevention programs, which incorporate a variety of strategies to prevent falls. Some of these strategies include the following:

1. • Clients in facilities are assessed to determine their risk for falls, and those who are at high risk are provided extra supervision and assistance.
2. • Staff who work with clients receive up-to-date information and training on how to move and care for clients at risk for falls. This education for staff includes training on how to use equipment properly and how to avoid getting injured themselves.
3. • Methods used to identify clients at risk include a colour-coded bracelet worn by the client and a sign, sticker, or tag placed on the client’s chart, at the nurses’ station, or, in a few agencies, above the client’s bed to indicate that the client is at greater risk of falling. Wherever these signs are used, they should be placed in such a way as to be respectful of the client’s right to privacy.
4. • Since most falls occur in bedrooms, bathrooms, and on stairs, particular attention is paid to making these areas as safe as possible.
5. • Since falls sometimes occur when clients rush to get to the bathroom or commode without help, they are repeatedly reminded to ask for assistance.
6. • Most falls in facilities occur in the evening, between 1800 hours (6:00 p.m.) and 2100 hours (9:00 p.m.), when the hallways may be darker than they were during the day. For this reason, keep hallways well-lit and free of obstacles or clutter.
7. • Falls also are more likely during shift changes, when most of the staff are trying to listen to the shift report. To remedy this risk, many agencies use overlapping shifts so that staff remains available to check on clients while the shift report is taking place.
8. • The safety measures listed in the Think About Safety: Measures to Prevent Falls Among Older Persons and Others at Risk box help prevent falls in home and facility settings. Most of these measures are included in fall prevention programs and care plans. Always follow the client’s care plan for specific safety measures. Students and staff alike could benefit from participating in any fall prevention workshops offered by the facility.

### Preventing Falls in Children

Falls are the leading cause of injuries in children, and accidental injuries are the leading cause of death among Canadian children.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur205) Infants can easily roll off a change table or bed. Falls out of highchairs and infant seats and falls down the stairs are also common accidents among babies and children. Older children often fall when running or playing. When children are tired, they may not pay close attention to the potential dangers around them and may also have a delay in reaction time, which contributes to risk for an injury. Refer to the Think About Safety: Safety Measures to Prevent Falls Among Infants and Children box for ways to help ensure a child’s safety.

### Using the Call Bell

Call bells are important safety devices for clients in facilities such as hospitals and long-term care residences. These devices may vary from one facility to another, so it is important to become familiarized with the devices’ use at each facility. A call bell enables a client to call for assistance. Different facilities use different call systems. The term call bell is used in this book when referring to any of a number of call systems that a facility may use. Most systems consist of a cord that is attached to the bed or a chair. A button is at the end of the cord ([FIGURE 20.9](#f0050)). To get help, the client presses the button, which causes a signal to ring at the nurses’ station. Or pressing the button may turn on a light above the client’s door and at the nurses’ station ([FIGURE 20.10, A](#f0055)). These signals alert the health care team that the client needs assistance.

Some call bells are also connected to an intercom at the nurses’ station ([FIGURE 20.10, B](#f0055)). Using the intercom, a support worker or a nurse can talk with the client who has called for help. In newer facilities, the call bell system may be directly connected to cordless phones carried by the staff. As a support worker in the facility, you would respond directly to the client by phone, and the client can tell you what is needed. However, be careful when using an intercom or a cell phone, as people nearby can hear what you say; keep in mind the client’s right to confidentiality.

1. • Report throw rugs and small mats that are not secure. They can be secured with carpet tape or nonskid backing. Make sure throw rugs and small mats are not in high-traffic areas or at the top of stairways.
2. • Report loose floorboards and tiles. Report frayed, torn, or bumpy rugs and carpets.
3. • Keep traffic areas free of telephone and electrical cords. Cords and wires can be taped to the wall so the client will not trip over them.

6. • Clean up water and other spills immediately.

Safety Measures to Prevent Falls Among Infants and Children

1. • Do not leave infants and young children unsupervised. Never leave them unattended in highchairs or on a table, couch, bed, or other high surface.

4. • Use safety gates at the top and bottom of stairs. Make sure the child cannot get caught in the slats.
5. • Keep one hand on a child lying on a scale, bed, change table, or other furniture ([FIGURE 20.8](#f0045)).
6. ![Set of two photos. Hand of a person holding a call light at the top (left). Hand of a person wearing ID bracelet is shown pressing button of the call light (right).](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000201/main.assets/f20-08-9780323711654.jpg)

## Preventing Poisoning

The most common ways by which adults are poisoned are by eating or drinking contaminated food and water and by overdosing on medications. Carelessness, confusion, and difficulty reading medication labels can lead to accidental overdose. For example, confused or disoriented adults may eat poisonous substances. Sometimes poisoning occurs as a suicide attempt. The safety measures for children ([FIGURE 20.12](#f0065)) should be followed for adults as well. Common harmful substances include the following:

1. • Medications and vitamins
2. • Household cleaners, such as bleach; drain cleaner; floor wax; furniture polish; soaps; detergents; and toilet, window, and oven cleaners
3. • Personal care products, such as aftershave, rubbing alcohol, nail polish and remover, shampoo and
   conditioner, bath oil, bubble bath, cosmetics, perfumes, deodorant, mouthwash, diaper wipes, and baby oils and powders
4. ![A, Kid opening the low cabinets and touching household cleaner's bottle. B, Kid taking the medicine out of wrapper and placing them in hands.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000201/main.assets/f20-12-9780323711654.jpg)

### Preventing Accidental Poisoning

All children, especially when they are very young, put things in their mouths as part of learning and exploring their world. This natural instinct is one reason hundreds of children every year are victims of accidental poisonings.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur205) Parents, guardians, and caregivers can prevent many of these accidents by identifying and locking away toxic materials.

Accidental poisoning is most common in children between the ages of 1 and 4 years, when they are crawling or walking and exploring their environments. Eating substances such as aspirin, cigarette butts, or vitamin pills is a common cause of childhood poisoning. Poisoning also occurs when certain poisonous substances—for example, oven cleaner or pesticide—spill on the skin and are absorbed into the body.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur205) (See the Think About Safety: Ways to Prevent Accidental Poisoning box.)

### What to Do if You Suspect Poisoning

In community settings, the phone number for the local poison control centre should always be posted near the telephone, programmed into your cell phone, or carried in your bag. You might suspect poisoning if you find empty pill bottles or hazardous products lying around. You might also suspect poisoning if the client suddenly collapses, vomits, or has difficulty breathing. If you suspect poisoning, follow these steps:

* 1. Contact the emergency medical services (EMS) for your area by dialling 9-1-1 on the telephone. The EMS operator will give you clear instructions.
* 2. Gather any empty pill bottles or other evidence of poisoning to determine what has been ingested and how much.
* 3. Stay with your client. Be sure to remain calm.

## Preventing Burns

Often, burns to older persons occur in the kitchen—for example, while cooking, the person may be wearing clothes with loose-fitting sleeves, which could dangle over the burner and catch fire. Bathwater and heating pads that are too hot can cause burns. Careless smoking or unattended cigarette butts in ashtrays are also common causes of burns (see the Think About Safety: Measures to Prevent Burns box).

### Protecting Children From Burns

Accidental burns are common in children and can cause significant morbidity (illness) and mortality (deaths). Accidents are the leading cause of deaths in children in Canada.[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur207) Degrees of burns are determined by the extent of tissue damage. Children and older persons are both at great risk for burns.

## Preventing Accidents with Equipment

As a support worker, you will be required to use equipment such as household appliances, mechanical lifting devices, wheelchairs, and walkers in clients’ homes as well as in facilities. All equipment is unsafe if broken, not used correctly, or not working properly.

When using any type of equipment, you must know what you are doing. You also must make sure that the equipment is in safe working order (see the Think About Safety: Measures to Prevent Equipment Accidents box).

Ways to Prevent Accidental Poisoning

1. •
   Read the labels on all household products before buying them, and try to use the least toxic ones.
    Among the household products generally considered less hazardous are nonchlorine bleaches, vinegar, borax, beeswax, mineral oil, and compressed air drain openers (rather than corrosive liquids).
2. •
   Lock up all medicines and harmful substances.
    Secure all cupboards that contain poisonous substances, even those you might assume to be out of reach. Poison experts have come across numerous cases of toddlers dragging a chair over to a kitchen counter, climbing onto the counter or even the refrigerator, and opening a cupboard that is then at eye level.
3. •
   Do not rely on childproof container lids.
    These are not always childproof. No bottle top can be made so fail-safe that a child cannot find a way to get it off. It is not unusual for a 2-year-old, left alone for 30 minutes, to break into even the “safest” devices of a manufacturer.
4. •
   Keep medicines, pesticides, and even detergents in their original containers. Rewrite any labels that get smudged so that you know exactly what is in each bottle. Never put poisonous or toxic products in containers that were once used for food. Emergency medical staff have heard far too many horror stories of toxic liquid put into an unmarked container and having been mistaken for juice.
5. •
   Never refer to any kind of medicine as “sweets.”
    Even if you are trying to get a reluctant toddler to take his cough syrup, do not present it as something good to eat. Children learn by imitation, so when you are taking your own medications, make sure that they are not watching.
6. •
   Never let children play with chipped or flaking paint. Old paint contains lead, which is a toxic substance known to cause neurological damage in children. Remove all chipped paint and consult your local poison control centre or a reputable paint store for ways to remove the lead paint from your walls and woodwork.

Examples of Materials Found in the Home That Are Toxic

1. •
   Household cleaning products. Assume that they are all dangerous. The safest thing to do is to keep them all out of the reach of children or disoriented adults living in the home, including drain cleaner, oven cleaner, toilet cleaner, dishwasher detergent, and rust remover. Even if individuals do not ingest them, they may spray themselves with them, burning their eyes or skin.
2. •
   Pills and medicines. Always assume that any medicine the child or disoriented client takes—whether it has been prescribed for them or not—can be dangerous. Even vitamin supplements can be fatal in large doses, so keep them locked in a cupboard. Prescription drugs, especially antidepressants and time-release drugs, have a cumulative effect. Travel medicines, too, can be dangerous; keep them all inside a locked medicine cabinet.
3. •
   Cosmetics.
    Lock cosmetics in the medicine cabinet. Even an everyday product such as hair remover can be dangerous if swallowed.
4. •
   Household plants.
    Many garden plants are poisonous, especially philodendron, rue, privet, laurel berries, yew berries, and laburnum seeds. Make sure that you and all of the child’s or disoriented client’s caregivers know the names of all the plants and trees inside and outside the home, just in case. It is a good idea for caregivers to leave the tags on all items that are brought home from a plant nursery. An expert from a plant nursery can help identify the plant and also provide tags to place near the plants.
5. •
   Paint thinner and paint remover, gasoline, paraffin, and metal polishes. Keep these items locked out of reach in the garage, and do not let children or disoriented clients into a workshop unsupervised.
6. •
   Pesticides.
    Lock them away, even the “friendly” replacements for pesticides, because they can also make pets and people ill.

To protect children, older persons, and any others at risk from burns, do the following:

2. • In facilities, ensure that clients smoke only in designated smoking areas, if at all. Most facilities no longer allow smoking on their premises. Clients who smoke must be a designated distance away from the doors of the facility.
3. • Supervise clients who smoke and who cannot protect themselves. Some facilities insist that some or all of the clients who smoke wear “smoking jackets,” which are made of nonflammable material, to prevent burns in case of a dropped cigarette.
4. • Do not allow clients to smoke in bed. The client could fall asleep and drop the cigarette onto the bedsheets or on clothing and start a fire.
5. • Follow safety guidelines when applying heat and cold (see [Chapter 40](../B9780323709392000407/B9780323709392000407.xhtml)).
6. • Do not drink or carry hot liquids near infants and children. Make sure children are not in your way when you are carrying anything hot, including drinks, clothes irons, or dishes from the oven.
7. • Do not heat a baby bottle or baby food in a microwave oven. Always shake the bottle or stir the food and test the temperature before feeding the baby.

### Fires and the Use of Oxygen

Some clients have difficulty breathing or do not receive enough oxygen from the air, so oxygen therapy is prescribed by physicians. Oxygen therapy provides additional oxygen through a nasal tube or face mask. The oxygen is supplied through portable oxygen tanks, wall outlets, or oxygen concentrators (see [Chapter 39](../B9780323709392000390/B9780323709392000390.xhtml)).

Oxygen systems are widely used in facilities and for home care. However, oxygen is a serious fire hazard, as it supports combustion (see the Think About Safety: Using Oxygen Equipment Properly box). Therefore, you should ensure that combustible items (such as petroleum jelly on the client’s lips, or clothing, towels, and sheets that are static charged) are kept away from the source of the oxygen to reduce the risk of a fire. Special safety measures are needed where oxygen is used and stored (see the Think About Safety: Fire Prevention Measures box).

### Types of Burns

Burns can severely disfigure and disable a person and can even cause death. Most burn injuries occur in the home. Infants and children as well as older persons are at highest risk. Burns are described as being first degree (red, painful skin that is not blistered); second degree, which affects the epidermis and dermis layers (resulting in pain, redness, blistering, and swelling of the skin); and third degree, which extends below the dermis to underlying layers and nerves (resulting in blackened, charred skin, which may be numb).

Causes of burns include the following (also refer to [Chapter 40](../B9780323709392000407/B9780323709392000407.xhtml)):

1. • Dry heat—fire, stoves, space heaters
2. • Moist heat—hot liquids, steam
3. • Chemicals—oven cleaner, drain cleaner, rust remover, and so on
4. • Electricity—faulty electrical equipment, live wires, or lightning ([FIGURE 20.21](#f0110))
5. • Radiation—sunlight

Burns are more severe and require emergency help in the following circumstances:

1. • They are located on the head, face, neck, hands, feet, or genitals.
2. • They are spread over a large area of the body.
3. • The burned person is under 2 or over 50 years of age or has a pre-existing medical condition (such as diabetes or hypertension).

The size and depth of the burn also affect its severity.

## Preventing Suffocation

Suffocation occurs when breathing stops due to lack of oxygen. Brain damage or death may occur, depending on how long the person has been deprived of oxygen, sometimes within 3 to 4 minutes. Common causes of suffocation include choking, drowning, inhaling gas or smoke, strangulation, and electrical shock. Children under 1 year, clients who have difficulties chewing and swallowing, and clients with serious mobility disorder or intellectual disability are at risk for suffocation (see the Think About Safety: Measures to Prevent Suffocation in Adults box).

### Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

Carbon monoxide (CO), also sometimes known as the “silent killer,” is a colourless, odourless, tasteless gas, produced by the burning of material containing carbon, such as in common household appliances or fireplaces. CO is also present in car exhaust fumes, so vehicles running in confined spaces can cause CO poisoning. When automobiles, appliances, or fireplaces are not properly ventilated, carbon monoxide can build up in the air and enter the bloodstream of the unsuspecting people or animals that inhale the air. They have no idea what is happening to them—that this gas, instead of oxygen, is quickly binding to the hemoglobin in their red blood cells, which can be fatal (see [Chapter 39](../B9780323709392000390/B9780323709392000390.xhtml)).

### Carbon Monoxide Detectors

Carbon monoxide detectors are strongly recommended for all homes, especially in areas of the home where the family members gather and spend the most time, such as kitchens and bedrooms. It is important that the manufacturer’s instructions are followed and that the devices are checked periodically to ensure that they work properly. Some CO detectors are connected to household electricity (they may be hard-wired or plugged in), and some work on batteries. Batteries must be replaced routinely, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Carbon monoxide detectors are inexpensive, but they can save lives!

## Restraints and How to Avoid Using Them

A restraint is any device, garment, barrier, furniture, or medication that limits or restricts freedom of movement or access to one’s own body. Restraints are rarely used now; only a small number of the types of restraints that were allowed in the past are still allowed in Canada today. Only restraints currently in use will be discussed in this textbook.

It is important to know that every effort should be made to protect clients without resorting to the use of restraints. In the past, people were severely injured (or even died) as a result of restraint application, so laws that prohibit their general use have since been enacted.
Restraints can cause emotional harm and serious physical injury
 (see the Providing Compassionate Care: Understanding the Effects of Restraints on Clients box).
Restraints require a physician’s order, after consultation with the client’s family.

### Types of Restraints

There are three types of restraints:

* 1. Physical restraints—garments or devices used to restrict movement of the whole body or parts of the body. They are attached to a body part (e.g., a mitt restraint) or go around the client’s waist and wheelchair (e.g., a belt restraint) ([FIGURE 20.22](#f0115)). Seatbelts on wheelchairs and in child and infant care carriers are included in this section.
* 2. Environmental restraints—barriers, furniture, or devices that prevent free movement. Bed siderails are often considered to be a form of restraint. Environmental restraints are near the body, but not directly attached to it. They confine the client to a specific place such as a bed, chair, or room. Examples of environmental restraints include the following:

### Guidelines Regarding the Use of Restraints

All support workers need to keep in mind the guidelines discussed in this book regarding restraints. Remember that:

1. • Unnecessary restraint is false imprisonment (see [Chapter 5](../B9780323709392000055/B9780323709392000055.xhtml)). There must be a written consent in the client’s chart for using the restraint, and it must be indicated in the client’s care plan.
2. • Restraints are never used to discipline a client. Discipline is any action that punishes or penalizes.
3. • Restraints are never used for staff convenience. They should not be used to make the staff’s jobs easier. If a restraint must be used, the least restrictive method is chosen
   by the physician. If clients are in danger of harming themselves or others, physicians usually advise them to enter a facility, where safe care can be provided.
4. • Always try to determine the cause of the client’s agitation or behaviour (see the section Managing Responsive Behaviours, in [Chapter 38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml)). If the cause is known and addressed, the need for restraints can usually be prevented. When determining the causes of harmful behaviour, health care providers consider some of the following questions:
5. 1. • Is the client injured or in pain? Clients who feel uncomfortable may react in an agitated and even aggressive manner.
   2. • Is the client afraid?
   3. • Is the client seeing, hearing, or feeling things that are not real?
   4. • Is the client confused or disoriented?
   5. • Are medications causing the behaviour?
      A recent change in the client’s medications can cause a change in behaviour. This reaction should be reported immediately to the supervisor.
   6. • Is the behaviour occurring because the client has not taken the medication?
   7. • Does the client need to urinate or defecate?
   8. • Is clothing or a dressing tight or causing other discomfort?
   9. • Is the client’s position uncomfortable?
   10. • Is the client too hot, too cold, hungry, or thirsty?
   11. • Are body fluids, secretions, or excretions causing skin irritation?
6. •
   Restraint use should always be avoided. Most agencies have a “No Restraint” policy that staff must strictly adhere to. The health care providers caring for the client usually identify restraint alternatives ([BOX 20.4](#b0025)), and these should become part of the care plan. As the support worker, it is your responsibility, along with that of the rest of the health care team, to follow the care plan. Changes to the care plan may be made as needed. If restraint alternatives do not protect the client, the physician may, as a last resort, order a restraint to be applied.
7. • Informed consent is required. Restraints cannot be used without informed consent. The client must understand the reason for the restraint. The client is told how the restraint will help in the planned treatment and what the risks of restraint use are. The client has the choice to give or withhold consent. If the client is not capable of giving informed consent, a family member or substitute decision maker is given the information, and this person must decide whether or not to give consent. #### When to Use Bed Rails

Your supervisor and the care plan will tell you when to raise the bed rails. For example, clients who are unconscious or sedated, as well as those who are confused or disoriented, need the bed rails raised. Some clients just feel safer with the bed rails up, and some others use them to change positions in bed. If the care plan calls for raised bed rails, keep them up at all times, except when giving bedside care. Beds should also be lowered to their lowest setting, except when bedside care is given.

In the past, health care providers were taught that bed rails on a bed should always be placed up. However, having the bed rails up all the time can cause some clients to become restless. Check the client’s care plan, and report any observations of increased restlessness to your supervisor.

#### Hazards Associated With Bed Rail Use

Bed rails present certain hazards ([FIGURE 20.24](#f0125)). The client can fall while trying to climb over them, or a client can get caught, trapped, or entangled in the following gaps:

1. • Between the bars of the bed rail
2. • Between two half-length rails
3. • Between the bed rail and the headboard or footboard
4. • Between the bed rail and mattress

Injury or death can occur if the client’s head, neck, chest, arm, or leg becomes trapped in these gaps.

## Promoting Your Personal Safety

As a support worker, you are sometimes exposed to safety hazards on the job. You could have an accident if your workplace environment is not safe. You could be injured when working with hazardous materials. You might also be at risk for workplace violence.

### Creating a Safe Workplace

In Canada, each province and territory has occupational health and safety (OH&S) legislation. OH&S legislation is designed to protect employees from injuries and accidents in the workplace. The name of this legislation varies across the country, but in most places
it is called the Occupational Health and Safety Act (as in, for example, Ontario: <https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90o01>). It is revised regularly to reflect changes in policies.

The details of the legislation also vary from one place to another, but the basic elements are similar. In general, the legislation assumes that all people in the workplace—employers, supervisors, and workers—are responsible for their own health and safety and also have certain rights and duties. To reduce the risk of a client displaying escalating responsive behaviours (see [Chapter 38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml)) the support worker should always practice the following steps:

1. • Follow the client’s care plan. For example, if the care plan calls for two-person care, do not provide care by yourself.
2. • Watch for verbal and nonverbal signs agitation or anxiety (see [Chapter 38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml)). If these signs are present, STOP what you are doing, revise your approach, and, if necessary, call for help.
3. • Always be aware of your environment. Ensure that you are always situated close to an exit if the client’s behaviour starts to escalate.
4. • Explain every step you are performing to the client.

#### Employers’ and Supervisors’ Responsibilities

According to OH&S legislation, your employer and supervisor must take every reasonable precaution to protect your health and safety (see the Focus on Home Care: OH&S Legislation box).

Your employer is responsible for the following:

1. • Having written policies that promote safety
2. • Training and educating you about these policies
3. • Creating a health and safety committee to identify hazards in the workplace and investigate accidents
4. • Responding to reports of workplace hazards
5. • Warning you about safety hazards and correcting these hazards, whenever possible
6. • Reporting all accidents promptly to the government department responsible for occupational health and safety
7. • Making sure that all necessary equipment (including personal protective equipment such as gloves and masks) is available and is kept in good working order

### Handling Hazardous Materials

A hazardous material is any substance that presents a physical hazard or a health hazard in the workplace. Physical hazards can cause fires or explosions. work with or around hazardous materials in some settings—for example, the following:

OH&S legislation requires that health care employees understand the risk of hazardous materials and know how to handle them safely. To provide this information, the Canadian government created the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System (WHMIS) and has aligned with the Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling for chemicals (GHS). WHMIS is a comprehensive system for providing health and safety information on the safe use of hazardous products used in Canadian workplaces. It includes hazard identification and product classification, cautionary labelling of containers, the provision of material safety data sheets (MSDSs), and worker education and training programs. The key duties of suppliers, employers, and workers in regard to WHMIS are listed in the next sections. For more details about WHMIS and GHS, visit the Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety website (<https://www.ccohs.ca>).

#### Suppliers’ Duties Under WHMIS[9](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur209)

Suppliers, the organizations that sell or import hazardous products, must ensure the appropriate classification of hazardous products. When a product is considered to be a “hazardous product,” the supplier must label the product or container and must provide an MDS to its customers. The purpose of the label is to clearly identify the hazardous product, the supplier, the hazards, and precautionary measures. The MDS provides more information about that product. Staff are required to know what these are and how to access them in the workplace.

#### Employers’ Duties Under WHMI9[9](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur209)

1. • Educate and train workers on the hazards and safe use of products
2. • Ensure that hazardous products are properly labelled
3. • Prepare workplace labels, as needed
4. • Prepare safety data sheets (SDSs), as necessary (e.g., if an employer manufactures a hazardous product that is used on-site)
5. • Provide access to up-to-date SDSs to workers
6. • Ensure that appropriate control measures are in place to protect the health and safety of workers

#### Workers’ Duties Under WHMIS[9](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur209)

1. • Participate in WHMIS education and training programs
2. • Take necessary steps to protect themselves and their co-workers
3. • Participate in identifying and controlling hazards

#### Consumer Products Use in the Workplace

Consumer products are those products that can be purchased in a store and are generally intended for use in the home. They include cleaning products, adhesives, and lubricants. These products are labelled according to other legislation, not covered by WHMIS.

A comprehensive chemical safety program would include both hazardous products as regulated by WHMIS and any other products that a worker may be exposed to (including consumer products). Workers should receive education and training for safe use of all these products.

#### Components of the WHMIS Labels

WHMIS labels provide the essential information needed to handle a controlled product safely ([FIGURE 20.28](#f0145)), including hazard pictograms representing the kind of danger the product presents ([FIGURE 20.29](#f0150)).

It is important to read and follow all warning labels. A container must have a label, and this label must not be removed or damaged in any way. If a warning label is removed or damaged, do not use the product. Take the container to your supervisor and explain the problem. Do not leave the container unattended.

The supplier label contains the product identifier, hazard pictograms, signal words, hazard statements, and precautionary statements. Workplace labels provide the product identifier, safe handling precautions, and a reference to the SDS.

### Reducing Personal Security Risks

Workplace violence is any physical assault or threatening behaviour that occurs in a work setting (also see Chapter 12). It includes the following:

1. • Psychological trauma—threat, obscene phone call, and harassment of any nature (e.g., being followed, sworn at, or shouted at)
3. • Robbery
4. • Rape
5. • Kidnapping
6. • Beating, stabbing, or shooting
7. • Murder

Workplace violence can occur in any environment where an employee is carrying out a work-related task, including buildings, parking lots, field locations, homes, and means of travel to and from work assignments. The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety (<https://www.ccohs.ca/>) provides information about healthy workplaces, workplace violence, and harassment.

Home care workers in particular have personal security risks. They work alone in an unknown, uncontrolled environment and often work early or late hours. Clients, family members, or any person in the home could act violently toward them.

You can expect your employer to make your workplace as safe as possible. However, you must also protect yourself from harm (see the Think About Safety: Personal Safety Measures box). Follow the personal safety measures all the time. Most of the rules presented here apply to support workers working in facilities and in the community.

Personal Safety Measures

When Considering Your Own Safety, Be Sure to Also Take Steps Toward Your Electronic Safety:

1. • Do not provide personal information about yourself that can give strangers access to you on any website, chat room, or social networking site.
2. • Take electronic threats directed toward you as seriously as you would any threat received in person. Report it to your local police department. If the threat has come from a co-worker or client, notify your supervisor.
3. • Do not respond to any electronic threat, as responding may only make the situation worse. If possible, print off a hard copy of the threat or save it electronically to provide proof of receiving the threat.

When Driving to and From Work, Take the Following Precautions:

1. • Familiarize yourself with the area where you will be driving. Plan your route in advance, keeping to well-travelled streets and routes that you know. Note the locations of nearby police stations, gas stations, and other public buildings that may be of help in an emergency.
2. • Let your supervisor and a member of your family know the route you will take.
3. • Keep with you the phone number of a reliable tow truck company.
4. • Keep maps or a global positioning system (GPS) in the car.
5. • Maintain your car in good working order at all times.
6. • Always use a seat belt.
7. • Be prepared for winter driving conditions. Keep a survival kit in the car—booster cables, shovel, extra clothing, a blanket, sand, and road flares.
8. • Keep cash with you for gas or to make a phone call.
9. • Have a cell phone for emergencies on the road, and program emergency numbers into the phone. Do not use the phone while driving. Pull off the road if you need to make a call.
10. • Pull over to a safe spot away from traffic if you have car trouble. Put on your hazard lights, and call for help if you have a cell phone. Keep your doors locked and windows rolled up until help arrives. Never leave the car and walk off alone for help.
11. • Check for places to park. Choose a well-lit, busy area. If using a parking garage, park near entrances or exits and on a lower level. Try to stay close to the parking attendant if possible. Be aware of passengers sitting in parked cars, and avoid parking by these cars if possible. Lock your car doors as you leave.
12. • Ask for an escort to walk you back to your car after work. Some facilities provide this service. In a home setting, ask a family member to escort you.
13. • Have your car key ready so you can get into the car quickly. Do not fumble for keys on the way to or at the car. If necessary, you can use your house or car keys as a weapon. Carry them in your strong hand. Have one key extended. If you are attacked, slash at the attacker’s face with the key.
14. • Check for a possible intruder in the front and back seats before getting into your car.
15. • If you think someone is following you, cross the street or change directions. Head toward a public building or a lighted house. If you are scared, yell for help.
16. • Lock the car doors when you get in the car. Keep the windows rolled up.
17. • Keep purses, backpacks, and other valuables under the seat or near your side. Do not leave them on the seat. They are easy targets for robberies.
18. • Do not carry valuables. Do not bring your purse or valuables into clients’ homes. Leave them at home or in the car trunk. If an attacker demands something you have, give it to them. The only thing of value is you. Report the crime to the police.
19. • Carry a whistle or a “shriek alarm.” Carry a travel-size can of aerosol hairspray. Aim for the attacker’s
    face. If someone tries to force you into a vehicle, do whatever you can to resist being pulled in. Fight back even if you see a weapon (yell, kick, push your thumbs into the attacker’s eyes). You are at much greater risk if you are forced into the vehicle.

When Visiting a Client, Take the Following Precautions:

1. • Call your supervisor when you arrive at and leave a client’s home.
2. • Be cautious in hallways, elevators, and stairwells in apartment buildings. Do not get on an elevator with a stranger who makes you nervous.
3. • Stay in the centre of the hallways and avoid hidden corners in apartment buildings. If someone threatens you, knock on as many doors as possible and yell to get attention. If you are attacked, pull the fire alarm.
4. • Pause for a few seconds when entering someone’s home. Assess the environment. Check any threats to your safety, including used syringes, strange odours, clutter, unfamiliar people, or household items that could be used as weapons. Note the location of the phone. Note any obstacles blocking exits.
5. • Keep your shoes on. In an emergency, you may need to leave in a hurry. In the winter, bring a pair of shoes for indoor use.
6. • Leave if a client or anyone else is abusive or makes sexual comments or suggestions.
7. • If you feel uncomfortable or threatened in any way, go to a safe place and immediately call your supervisor.
8. • Let the client lead the way down corridors, up staircases, and so on.
9. • Leave an exit route, or place yourself between the exit and the client. Never allow yourself to be cornered. Decide whether you should leave a door open or unlocked. Tell the client not to shut or lock a door if the action makes you uncomfortable.
10. • Sit in a hard-backed chair. You can get up faster from a firm chair than from a soft chair or sofa.
11. • Sit with your strong leg back and your other leg forward. This position lets you get out of your seat quickly without using your hands.
12. • Ask that pets be restrained or kept out of the room if you are nervous about them.
13. • If a client or family member becomes angry and begins to scream, stay calm. (Refer to the Think About Safety: The ABCDs of Managing Responsive or Challenging Behaviours box in [Chapter 38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml)).
14. • Report any unusual incidents to your supervisor as soon as possible.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Because of age or physical or emotional health condition, clients may be at great risk for accidents and injuries. It is up to facilities and community care agencies to try to create and maintain a safe environment for their employees and their clients.

• Older persons, children, and people living with illnesses or disabilities are especially vulnerable to experiencing accidents. Some of their accident risk factors include (1) impaired awareness of surroundings, (2) impaired vision, (3) impaired hearing, (4) impaired taste, smell, or touch, (5) impaired mobility, (6) medication effects, and (7) age.

• All health care workers should be aware of (1) common poisonous substances and what to do if a poisoning is suspected, (2) ways to prevent burns in older persons and others at risk, (3) ways to prevent suffocation in adults, (4) ways to prevent equipment accidents, and (5) safety measures for preventing fires, including how to use oxygen safely and how to assist in the evacuation of a building in case of a fire.

• Health care workers must implement safety measures aimed at preventing falls among older persons and other clients at risk.

• Although you should always supervise all of your clients, you should pay particular attention to children, clients in wheelchairs, clients with a history of injury, sedated clients, older persons, and clients who are cognitively impaired, as they are at greatest risk for injuries related to falls.

• Restraints can be classified as (1) physical restraints, (2) environmental restraints, and (3) chemical restraints.

• A restraint is any device, garment, barrier, furniture, or medication that limits or restricts freedom of movement or access to one’s own body. Restraints can cause emotional harm and serious injury.

• The use of restraints requires a physician’s order after consultation with the client’s family.

• Support workers should be aware of the many alternatives to using restraints.

• Occupational health and safety laws require health care employees to understand what hazardous materials are and how to safely handle the ones they may come in contact with. This educational system is called WHMIS, or the Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System.

• Health care workers should be aware of the measures to prevent personal injury both at work and outside of work and be prepared to use them if necessary.

## The Client’s Bed

For those who are confined to bed, comfort is especially important. Many home care clients have regular beds. Use pillows to help clients sit comfortably in a regular bed.

Hospital beds are used in hospitals and long-term care facilities, as well as by home care clients in some homes. Hospital beds usually have electric controls that allow clients to sit up and lie down without effort. Many hospital beds have at least partial bed rails (see [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml) for a discussion of bed rails and their use). Remember, bed rails have many safety hazards and can be a form of restraint. Raise and lower them only as directed by your supervisor and the care plan.

### Regular Beds

In clients’ homes, you will see twin-, double-, queen-, and king-sized beds, as well as waterbeds, sofa sleepers, cots, and recliners. Also, a number of private homes have motorized and electrically adjusted beds. Clients receiving palliative care in the community often have hospital-type beds.

Regular beds cannot be raised. The lower the bed, the more you will have to bend and reach when giving care or making the bed, so use good body mechanics to protect your back (see [Chapter 14](../B9780323709392000146/B9780323709392000146.xhtml)).

A good mattress is neither too hard nor too soft. A poor-quality mattress may not provide the needed support or comfort and could harm the client’s back or skin. If you have concerns about a client’s mattress, tell your supervisor. Many clients who are thin or bedridden, or who require frequent repositioning, are prone to skin breakdown and pressure ulcers. An alternating pressure mattress is a special mattress that has alternating areas of pressure of air, gel, or water baffles to reduce the risk for pressure sores (see Think About Safety: Alternating Pressure Mattresses and Side Rails).

Alternating Pressure Mattresses and Side Rails

The air, gel, or water baffles in an alternating pressure mattress reduce the risk for pressure sores. It is important when making a bed with an alternating pressure mattress to ensure that layers of bed linens do not reduce the effectiveness of the baffle action. Most manufacturers recommend that incontinence pads of any type not be used, and some recommend that even a bottom sheet not be used. If they can be used,
bottom sheets should not be tucked under the mattress as they would be with a regular mattress
. Instead, they should be allowed to hang down at the sides. If the client needs to be repositioned, a slider sheet (or slider sheet system) designed for this use should be used (see [Chapter 14](../B9780323709392000146/B9780323709392000146.xhtml)). Bottom sheets that cannot be tucked in can be a source of wrinkles (and pressure areas) under the client’s fragile skin. It is therefore important to pull and straighten the sheet under the client whenever the client is repositioned.

Any client using an alternating pressure mattress should be checked frequently. There have been reported cases of clients who have died as a result of being wedged between the air mattress and the bed frame.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur211) Bed rails also can cause injury to the client who is on an alternating pressure mattress. Always follow the client’s care plan regarding their use of bed rails and regularly monitor any client who has side rails. Refer to [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml) for a discussion on side rail safety.

### Hospital Beds

Hospital beds have electrical or manual controls and can be raised horizontally to give care, reducing worker bending and reaching. The lowest horizontal position lets the client get out of bed with ease ([FIGURE 21.6](#f0035)) and is also necessary as a safety measure for the client. The head of the bed can be kept flat or raised to varying degrees for the client’s comfort.

![Four controls marked on the bed rail (A) while a hand-held bed control at the bottom shows labels for hand of bed controls, raise and lower bed controls, and foot or bed controls (B).](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000213/main.assets/f21-07-9780323711654.jpg)

Most hospital beds are electrically controlled. Controls are on a side panel, on the bed rail, or on a panel at the foot of the bed ([FIGURE 21.7](#f0040)), and clients are taught how to safely use the controls. Clients are advised of the risks associated with having the bed in the high position. They are also advised of any position limitations or restrictions.

Most electrically controlled beds can be “locked” into any position to prevent the client from raising or lowering the head or foot of the bed. Clients restricted to certain positions—for example, clients with dementia—may need to have their beds locked. You must also be alert to potential dangers when children, confused clients, or clients with impaired cognition or altered levels of consciousness have easy access to the controls. Follow the care plan, and check with your supervisor.

![Diagram of a manually operated bed shows labels for a handle marked raises and lowers bed horizontally, a handle on inner leg of the bed marked raises and lowers head of bed, and a handle on frame of bed facing front, marked raises and lowers knee position.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000213/main.assets/f21-08-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 21.8 Manually operated hospital bed.

Manually operated beds are also still in use in some facilities and homes. These beds have cranks at the foot of the bed ([FIGURE 21.8](#f0045))—the left crank raises or lowers the head of the bed; the right crank adjusts the knee portion; and the centre crank raises or lowers the entire bed. The cranks are pulled out and up for use, and after use they should be pushed in and turned down. Cranks in the “up” position are safety hazards, as anyone walking past could bump into them.

Hospital beds should always be left in their lowest position for the safety of the client. Raise the bed only immediately before giving care and when making the bed. As soon as you have finished, return the bed to its lowest position. Never leave the client alone when the bed is raised.

Hospital bed legs have wheels that let the bed move easily. Some beds have wheels that can be locked to prevent the bed from moving ([FIGURE 21.9](#f0050)). Other beds have different locking mechanisms. Make sure bed wheels are locked at all times unless you are moving the bed to another location. Ensure that bed rails are up if moving a client in the bed within your facility. You or the client could be injured if the bed moves during any care or transfer.

## Linen

How often linen is changed varies among work settings ([TABLE 21.1](#t0010), page 465). Follow the care plan and your employer’s policy. For example, in most long-term care facilities, linen changes are routinely done on days the client is showered or bathed.
In all settings, linen is changed when it is wet, damp, or soiled.

When handling linen and making beds, follow the rules of Standard Practices. Perform hand hygiene before collecting clean linen. Your uniform is considered dirty, so you must always hold linen away from your body and uniform ([FIGURE 21.17](#f0090)). Never shake linen in the air, as doing so could spread microbes. Clean linen should be placed on a clean surface.
Never put clean or soiled linen on the floor
. If clean linen accidentally touches the floor, put it in the laundry. If you are changing the linens in a semi-private room or a room with four beds, remember that linens for one client should be placed only on that client’s furniture, not on another client’s furniture.

In order to reduce unnecessary time and steps, as well as to prevent cross-contamination of germs from one client’s room to another (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)), you need to plan your steps. Take only the linens you will need into the room. If you find you have taken extra
linens into the room, do not place them back in the linen room or on the linen cart—leave them in the client’s room. The linens can then be used the next time linens are changed.

A turning pad (or turning sheet) is a small pad placed over the middle of the bottom sheet. It is used to turn and move the client in bed (see [FIGURE 21.25](#f0130) on page 471). If the client’s care plan calls for an incontinence pad to protect the bed, some agencies and facilities use washable incontinence pads as turning pads (see Focus on Home Care: Incontinence Pads); however, you must follow your agency’s policy for this. Turning pads prevent pain, skin damage, and bone and joint damage by protecting the client’s skin from friction and shearing when the client is moved in bed. A turning pad is also known as a sliding pad or transfer sheet in some facilities. In some settings where turning pads are unavailable—and only if necessary for brief use—flat sheets are folded into the size of a turning pad and used as a turning pad. However, the folded flat sheet should never be left under the client, as the increased bulk of the folded sheet can cause pressure areas under the client. The flat top sheet should be mitred in the corners. A mitred corner is a type of fold done to the top layers of bedding which keeps the layers neat and in place (see [FIGURE 21.23](#f0120) on page 469).

In most facilities throughout the country, no-lift policies exist for the safety of the client and the
health care worker. For this reason, turning pads are never referred to as lift pads in these facilities. If your employer has a no-lift policy, you should never attempt moving or lifting the client without checking first with your supervisor.

Most facilities use either reusable or disposable incontinence pads. Follow your facility’s policy. Check your provincial or territorial legislation and your facility’s policies regarding the use of incontinence pads.

Incontinence Pads

Medical supply stores sell disposable or washable waterproof pads which are used as incontinence pads. Disposable waterproof pads should never be used as a turning pad as they will tear if used for this purpose. If permitted at your agency, washable incontinence pads can be used for turning most clients because they are usually strong and large enough.

The case manager will discuss the need for these items with the client and family. Some clients place plastic mattress protectors on their beds, but these protectors do not protect the bottom linens (bottom sheet and mattress pad). Never use plastic garbage bags or dry-cleaning bags, as these are not strong enough to protect the linens and mattress. As well, they slide easily and can move out of place, and the danger of suffocation is great if the client’s movements result in the bag covering the client’s nose and mouth. Again, your supervisor and the care plan will tell you what is safe to use for the client.

## Bed Making

Your job as a support worker will include making beds. No matter what type of bed you make, safety, proper body mechanics, and medical asepsis are important. Follow Standard Practices. [BOX 21.1](#b0010) lists the guidelines for bed making (also see the Focus on Children: Crib Safety box).

Focus On Children
![icon](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000213/main.assets/icon01-9780323711654.jpg)

Crib Safety

Cribs, crib linen, mattresses, other linen, and bumper pads all pose many dangers to infants, as they can lead to strangulation and suffocation. Report any safety hazard to your supervisor. Always follow these safety rules:

1. • The crib mattress must be firm. A soft mattress can cover the baby’s nose and mouth, causing suffocation.
2. • The mattress must fit snugly in the crib frame. The baby’s head could get caught between the frame and the loose mattress and cause strangulation.
3. • The space between the mattress and crib sides should be no more than 3 cm. Only two adult fingers should fit in the space. If more than two fingers fit, the mattress is too small.
4. • The mattress must be at least 65 cm (34 in.) lower than the top of the crib rails to protect the baby from falling out of the crib. The mattress should be lowered even more when the baby can stand in the crib.
5. • Plastic garbage bags and dry-cleaning bags must not be used to protect the mattress.
6. • Bumper pads are no longer recommended for use in cribs, as they pose a risk for strangulation of the baby.
7. • Do not tuck blankets and top sheets under the mattress, as the baby can get caught in them.
8. • Do not place pillows, fluffy comforters, stuffed toys, or heavy blankets in the crib when the baby is in it, as these items can cause suffocation.
9. • Check linens for loose threads or stitching, and trim any found, as loose stitching can cause strangulation.
10. • The space between the side bars should be no more than 6 cm.
11. • Cribs manufactured before 1986 do not meet safety regulations and must not be used.

### The Closed Bed

A closed bed is made when the bed is expected to be unoccupied for several hours or days. The top linen is pulled up, and the bedspread is neatly pulled over the pillow. In some facilities, the pillow is placed on top of the bedspread ([FIGURE 21.20](#f0105)). A closed bed is usually made when the client is up for most or all of the day. Clean linen is used as needed.

In facilities, after a client is discharged, the bed frame and mattress are cleaned and disinfected. A closed bed is then made and kept ready for a new client.

BOX 21.1 Guidelines for Bed Making

1. • Use good body mechanics at all times.
2. • Follow the rules of medical asepsis.
3. • Follow Standard Practices.
4. • Make the bed according to the client’s wishes and the care plan. If the client’s wishes are unsafe, tell your supervisor.
5. • Have a bag bin ready for disposal of soiled linen. Follow your employer’s policies.
6. • Perform hand hygiene before handling clean linen and after handling dirty linen.
7. • Bring enough linen to the client’s room. Try not to bring more linen than what is needed.
8. • Do not use torn linen.
9. • Never shake linen in the air. Shaking linen spreads microbes, lint, and dust.
10. • Hold linen away from your uniform. Dirty linen as well as clean linen must not touch your uniform.
11. • Never put used, soiled linen or clean linen on the floor. Handle and dispose of linen according to employer policy (see [Chapters 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml) and [20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)).
12. • If the client is on cytotoxic medications (chemotherapy), see [Chapter 44](../B9780323709392000444/B9780323709392000444.xhtml) for safety information regarding special handling of soiled linens.
13. • Linen must be straightened whenever loose or wrinkled and at bedtime.
14. • Make one side of the bed, as much as possible, before going to the other side. This practice saves time and energy.
15. • Change wet, damp, or soiled linen right away to help prevent breakdown of your client’s skin.
16. • Manually operated beds have handles used to position the beds. The handles are pulled out during use. Ensure that after use, the handles are pushed in and turned down, as anyone walking past could bump into them.
17. • Hospital beds have wheels that let the bed move easily. Make sure the bed wheels are locked when giving bed care or transferring your client to or from the bed.
18. • Clients need to be advised of the risks associated with having the bed in high positions. They are also advised of any position limitations or restrictions.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• The basic bed positions are the flat position, Fowler’s positions, the Trendelenburg position, and the reverse Trendelenburg position. The care plan will tell you which position to place the client in. The client’s position depends on their condition.

• The linens required for bed making and the frequency of linen changes will vary among health care settings. Follow your employer’s policies.

• Clean linen must be held away from your uniform to prevent transfer of microorganisms, and dirty linen must be removed with gloves and placed into the receptacle approved by your employer.

• A closed bed is made when the bed is expected to be unoccupied for a while; the top linens are pulled up, with the top sheet folded over the bedspread into a cuff or the bedspread pulled neatly over the pillows, as per facility policy. An open bed is made shortly before the bed is to be occupied; top linens are folded back so that the client can easily get into bed. An occupied bed is made with the client in it.

• One of the most important concepts in this chapter to remember is that the bottom sheet, next to your client’s skin, needs to be wrinkle free to prevent skin irritation, which can lead to skin breakdown of buttocks, ankles, and elbows and to bedsores.

• Use good body mechanics to reduce your chance of injury when making a bed.

Skin is the body’s first line of defence. It protects the body from microbes that cause infection and disease. As a support worker, you need to consider providing good skin care to your clients one of your most important tasks.

Infants, older persons, and clients who have disabilities are at greatest risk for skin breakdown because their skin can be easily injured due to limited mobility and certain health conditions that make their skin more susceptible to injury. It should be noted that the skin of infants and the skin of older people are quite different from each another. As a support worker, you should also be aware that clients who are diabetic or have circulation challenges (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)) tend to have delayed healing times and can be at greater risk for wound and skin infections. For every client, you need to look for signs of skin problems and report them immediately to either the nurse or your supervisor, depending on the workplace. [BOX 22.1](#b0010) lists the common causes of skin breakdown.

A wound is a break in the skin or the mucous membrane, which becomes a portal of entry for microbes. Wounds have many causes. A surgical incision leaves a wound, for example, and wounds often result from trauma—an accident (such as a fall or vehicular accident) or violent act (such as a gunshot or stabbing) that injures skin, mucous membranes, bones, or internal organs. Other wounds may result from immobility or poor blood circulation.
As a support worker, it is important to prevent skin injury by providing frequent and thorough skin care.
 When injury does occur, infection is a major threat, so wound care is very important for preventing infection and further injury to the wound and nearby tissues. A good source that provides information on wound care is Wounds Canada (<https://www.woundscanada.ca/>). In addition to the types of wound care listed in this chapter, physicians may also order heat and cold applications for wound care (see [Chapter 40](../B9780323709392000407/B9780323709392000407.xhtml)) to promote healing and comfort and to reduce tissue swelling.

The support worker’s role in wound care depends on provincial or territorial laws, job description, and the client’s condition. Whatever your role, you need to know the various types of wounds, how wounds
heal, and how to promote wound healing. It is also important to follow Standard Practices and to wear gloves while dealing with any open wound. Wound care protocols may differ between agencies, municipalities, or provinces/territories, so for the sake of consistency it is important that you adhere to the protocols followed by your agency.

## Types of Wounds

Wounds are described in many ways ([BOX 22.2](#b0015)), including the following:

2. • Contusion—a closed wound caused by a blow to the body (a bruise) ([FIGURE 22.1, B](#f0015)).
3. • Incision—an open wound with clean, straight edges; it is usually intentionally created with a sharp instrument ([FIGURE 22.1, C](#f0020)).
4. • Laceration—an open wound with torn tissues and jagged edges ([FIGURE 22.1, D](#f0025)).
5. • Penetrating wound—an open wound which can vary from a superficial puncture in the uppermost layer of skin to a deep penetration of major body systems; it can be caused an object such as a knife or bullet entering the body ([FIGURE 22.1, E](#f0030)).[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur221),[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur222)

Intentional Wound and Unintentional Wound

1. • Intentional wound—created for treatment; for example, surgical incisions, as well as sterile venipunctures (vein punctures) for starting intravenous (IV) therapy or for collecting blood specimens
2. • Unintentional wound—results from trauma; for example, falls, vehicle accidents, gunshots, stabbings

1. • Open wound—a break in the skin or mucous membrane; intentional and most unintentional wounds are open
2. • Closed wound—injury to tissues without breaking the skin; for example, bruises, twists, and sprains

Clean Wound and Contaminated Wound

1. • Clean wound—not infected, meaning microbes have not entered the wound; closed wounds and intentional wounds created under surgically aseptic conditions are usually clean; because the urinary, respiratory, and digestive systems are not entered, there is a reduced risk of infection
2. • Clean-contaminated wound—result of the surgical entry (sterile technique used) of the urinary, reproductive, or digestive system; because these systems are not sterile and contain normal flora, the risk for infection is greater than with a clean wound
3. • Contaminated wound—unintentional wound (such as from a stabbing) that is not created under sterile conditions, has been exposed to pathogens, and has a high risk for infection; wound contamination can also occur from a break in surgical asepsis and from the spillage of intestinal contents; tissues may show signs of inflammation
4. • Infected wound (dirty wound)—wound containing large numbers of bacteria and showing signs of infection; for example, old wounds, surgical incisions into infected areas (such as a ruptured appendix), and traumatic injuries that rupture the bowel
5. • Chronic wound—one that does not heal easily in a timely manner, such as a pressure injury or circulatory ulcer (see page 486); any wound that is continually exposed to friction, pressure, or moisture can become chronic

Partial- and Full-Thickness Wounds (Described by Wound Depth)

1. • Partial-thickness wound—the dermis and epidermis of the skin are broken
2. • Full-thickness wound—the dermis, epidermis, and subcutaneous tissue are penetrated; muscle and bone may be involved

## Skin Tears

A skin tear is a break or rip in the skin. The epidermis (top skin layer) separates from the underlying tissue. The hands, arms, and lower legs are common sites for skin tears. Many older people have very thin and fragile skin, and even slight pressure or friction can cause a skin tear. Friction is the resistance that skin encounters when it rubs against another surface such as clothing, bedding, or another fold of skin. Clients who are most likely to have skin tears are those with very dry and paper-thin skin.

### Causes of Skin Tears

Skin tears occur from friction, shearing, pulling, or direct pressure on skin. Shearing is the tearing of skin tissues as a result of skin sticking to a surface (usually the bed or chair) and deeper tissues moving downward, exerting pressure on the skin (see [Chapter 14](../B9780323709392000146/B9780323709392000146.xhtml)). Blood vessels and tissues become damaged, reducing blood flow to the area and increasing the risk for a pressure injury. Shearing occurs when the client slides down in the bed or chair. Skin tears are commonly caused by the following:

1. • Bumping a hand, arm, or leg on any hard surface such as a bed, bed rail, chair, wheelchair footrest, or table
2. • Holding on to a client’s arm or leg too tightly
3. • Repositioning, moving, or transferring a client without a transfer sheet or other nonfriction surface, the absence of which can cause the client’s skin to rub against the surface and even tear
4. • Bathing, dressing, and other tasks
5. • Pulling buttons or zippers across fragile skin

It is always your responsibility to be careful when moving, repositioning, or transferring clients. Skin tears are painful, and they can become portals of entry for pathogens. Tell your supervisor at once if you cause or find a skin tear, bruise, bump, or scrape (see the Supporting Mrs. Nippeskaya: Preventing Further Skin Injuries in a Frail Client box).

## Pressure Injuries

A pressure injury (also called a decubitus ulcer, bedsore, pressure ulcer, or pressure sore) is any injury caused by unrelieved pressure. It usually occurs over a bony prominence—an area where the bone seems to “stick out” and is compressed under the client or between the client’s skin folds. The shoulder blades, elbows, hip bones, sacrum (the bone in the lower part of the spine), knees, ankle bones, heels, and toes are all bony prominences.

These bony prominences are called pressure points because they bear the weight of the body in certain positions ([FIGURE 22.2](#f0040)). Pressure from body weight can reduce blood supply to the area, which results in a pressure injury. Pressure points that are moist with perspiration or body excretions are especially prone to developing a pressure injury, as well as bacterial infections that are very difficult to treat once they set in.

### Causes of Pressure Injuries

Pressure, friction, and shearing are common causes of skin breakdown and pressure injuries. Other factors include breaks in skin; poor circulation to an area; moisture (such as perspiration or urine); dry, flaky skin; and irritation from urine and stool. Pressure occurs when the skin over a bony prominence is squeezed between hard surfaces (the bone itself and another surface, such as a chair seat or mattress). In obese people, pressure injuries can develop in areas where friction is caused by skin-to-skin contact. Pressure injuries can also occur in thin people when two bony areas are in direct contact with each other (such as knees or ankles
rubbing together). This squeezing or pressure interferes with the blood flow to the skin and underlying tissues, preventing oxygen and nutrients from reaching cells (see the Supporting Mr. Hansen: How Pressure Can Result in Skin Blistering box) and causing the death of the skin and tissues in the area (FIGURES [22.3](#f0045) and [22.4](#f0050)). In many settings in both the United States and Canada, health care providers use the Braden Scale, which is used to predict the likelihood of a client developing a pressure injury ([FIGURE 22.5](#f0055)). It takes into account the client’s sensory function, skin moisture, activity level, nutrition, and likelihood of friction and shear when the client is moved.

#### Other High-Risk Areas

Skin fold areas—under breasts, between abdominal folds, on legs and buttocks, and between toes—are also at risk for infection (which can lead to the formation of pressure sores). These areas, often missed during a bed bath, are frequently warm and moist because of perspiration and infrequent exposure to air. ### Prevention and Treatment of Pressure Injuries

Pressure injuries develop over time. The longer pressure is exerted on skin, the greater the risk is that a pressure injury will develop. Preventing pressure injuries is much easier than healing them. Good support care (which includes frequent position changes and ensuring a proper diet with lots of fluid), cleanliness, and skin care are essential. The Think About Safety: Guidelines for Preventing Pressure Injuries box lists guidelines for preventing skin breakdown and pressure injuries. As always, it is important that you also follow your client’s care plan and adhere to the preventive measures described on it.

Clients at risk for pressure injuries are placed on a surface that reduces or relieves pressure—for example, foam, air, alternating air, gel, or water mattresses. The health care team decides on the best surface for the client.

A physician orders specific pressure injury treatment—wound care products, medications, treatments, and special equipment—for each client. Your supervisor and the care plan will tell you what to do. Special beds and other protective devices, described below, are often ordered to prevent and treat pressure injuries and other types of skin breakdown.

## Leg And Foot Ulcers

Some diseases may affect blood flow to and from the legs and feet, which can cause pain, open wounds, and edema (swelling caused by fluid collecting in tissues). Infection and gangrene can result from the
open wound and poor circulation. Gangrene is a condition in which tissue dies and then decays. Gangrenous tissue may be black and have a foul smell (see [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml)).

Clients with leg and foot ulcers need special skin care, as directed by a physician. The nurse uses the care-planning process to prevent skin breakdown on legs and feet.

### Circulatory Ulcers

Circulatory ulcers (vascular ulcers) are open wounds on the lower legs and feet caused by decreased blood flow through arteries or veins. People with diseases affecting blood vessels are at risk for these ulcers on legs and feet, which can be painful and hard to heal. Depending on where they originate, they are classified as venous ulcers or arterial ulcers.

#### Venous Ulcers

Venous ulcers (stasis ulcers) are open wounds on the lower legs and feet caused by poor blood return through veins ([FIGURE 22.13](#f0095),
A
). Blood travelling from the feet to the heart must defy gravity. In people who are active, the use of leg muscles assists the valves in their leg veins to pump the blood upward.

Unfortunately, in some clients who are not active, who are extremely obese, or who have circulatory problems, blood will pool in the leg veins, or the valves will not close well. ##### Appearance

Edema in tissues will give them a swollen appearance. If the edema is fairly recent, skin can also appear shiny and stretched. Edema and venous ulcers are painful and make walking difficult. Venous ulcers may “weep” fluid. Healing is slow, and infection is a great risk. If the edema lasts for a long period, skin will change in appearance and texture. Small veins in skin can rupture, allowing the hemoglobin in blood (which gives blood its red colour) to enter tissues, causing skin to darken and become dry, leathery, and hard. Itching is common.

##### Causes

Heels and the inner sides of ankles are common sites for venous ulcers because these areas can be easily injured. Scratching is also a common cause of venous ulcers. Some injuries occur spontaneously, without any specific cause.

##### Prevention

It is important to prevent skin breakdown caused by poor circulation because skin breakdown leads to venous ulcers, which are hard to heal. The Think About Safety: Guidelines for Preventing Venous Ulcers box lists guidelines that may be part of the client’s care plan.

The physician may order elastic stockings or elastic bandages (see [Chapter 18](../B9780323709392000183/B9780323709392000183.xhtml)), which promote comfort and circulation by providing support and pressure to the veins. Foot care by a professional with specialized
training in foot care, as well as medications or wound care products, may be ordered by the client’s physician or nurse practitioner.

## Wound Healing

The healing process has three phases:

1. •
   Inflammatory phase
    (3 days). Bleeding stops, and a scab forms over the wound, which prevents microbes from entering the wound. Blood supply to the wound increases, and the blood brings nutrients and healing substances. Because blood supply increases, signs and symptoms of inflammation—redness, swelling, heat or warmth, and pain—may appear, and there may be some loss of function.
2. •
   Proliferative phase
    (day 3 to day 21). Proliferate means multiply rapidly. Tissue cells multiply to repair the wound.
3. •
   Maturation phase
    (day 21 to 1 or 2 years). The scar tissue gains strength and appears red and raised but eventually becomes thin and paler than surrounding skin.

### Types of Wound Healing

The healing process occurs through primary intention, secondary intention, or tertiary intention. With primary intention (also called first intention or primary closure), the wound is closed with sutures (stitches), staples, clips, or adhesive strips to hold the wound edges together.

Secondary intention (also called second intention) is used in contaminated and infected wounds. Wounds are cleaned and dead tissue removed. Wound edges are not brought together—the wound is left gaping. Healing occurs naturally but takes longer, and a larger scar is formed. These wounds have a high risk of infection.

Tertiary intention (also called third intention or delayed intention) involves leaving a wound open for a time before closing it. Thus, tertiary intention combines secondary and primary intentions. Infection and poor circulation are common reasons for choosing tertiary intention.

### Complications of Wounds

Many factors affect healing and could increase the risk for complications—for example, the client’s age, general health, nutritional status and lifestyle, and the type and location of the wound. Clients with diabetes are at increased risk for infection. The client might be very old, might have a medical condition (such as circulatory disease or diabetes), or may have a lifestyle risk factor (such as smoking or poor diet) that might slow down the healing process. Other clients may be on certain medications (such as warfarin or heparin) that will prolong bleeding and possibly delay healing.

Wound healing is dependent on good blood circulation to the wound as well as good nutrition for the client, with a diet rich in fluids (needed for tissue hydration), protein, and vitamin C (needed for tissue growth and repair). The wound must be protected from infection. Sometimes, the client’s doctor may prescribe antibiotics to prevent the development of an infection.

#### Hemorrhage

Hemorrhage is excessive loss of blood within a short period. If bleeding is not stopped, death results. Hemorrhage may be internal or external. Internal hemorrhage, when bleeding occurs into tissues and body cavities, cannot be seen. It leads to a hematoma, which is a collection of blood under skin and tissues. The area appears swollen and has a reddish-blue or grey colour. Shock, vomiting blood, coughing up blood, and loss of consciousness are signs of internal hemorrhage. External bleeding is visible as bloody drainage or through dressings soaked with blood. As with internal hemorrhage, shock can occur.

Shock is a result of there not being enough blood supply to organs and tissues. Signs and symptoms of shock include low or falling blood pressure; rapid and weak pulse; rapid respirations; and cold, moist, and pale or greyish skin. A client experiencing shock is restless and may complain of thirst. Confusion and loss of consciousness eventually occur.

Hemorrhage and shock are emergencies, so notify your supervisor immediately, and assist as requested. Remember to follow Standard Practices when in contact with blood. Gloves should always be worn, and gowns, masks, and eye protection are necessary when blood splashes and splatters are likely.

#### Infection

Wound contamination can occur at any time during or after an injury or surgery. Trauma often results in contaminated wounds, and surgical wounds can
be contaminated during or after surgery. An infected wound has drainage and is painful and tender to the touch ([BOX 22.5](#b0030)). A client with an infected wound may have a fever.

#### Dehiscence

Dehiscence is the rupture or sudden separation of the wound layers along a surgical suture line ([FIGURE 22.14](#f0100)). Separation may occur just at the skin layer or affect underlying tissues, as usually seen in abdominal wounds. Coughing, vomiting, and abdominal distension place stress on the wound. The client often describes a sensation of the wound popping open.

#### Evisceration

Evisceration is the separation of the wound, which is accompanied by protrusion of abdominal organs ([FIGURE 22.15](#f0105)). Causes are the same as those of dehiscence.

Dehiscence and evisceration are surgical emergencies. Tell your supervisor at once if you see them. A nurse will cover the wound with a large sterile dressing saturated with sterile saline, and the client will need emergency medical care.

During the healing process, physicians and nurses routinely observe the wound and its drainage for healing and complications. ### Wound Drainage

During injury and during the inflammatory phase of wound healing, fluid and cells escape from tissues. The amount of drainage may be small or large, depending on wound size and location and the presence of bleeding or infection. Although the nurse is responsible for changing dressings, you need to observe and report to the nurse any type of wound drainage. You may also be shown how to measure the amount of drainage and to document the amount of drainage, depending on your employing agency and your scope of practice in your province.

Major types of wound drainage are as follows:

1. • Serous drainage—clear, watery fluid ([FIGURE 22.16, A](#f0110)). The fluid in a blister is serous (from the Latin word serum, meaning “clear, thin, fluid portion of blood”). Serum does not contain blood cells or platelets.
2. • Sanguineous drainage—bloody drainage (see [FIGURE 22.16, B](#f0110)). The amount and colour of sanguineous (from the Latin word sanguis, which means “blood”) drainage are important. Hemorrhage is suspected when large amounts are present. Bright red drainage means fresh bleeding, whereas older bleeding is darker.
3. • Serosanguineous drainage—thin, watery drainage (sero) that is blood-tinged (sanguineous) (see [FIGURE 22.16, C](#f0110)).

## Dressings

Wound dressings have many functions:

1. • They protect wounds from injury and microbes.
2. • They absorb drainage.
3. • They remove dead tissue.
4. • They promote comfort.
5. • They cover unsightly (ugly) wounds.
6. • They provide a moist environment for wound healing.
7. • When bleeding is a problem, pressure dressings help control bleeding.

### Sterile Dressings

The type and size of dressing used depend on many factors—(1) the type of wound, (2) its size and location, (3) the amount of drainage, (4) the presence or absence of infection, (5) the dressing’s function, and (6) the frequency of dressing changes. The physician and nurse choose the best type of dressing for each wound. In many agencies, wound care is assessed by a nurse who specializes in skin care of wounds and around ostomies (see [Chapters 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml) and [30](../B9780323709392000304/B9780323709392000304.xhtml)). You may be asked to assist in the application of a sterile dressing (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)).
Because there is such a wide range of sterile dressing types and indications for the use of each, and because it is beyond your scope of practice to decide on the type of dressing, this chapter focuses on the support worker’s responsibilities in regard to a client’s dressing.

Although you are not responsible for sterile dressing changes or for skin assessment (these tasks are the responsibility of the nurse), you should function as the “eyes and ears” of the client, reporting important details about the dressings to the nurse ([BOX 22.6](#b0035)).

### Types of Dressings

Dressings are described by the material used and application method. Many products are available for dressing wounds. Dressings may be dry, moist, or hydrocolloid (a substance that forms a gel in the presence of water). The following are common dressings:

1. •
   Gauze
   —comes in squares, rectangles, pads, and rolls ([FIGURE 22.20](#f0130)). Gauze dressings absorb moisture.
2. •
   Non-adherent gauze
   —a gauze dressing with a nonstick surface. It does not stick to the wound and is removed easily without injuring the tissue.

Some dressings contain special agents to promote wound healing. If it is considered within your scope of practice, and you are to assist with a dressing change, your supervisor will explain the use of the dressing to you. Dressing application methods involve a number of dressing types, depending on the client’s particular type of wound. Your supervisor or the nurse will direct you in regard to the particulars of dressing change and care for each client.

### Securing Dressings

Dressings must be secure over wounds to avoid microbes entering the wound and drainage escaping (see the Focus on Children: Dressings box and Focus on Older Persons: Dressings box).

#### Tape

Adhesive, paper, plastic, and elastic tapes are common. Adhesive tape sticks well to the skin, but removing the remaining adhesive can be difficult, and the adhesive can irritate the skin. Some skin may come off with the tape, causing an abrasion. For clients who are allergic to adhesive tape, paper and plastic tapes are used since they do not usually cause allergic reactions. Elastic tape allows easier movement of the body part.

Tape is applied to secure the top, middle, and bottom of the dressing ([FIGURE 22.21](#f0135)). The tape extends several centimetres beyond each side of the dressing.
The tape must not encircle the entire body part. If swelling were to occur, circulation to the part would be impaired.

If the client can tolerate it, a picture-frame dressing—where the tape is applied to all four edges—can be used. This method of wound dressing is less likely to wrinkle or fall off. Many simple bandages already have adhesive tapes on all four edges or are transparent and allow for vapour and oxygen exchange through it ([FIGURE 22.22](#f0140)).

Your supervisor may ask you to assist with dressing changes. Some employers allow support workers to apply simple, dry, nonsterile dressings to simple wounds. [BOX 22.7](#b0040) lists guidelines for applying nonsterile dressings (see the Focus on Home Care: Changing Dressings box).

Focus On Children
![icon](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000225/main.assets/icon01-9780323711654.jpg)

Dressings

Children are often afraid of dressing changes. Tape removal is often painful for them, and the wound’s appearance can be frightening. It is important to ensure that the child is calm and cooperative, or the sterile field could become contaminated. A parent or caregiver should hold the child so that the wound can be reached with ease. Holding or playing with a favourite toy is often comforting to the child. To help decrease the child’s fear and apprehension about dressings, you can suggest that the child apply a dressing to a doll (and remove it).

Focus On Older Persons
![icon](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000225/main.assets/icon05-9780323711654.jpg)

Dressings

As we age, our skin becomes thinner and more fragile, so skin tears are more common in older persons. Extreme care is therefore necessary when removing tape. One method that many nurses suggest using is to “peel” the tape off by folding the edge of the tape over and dragging it back. This technique avoids lifting the skin, which can be uncomfortable to the client and can injure the client’s skin.
