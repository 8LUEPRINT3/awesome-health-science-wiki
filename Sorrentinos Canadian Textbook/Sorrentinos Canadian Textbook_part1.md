MARY J. WILK, RN, GNC(C), BA, BScN, MN

In keeping with the approach of the previous editions, the fifth edition of Sorrentino’s Canadian Textbook for the Support Worker serves the needs of students and instructors in educational programs taught in community colleges, secondary schools, and private colleges. This textbook has been written to prepare students to function in the role of support worker in communities and facility settings across Canada. It has been prepared entirely by Canadians for Canadians. Similar to the last edition, this textbook has been written in language that is interesting, easy to read, and easy to understand for most students, regardless of their previous educational background. Because this book is used throughout the country, great care has been taken to use terminology and content that is common and not specific to any one area or province or territory.

Health care workers of today will continue to face legal, ethical, and physical challenges in our diverse and constantly evolving country and world. This textbook is also designed to be an excellent resource for support workers already working in the field—whether in facilities or in community settings—who may have questions about issues they have encountered in their clinical practice. Support workers will learn many new things, experience new situations, and even acquire new skills in the course of their work. Whatever the setting, they will find that learning is an ongoing process, and this textbook and its available learning tools will be a valuable resource that will aid them in that process.

While writing this textbook, we consulted a number of legal and health care experts because we recognize the importance of using terminology and concepts that are both accurate and appropriate to Canada. For example, lawyers were consulted to ensure the accuracy of the legal concepts relating to wills, powers of attorney, and end-of-life care. At the time of writing, COVID-19 has impacted the entire world, and infectious diseases experts and Health Canada have been consulted to provide the most updated information possible. In regard to the content on medication delivery techniques, pharmacists and medical doctors were consulted. In yet another example, in the section on dementia care, mental health specialists and the Alzheimer Society of Canada were consulted and asked for input. Such consultation has resulted in a correct, current, and accurate reflection of today’s educational trends and content for support workers.

## New to the Fifth Canadian Edition

Since the last edition was published, educational and vocational standards for support workers have been released both nationally and within many provinces. To reflect these new standards, as well as anticipated practice trends across Canada, Sorrentino’s Canadian Textbook for the Support Worker, fifth edition, has been revised, updated, and reorganized while keeping the features that students and instructors have benefited from and depended on. Based on feedback from both students and instructors, the order of the chapters has been revised to make them easier and more intuitive for both instructors and students to follow.

### Indigenous Issues

Indigenous peoples represent a significant and expanding group within Canada and include three groups: First Nations, Métis, and Inuit. First Nations refers to Status, Non-Status, and Treaty Indian peoples of Canada. These include the nations, bands, or groups of people who were originally living in Canada before the European explorers began to arrive in the 1600s.

Indigenous issues have been at the forefront of Canadian politics and policy over the past few years. In 2016, Canada adopted the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) and also continued to implement the recommendations of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) in an effort to address many years of legislated wrongs and inequities against Indigenous people.

It is known that TRC recommendations and UNDRIP have started to influence health policies and practices for the diverse Indigenous populations in Canada. In support of UNDRIP and the process of healing and reconciliation, the terms Indigenous people(s) and Indigenous health are used throughout this text to acknowledge the inherent rights and political views of the diverse groups of original peoples with historical and cultural ties to Canada. The term Aboriginal does not fully recognize the diversity of Indigenous Peoples but remains in common use. The term Aboriginal can be found in this textbook when referring to specific current titles or groups.

### Inclusive Language

Canada is made up of individuals from differing socio-economic, cultural, generational, spiritual, geographical, and geopolitical backgrounds. To that end, throughout this text, great effort has been made to capture this mosaic and spectrum of peoples. There has been a move away from a cis norm–focused mindset, and binary terms such as he/she have been replaced with the more inclusive they.

### Trends in Support Work

Health care is constantly changing, and this textbook has been revised to reflect new trends and policies that affect support workers throughout Canada. The chapters on body structure and function and diseases and conditions have been significantly enhanced to ensure that students have the suitable background of anatomy, physiology, physical changes related to aging, and disease processes. The scope of the chapters on mental health disorders and disorientation, delirium, and dementia have been almost completely rewritten to reflect the changes in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5). For example, names of the various disorders have been updated, and the chapters have been expanded to include important discussions on suicide among older persons, post-traumatic stress disorder, and the types of primary and secondary dementias.

An emerging trend in Canada is the expanding role of the support worker in regard to performing delegated acts and working in acute-care settings, where support workers may have the opportunity to perform increasingly advanced tasks and to problem-solve more than in the past. [Chapter 46](../B9780323709392000468/B9780323709392000468.xhtml) focuses on practice that is specific to acute care. Students who read this textbook are frequently reminded about when they should be consulting the client’s care plan or their supervisor for instructions.

This text also recognizes that language is fluid and ever evolving. The author recognizes that childbirth is not only experienced by women but may be experienced by others who do not identify as female or who find the term woman to not be representative of how they identify themselves. The terms client, person, or parent are used when possible in the text. Woman/women is used when the research is specifically done with a population that identifies as women. The language and terminology presented in this text endeavour to be inclusive of all peoples and reflect what is, to the best of our knowledge, current at the time of publication.

Perhaps most importantly, this new edition places a stronger emphasis on understanding the role of support workers and their scope of practice.
Throughout this textbook, students are reminded to check their scope of practice and to be aware of provincial or territorial legislation related to their profession.
 The Appendix at the end of the book outlines the provincial and territorial differences in support worker titles.

## Guiding Principles

This textbook is structured around several key ideas and principles:

1. •
   Support workers provide services in a variety of community and facility settings.
    Because training programs prepare students for a variety of workplaces, multiple workplace settings—long-term care, home care, and hospital settings—are discussed throughout the text, especially in the
   Focus on Home Care
    and
   Focus on Long-Term Care
    boxes and the
   Procedure
    boxes, which highlight information and insights in regard to these settings.
2. •
   Each client is an individual with dignity and value.
    Throughout this textbook, students are reminded that each client is a whole person, with physical, emotional, social, intellectual, and spiritual dimensions. Students are encouraged to appreciate each client as a unique individual with a past, a present, and a future and to practise client-centred care at all times. Students are also taught to recognize a client’s basic needs and protected rights.
3. •
   An essential part of a support worker’s job is to provide compassionate care.
    The acronym DIPPS helps identify, recognize, and promote the six principles of support work—dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, and safety—that are highlighted in
   Providing Compassionate Care
    boxes, which discuss ways to promote the principles of support work when giving the care described in the chapter.
4. •
   Effective communication skills are necessary to develop good working relationships.
    [Chapter 9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml) is devoted to communication skills, and [Chapter 35](../B9780323709392000353/B9780323709392000353.xhtml) discusses communication with clients who have speech and language disorders.
   Case Studies
    and other boxes throughout the text also highlight the importance of clear communication.
5. •
   Support workers must respect the cultural and lifestyle diversity among their clients. Culture influences people’s attitudes and beliefs.
    [Chapter 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml) discusses the role of cultural heritage in health and illness practices as well as in other aspects of life, such as communication.
   Respecting Diversity
    boxes throughout the text provide examples of how support care must be adapted to respect each client’s culture and lifestyle.
6. •
   Students learn best by reading about real-life examples. Case Studies
    and examples that apply concepts to the real world of support work appear throughout the text. Supporting boxes discuss ways to solve the problems that may occur in support work in different settings and help students to understand a particular client’s health challenge or issue and to empathize with the client.
7. •
   Support workers need to understand their scope of practice and the delegation process.
    Because agencies and facilities across Canada vary in the way they utilize support workers, the responsibilities and limitations of support workers are emphasized throughout the text. The text presents many procedures that support workers across the country need to know and points out procedures that require extra training and supervision. Students are advised that they must understand and respect their employer’s policies as well as provincial or territorial laws governing scope of practice. [Chapter 4](../B9780323709392000043/B9780323709392000043.xhtml) focuses on ethical principles, [Chapter 5](../B9780323709392000055/B9780323709392000055.xhtml) addresses specific legislation that affects support workers in Canada, and [Chapter 10](../B9780323709392000109/B9780323709392000109.xhtml) addresses scope of practice and delegation issues.
8. •
   Providing safe care is at the core of support work.
    Ensuring the client’s safety is one of the top priorities in support work and is therefore emphasized throughout the text. The numerous
   Think About Safety
    boxes throughout the textbook list straightforward, easy-to-understand ways to ensure client safety. An entire chapter ([Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)) devoted to safety discusses the major types of accidental injuries among clients and measures to prevent them. It also discusses how support workers can take steps to ensure their own safety on the job. Other safety concepts are detailed throughout
   the book, such as how to prevent the spread of infection ([Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)), the impact of bullying in the workplace ([Chapter 12](../B9780323709392000122/B9780323709392000122.xhtml)), how to recognize and report abuse ([Chapter 12](../B9780323709392000122/B9780323709392000122.xhtml)), and the basic principles of body mechanics and safety while moving and transferring clients ([Chapter 14](../B9780323709392000146/B9780323709392000146.xhtml)).
9. •
   Following the client’s care plan is critical to providing client-centred and thorough care.
    [Chapter 26](../B9780323709392000262/B9780323709392000262.xhtml) describes the care-planning process in both facilities and communities. Students are reminded throughout the text that support workers must follow the care plan and their supervisor’s directions.
10. •
    Support workers need to be effective problem solvers.
     Support workers must make decisions throughout their day, such as what to report to their supervisor; how to safely feed a client who may have choking difficulties ([Chapter 27](../B9780323709392000274/B9780323709392000274.xhtml)); how to safely move and reposition a client ([Chapter 14](../B9780323709392000146/B9780323709392000146.xhtml)); how to dress and assist a client with activities of daily living ([Chapters 17](../B9780323709392000171/B9780323709392000171.xhtml), [18](../B9780323709392000183/B9780323709392000183.xhtml), [29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml), [30](../B9780323709392000304/B9780323709392000304.xhtml), and [45](../B9780323709392000456/B9780323709392000456.xhtml)); and even how to respectfully communicate with clients, taking into account their culture ([Chapters 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml) and [9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)), their age ([Chapters 41](../B9780323709392000419/B9780323709392000419.xhtml), [42](../B9780323709392000420/B9780323709392000420.xhtml), and [43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml)), and existing physical or mental health challenges ([Chapters 12](../B9780323709392000122/B9780323709392000122.xhtml), [33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml), [34](../B9780323709392000341/B9780323709392000341.xhtml), [35](../B9780323709392000353/B9780323709392000353.xhtml), [36](../B9780323709392000365/B9780323709392000365.xhtml), [37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml), and [38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml)).

## Important Notes on Terminology and Equipment

In summary, being aware of the terminology differences existing within this diverse country of ours, instructors (and students) should use whatever term is the choice in their particular region:

1. •
   The use of the generic term “support worker”
   —Throughout the book, the generic term
   support worker
    has been chosen to describe a worker who provides personal care and support to clients in a variety of settings. We recognize that, throughout Canada, support workers are designated a variety of titles; these titles may vary provincially, territorially, or even locally, and it is expected that instructors will explain this terminology difference to students.
2. •
   The use of the generic term “client”
   —The practice in an area or agency might be to refer to the recipient of support services as a patient, resident, consumer, or customer. However, for the purposes of this textbook, we have chosen to use the term client for the sake of simplicity and to make it easy for students, who are in the process of learning a large number of terms related to health care that constitute a whole new language. Depending on the location and the context of where services are provided, the client who receives the services of support workers may be referred to by different terms, For example, Mrs. Jones, who is receiving care in her own home (or in an assisted-living facility) would be referred to as a client by her caregivers. If she were to be admitted to an acute- or complex-care, continuing care, or subacute care facility—such as a hospital—she would then be called a patient. If she needed to live in a continuing care (or long-term care) facility, she would then be called a resident. We discuss the issue of differences in terminology in [Chapter 3](../B9780323709392000031/B9780323709392000031.xhtml). The chapter on medical terminology ([Chapter 25](../B9780323709392000250/B9780323709392000250.xhtml)) reflects current vocabulary and abbreviation practices used in health care facilities across Canada.
3. •
   The use of the generic term “long-term care”
   —In this text, we have also chosen to use the more generic and widely used term long-term care (referred to as LTC in the field) in the context of care that is ongoing and provides relatively stable assistance to people with their activities of daily living (ADLs). It also relates to any type of home or facility where clients are cared for and supported, including long-term care facilities (nursing homes) and even group homes for the developmentally delayed who are not physically or intellectually ill but are unable to care for themselves. While this term is not the preferred term in every province or regional area, we use long-term care throughout the text because it does not have different (and therefore misleading) meanings from one region to another within Canada.
4. In contrast, the term complex care refers to the old terms “intermediate” and “extended” care in British Columbia, while it can mean “acute or subacute care for people with multisystem failure,” a completely different meaning, in Ontario. Some provinces use the term continuing complex care to mean “ongoing care,” while other provinces do not use the term at all. The term continuing care is being widely used throughout Canada in this context, but it is a newer term than long-term care and therefore is not widely recognized in some areas of Canada.
5. •
   The types of treatments and equipment used throughout Canada
   —It should also be noted that we recognize that a wide range of equipment brands are used across the country, so it is possible that some instructors will find within this textbook photographs that depict a piece of equipment that has never been used in their area. We suggest that clinical instructors provide an opportunity for students to observe the equipment that is more familiar to their own area.

# Student Preface

As a support worker, you are a very important member of the health care team because you probably spend more time with your clients than any other member of the team does. Team members rely on your observations, reports, and recordings, especially of any changes in a client. Your clients and their families rely on you to provide professional and safe care. You and the care you give may be the bright spots in a client’s day.

This book was designed to help you learn by using its special features, which are described on the following pages. Since the last edition was published, educational and vocational standards for support workers have been released both nationally and within many provinces. To reflect these new standards, as well as anticipated practice trends across Canada, Sorrentino’s Canadian Textbook for the Support Worker, fifth edition, has been revised, updated, and reorganized to assist you, the student. Even after you graduate, the book will continue to be a useful resource to you in the field as you gain experience and expand your knowledge.

This preface presents some study guidelines and tips for using this book effectively. Your instructor will probably assign chapters or partial chapters from the textbook to read before or after class. When given a reading assignment, do you read from the first page to the last page without stopping? How much of what you read do you remember? Using an efficient study system will help you understand and retain all the information that you read. A useful study system has these steps:

1. • Preview or survey
2. • Question
3. • Read and record
4. • Recite and review

## 1. Preview

Before you start a reading assignment, preview or survey the assignment to get an idea of what it covers and to recall what you already know about the subject. Preview the chapter title, headings, subheadings, and terms or ideas in bold print or italics. Also, survey the objectives, key terms, introductory paragraph, boxes, key points, and the review questions at the end of the chapter. Previewing takes only a few minutes. Remember, previewing helps you become familiar with the material.

## 2. Question

After previewing, you need to form a list of questions to be answered as you read the material. Questions should relate to what might be asked on a test or how the information applies to giving care. Use the title, headings, and subheadings to form questions. Avoid questions that have one-word answers. Questions that begin with what, how, or why are most helpful. While reading, if you find that a particular question does not help you understand and retain the assignment material, change the question to make this step more useful.

## 3. Read and Record

Reading, which is the next step, is more productive after you have determined what you already know and what you need to learn. The purpose of reading is to:

1. • Gain new information
2. • Connect the new information to what you already know

Break the assignment into smaller parts, and as you read each part, try to find answers for the questions you had formulated earlier. Also, mark important information in the text by underlining, highlighting, or making notes, which will remind you later what you need to go back to in order to review and learn. Making notes helps you remember what you have learned. When making notes, write down important information in the text margins or in a notebook. Use words and summary statements that will jog your memory about the material.

After reading the assignment, in order to retain the information, you need to organize it into a study guide—in the form of diagrams or charts that show
relationships or steps in a process. Much of the information in this text is organized in this manner to help you learn. Note-taking in outline format is also very useful.

The following is a sample outline:

* I. Main heading
* + 1. Second-level heading
  + 2. Second-level heading
  + - a. Third-level heading
    - b. Third-level heading
* II. Main heading

## 4. Recite and Review

Finally, recite and review, using your notes and the study guide, by finding the answers for the questions you formed earlier and any others that may have come up during the reading and as you answered the review questions at the end of the chapter. Answer all questions out loud (recite). If you are unsure about the answers to any of the questions, consult your instructor.

Reviewing is more about when to study than what to study. You already decided what to study during the preview, question, and reading steps. Your instructor may have emphasized key points from the reading assignment in class. The best times to review both the information in your text and your notes from class are (1) the same day or evening of the class, (2) right after your first study session, (3) 1 week later, and (4) regularly before a quiz or test, midterm, or final exam. Studying the information many times will help you remember it.

# Chapter 1: The Role and Responsibilities of the Support Worker ![icon](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000018/main.assets/co01-9780323711654.jpg)

Objectives

After reading this chapter, the learner should be able to do the following:

• Describe the goal of support work.

• Identify the role support workers play within the health care team.

• Describe the main responsibilities of the support worker.

• Differentiate between activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs).

• Distinguish between regulated and unregulated health care providers.

• Describe the importance of understanding the roles and responsibilities of support work.

• Describe the significance of having a professional approach to support work.

• List the principles of client-centred care.

• Identify the things to consider when thinking critically and solving problems.

Key Terms

activities of daily living (ADLs)

   Self-care activities people perform daily to remain independent and to function in society. p. 4

advocate

   To speak or act on behalf of another person or group of people. p. 3

caring

   Concern for clients’ and their families’ dignity, independence, preferences, privacy, and safety at all times. True caring requires honesty, sensitivity, comforting, discretion, and respect while showing this concern. p. 16

client A general term for any person receiving care or support services in a community setting:

   hospital patients, facility residents, and clients in the community. p. 9

client-centred care

   Care that demonstrates concern for the dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, and safety (DIPPS) of clients and their families at all times. Also known as compassionate care or person-centred care. p. 16

compassionate care

   See client-centred care. p. 16

confidentiality

   Respecting, guarding, and using discretion in regard to personal and private information about another person. p. 14

critical thinking

   The ability to think clearly and logically, using reflection and reason, based on knowledge obtained from experience, observation, or education. p. 13

dignity

   The state of feeling worthy, valued, and respected. p. 15

DIPPS An acronym for the six principles of client-centred care (also known as compassionate care or person-centred care):

   dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, and safety. p. 16

discretion

   The use of responsible judgement to avoid causing distress or embarrassment to a person. p. 14

empathy

   The ability to recognize, perceive, and have an understanding of another person’s emotions by seeing things from his or her viewpoint. p. 3

instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs)

   The complex skills needed to successfully live independently. p. 4

licensed practical nurse (LPN)

   A health care provider licensed and regulated by the province or territory to carry out nursing techniques and provide client care. Known as a registered practical nurse (RPN) in Ontario. p. 13

live-in facility

   A facility that provides living accommodations and services; includes assisted-living facilities, long-term care facilities, group homes, and retirement residences. p. 9

patient

   A person receiving care in a hospital setting. Also known as a client. p. 9

primary care nurse

   A primary care nurse is responsible for the ongoing management of the health of a client. Duties include liaising with other health care team members, the client, and her or his family. p. 13

professionalism

   An approach to work that demonstrates respect for others, commitment, competence, and appropriate behaviour. p. 14

reflective practice review

   A review and self-evaluation of care provided with the goal of identifying ways to improve clinical performance and caregiving. p. 13

registered nurse (RN)

   A health care provider who is licensed and regulated by the province or territory to maintain overall responsibility for the planning and provision of client care. p. 13

registered practical nurse (RPN)

   See licensed practical nurse (LPN). p. 13

registered psychiatric nurse (RPN)

   A nurse who is educated and registered in his or her own province to provide care specifically to individuals whose primary needs relate to mental, emotional, and developmental health. p. 12

rehabilitation

   The process of restoring a person to the highest possible level of functioning through the use of therapy, exercise, or other methods. p. 10

resident

   A person living in a live-in facility (often called a long-term care facility). p. 9

scope of practice

   The limit and extent of a health care worker’s role as determined by your province’s educational standards and your employer’s policies in order to protect clients from harm. Scope of practice in a health care field varies from province to province and employer to employer. p. 12

social reintegration

   See social support. p. 3

social support

   Equipping a person with the skills and knowledge necessary to successfully live independently outside an institution. Also known as social reintegration. p. 3

support worker

   A health care worker who provides services to people, both in facilities and in the community, who need help with their daily activities. p. 3

Support workers provide care and assistance to clients of any age who have defined health care needs and who require help with their daily activities, whether they live in a facility or in the community.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur11) Client is a general term for any person receiving care or support services in a community setting, such as hospital patients, facility residents, and clients in the community. The ultimate goal of support work is to improve the quality of life of the client and family by offering client-centred care and support in a safe, kind, sensitive, caring, and understanding manner. Support workers can make a difference in people’s lives by alleviating loneliness, providing empathy, ensuring comfort, encouraging independence, and promoting the client’s self-respect ([FIGURE 1.1](#f0010)). They support the client and family in a holistic manner by addressing the client’s physical, psychological, social, cognitive, cultural, and spiritual needs and advocate for the client by speaking or acting on the client’s behalf.

Support workers may work as part of a health care team or work individually with a client. Legislation, employer policies, and a client’s condition all influence how support workers function and how much supervision they need. They may collaborate with health care providers or with professionals outside of the health care sector, depending on the needs of their clients. Support workers must use discretion at all times and honour the client’s right to confidentiality whenever discussing the client’s case with co-workers. Support workers are expected to adapt their work style to the setting and to the needs and wishes of the client receiving care.

## Support Work Across Canada

The nature of support work varies across the country. There are differences in educational programs, work settings, job responsibilities, and terms used to describe support workers. Some sections of this text may not apply to support work in your particular city, province, or territory. If you are unsure about which parts apply to your area, ask your instructor or employer.

The term support worker refers to the worker who provides personal care and support services. However, assisted-living worker, personal support worker, personal attendant, patient care assistant, resident care aide, resident care attendant, health care aide, home care attendant, home support worker, nursing aide, nursing attendant, community health worker, or continuing care assistant—among other titles—may be used in your province or territory to describe similar (but not always equivalent) jobs. The Appendix at the end of this book lists the support worker’s titles in each province and territory. Because of the wide variety of titles, the general public may not be aware of what support workers are called in their own province or territory or even within a facility they use. It is the role of support workers, their employing agencies, other health care providers, and their province’s or territory’s Ministry of Health to inform others of their role and how it fits in their current health care structure.

This list may also include several titles that are not equivalent to one another. For example, in Ontario, developmental service workers (DSWs) play a different role and follow different educational standards than personal support workers (PSWs), and yet both are considered to be support workers within the health care team. In some parts of Canada, the term personal attendant refers to a worker who is supervised directly by the person for whom they provide support services; personal attendants support people with physical disabilities. Educational programs to become a personal attendant are generally shorter than those for support workers.

Support workers work in a variety of settings (see [Chapter 3](../B9780323709392000031/B9780323709392000031.xhtml) for definitions and details of workplace settings). In all settings, support workers ensure the safety of their clients, including those who are living with cognitive or mental health challenges. Services provided by support workers to clients in their homes can enable some clients to live independently or with their families. Support workers may also assist clients with social support, or social reintegration, as
they prepare to move into an assisted-living facility or group home. In long-term care facilities, support workers assist clients with complex health challenges. Support workers also provide comfort and end-of-life care to dying clients so that they can die with dignity.

### Support Worker Responsibilities

A support worker’s general responsibilities, depending on the jurisdiction in which they work, can be grouped into six categories: (1) personal care, (2) support for nurses and other health care providers, (3) family support, (4) social support, (5) housekeeping or home management, and (6) documenting and reporting any basic assessments, observations, and care provided in the client’s record or chart.

#### Personal Care

Personal care responsibilities include assisting with activities of daily living (ADLs). These are the self-care actions that people perform every day to remain independent and to function effectively in society. Support workers help with daily activities such as eating, bathing, grooming, dressing, and toileting (“elimination”). They assist clients with limited mobility to change positions or move from one place to another and also help promote the client’s safety and physical comfort.

In community settings, support workers also assist clients with instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), which are the complex and necessary skills needed to successfully live independently. These IADLs may include assisting the client with handling finances, assisting with management of medications, arranging transportation, shopping, preparing meals, assisting with using a telephone or other communication devices, and doing housework and basic home maintenance.[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur12),[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur13)

The support worker is not responsible for deciding what should or should not be done for the client. However, while providing personal care, the support worker observes for and reports any changes in the client’s behaviour or health. Such information is important to share with the appropriate person (either the charge nurse or the client’s primary care nurse).

#### Support for Nurses and Other Health Care Providers

Support workers assist nurses or other health care team members by following the established care plan for each client; for instance, they may be required to clean equipment, measure and report vital signs, or assist with simple wound care. Support workers might also assist with oxygen therapy, heat or cold applications, and range-of-motion (ROM) exercises. They are often the supervisor’s “eyes and ears,” meaning that because they are the person who spends the most time with the client, they are most likely to observe or hear things that should be reported to the supervisor, who may be a nurse or another type of health care worker. Observing and reporting are very important parts of the support worker’s role and can have a great impact on a client’s care.

Support workers may have to consult with other health care providers, such as social workers or physiotherapists, and may also have to consult with other professionals, such as the client’s employers, clergy, or teachers, depending on the client’s individual care plan.

#### Family Support

In many facilities, support workers assist with admissions and discharges by introducing the client and family to the facility and helping the client unpack and settle in. In private homes, support workers help families care for loved ones who are living with health issues or need assistance with ADLs. Some families may need help with preparing meals and doing household chores; other families may need help with child care. Support worker services often give family caregivers a break from their duties.

#### Social Support

Support workers may help clients participate in social activities. These activities provide the client with enjoyment, recreation, and a chance to meet with friends. They may organize games and outings or may be hired privately to be a client’s companion. Support workers may also be responsible for teaching clients to learn to live independently and to cook, clean, or shop by themselves.

#### Housekeeping or Home Management

Support workers often do a variety of housekeeping tasks in a facility setting, including making beds, delivering meals, tidying up living areas, and maintaining supplies. In a private home, housekeeping is called home management. Services depend on the needs of
the person and the resources available to provide these services. Duties may include assisting clients with their IADLs, doing light housekeeping, doing laundry, and preparing and serving nutritious meals.

#### Documenting and Reporting any Basic Assessments or Observations and Care Provided

As part of their professional accountability, all health care workers are legally responsible for reporting and documenting any client care they provide, as well as basic assessments and important observations about their clients. Examples of basic assessments or observations include if the client complains of pain, or if they refuse to eat. Understanding what is important to report is part of the support worker’s need to think critically (see page 13). Depending on the agency, documentation may be in a written or electronic format (see [Chapter 26](../B9780323709392000262/B9780323709392000262.xhtml)).

In the Community (Home Care)

Each evening, Stephen receives his assignment for the next day from his supervisor. He uses the details in the assignment to plan his day. He consults a city map and plans his route. The people he is assigned to visit are living with a range of physical, emotional, and social issues and disabilities. Their major issues are briefly described below.

Ms. Lau, 32, is living with cerebral palsy. She uses a wheelchair. She lives alone. Two days per week, she works outside the home. She receives home care to help her prepare for work.

Mr. O’Connor, 59, is recovering at home from a stroke. He is paralyzed on one side of his body and has a speech–language disorder. His wife is his primary caregiver. Mr. O’Connor receives home care three mornings a week. Mrs. O’Connor, 51, is at work during Stephen’s visit.

Mr. Horowitz, 71, is living with dementia. His wife, 67, is caring for him at home. The couple gets emotional and social support from family and friends. Mrs. Horowitz looks after her husband’s personal care needs. They receive 2 hours of home care per week to give Mrs. Horowitz a break.

Ms. Adams, 25, is a single mother on social assistance. She is recovering from a Caesarean section. She gets very little social and emotional support. She has newborn twins and three young children aged 1, 3, and 4 years.

### People Who Receive Support Services

People receiving health care and support services are known by different terms, depending on the setting. A person receiving care in a hospital is usually called a patient. A person living in a live-in facility is called a resident. A person receiving care or support services in the community is called a client. Client is also an all-encompassing term for people receiving health care or support services: hospital patients, facility residents, and clients in the community.

Whether the individual receiving care is known as a client, patient, or resident, always remember that he or she is a person. Every person is unique, with unique life experiences and situations, desires and opinions. Each will have different needs and different abilities. Support workers work with people from a variety of cultures or backgrounds (see [Chapter 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml)). Part of the support worker’s job is to accept this diversity among people. The Respecting Diversity boxes that appear throughout this text outline the importance of diversity and how people’s backgrounds influence who they are and what they do.

Clients can be grouped according to their issues, needs, and ages:

1. •
   Older persons.
    Aging is a normal process and is not an illness or disease. Many older persons enjoy good health. However, body changes normally occur with the aging process. Social and emotional changes may also occur (see [Chapter 43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml)). The risks for contracting serious illnesses and becoming disabled increase with age. Most older persons remain at home as long as possible. Others are unable to manage even with assistance and move into a live-in facility. Throughout the text, issues relevant to older persons are discussed.
2. •
   People with disabilities.
    Some people are living with disabilities due to illness, injury, or conditions present at birth. Disabilities may affect physical functioning, mental functioning, or both. Many adults with disabilities live in their own homes. Many work outside their homes. Support workers might help clients living with disabilities with their ADLs or might be responsible for teaching them how to perform the ADLs independently.
3. •
   People with medical issues.
    Medical issues include illnesses, diseases, and injuries. These may include short-term (such as a broken bone), long-term (such as diabetes or multiple sclerosis), or progressive and life-threatening (such as some types of cancer) issues.
4. •
   People having surgery.
    Surgical clients are those being prepared for surgery or who have recently had surgery. Preoperative care includes preparing the client for what to expect after surgery and addressing the client’s fears and anxieties. Needs after surgery relate to relieving pain and discomfort, preventing complications, and helping the client adjust to body changes. People recover from surgeries in hospitals or in their homes.
5. •
   People with mental health issues.
    Mental health issues range from mild to severe. Some people function normally but need help making decisions or coping with life stresses. Others are severely affected and need assistance with ADLs.
6. •
   People needing rehabilitation.
    Rehabilitation is the process of restoring a person to the highest possible level of functioning through the use of therapy, exercise, or other methods. The person may need to regain functions lost due to surgery, illness, or accident. Some hospitals have special rehabilitation units. Many people receiving support at home and in long-term care settings require rehabilitation.
7. •
   Children.
    When hospital care is needed, children are admitted to the pediatric unit. In some areas of Canada, support workers are hired to work in pediatric units ([FIGURE 1.2](#f0015)). However, most support work for children occurs in community settings and long-term care facilities. Some children who receive care are living with physical or intellectual disabilities. Others need care because a parent has a medical challenge or has just had a new baby. The Focus on Children boxes and [Chapter 42](../B9780323709392000420/B9780323709392000420.xhtml) discuss issues related to caring for children.
8. •
   Birth parents and newborns. Complications and difficulties can occur at any time during pregnancy and even up to 6 to 8 weeks following childbirth. Some new birth parents need assistance with their own care or with their newborn’s care. Most support work with birth parents and newborns takes place in the home (see [Chapter 41](../B9780323709392000419/B9780323709392000419.xhtml)).
9. •
   People requiring special care.
    Some people who have serious and complex medical conditions need special care and equipment. Hospitals have special care units, including critical care units, coronary care units, kidney dialysis units, burn units, and emergency departments. In some areas of Canada, support workers are hired to work in these units. Support workers might transport people from one unit to another; take specimens to the lab; assist clients with bathing, feeding, mobility, or toileting; and assist other health care providers with special procedures. In some parts of Canada, support workers are not allowed to provide personal care to clients in unstable or critical conditions.

## The Health Care Team

A team is a group of people working together toward a common goal. Health care teams include professionals with a variety of skills and knowledge who work together to meet the client’s needs. Their goal is to provide quality care. Many professionals, including support workers, are involved in the care of one client. Which professionals are involved depends on the needs of the client.

### Regulated and Unregulated Workers

Health care professions are either regulated or unregulated. A regulated profession is self-governing. It has a professional organization called a college, which sets education and licence requirements. It also establishes the scope of practice, code of ethics, and standards of conduct for its members. In the case of a complaint about a member’s conduct, the college investigates and, if necessary, disciplines members guilty of misconduct. Each regulated health care profession has legislation that details the roles and responsibilities of its members. Nursing is one of many regulated health care professions.

Unregulated care providers (UCPs) are health care providers who perform clearly identified services under the direction and supervision of a client, family member, regulated health care provider, or employer. However, UCPs are not regulated through legislation and are not members of an organization or professional college that governs their role. While UCPs do not currently follow any official code of ethics, they must adhere to the codes of behaviour dictated by their employers (see [Chapter 4, Box 4.1](../B9780323709392000043/B9780323709392000043.xhtml), for A Sample Code of Ethics for Support Workers, on page 51). At this point, support workers in most provinces and territories are considered to be UCPs.

### Scope of Practice

Support workers must understand what to do, what not to do, and the limit and extent of a health care worker’s role as determined by their province’s educational standards and employer’s policies in order to protect clients from harm. The term scope of practice is not a copyrighted term, and it is not intended only for regulated professionals. Instead, it is used to identify what a support worker may and may not do in relation to client care.

Never act beyond the legal limits of your role. Also, never perform a function or task that you have not been trained to do. If you perform a task that is outside these limits, you could harm a client and create serious legal problems for yourself and your employer.

The support worker’s scope of practice in any province, territory, city, or municipality is determined from three important sources. Those sources include the following:

* 1.
  Provincial/territorial educational standard of practice:
   Many provinces now follow either the national or provincial program standards, which dictate what support workers need to be taught in their educational programs. The support worker educational program includes information on the scope of practice for support work in that province or territory. Students will learn about the laws and the support worker’s professional responsibilities in their part of the country. Students should ask their instructors if they require any clarification. (Please visit <http://www.novascotiacca.ca/DocumentViewer.aspx?elementId=106908&portalName=ha> to view an example of a scope of practice document for the province of Nova Scotia.)
* 2.
  Employer’s policies
  : Every employer has written policies that establish what can and cannot be done by support workers. These policies should be read carefully before starting work.
* 3.
  Supervisor:
   On the job, the supervisor is the best source of clarification of the employer’s policies. It is far better to ask for direction than risk harming a client, so never hesitate to ask questions or request clarification about any procedure.

#### Professional Development and Lifelong Learning

Being a professional requires critical thinking and reflective practice review. Critical thinking is the ability to think clearly and logically, using reflection and reason, based on knowledge obtained from experience, observation, or education. A reflective practice review is a self-evaluation of care provided with the goal of identifying ways to improve clinical performance and caregiving.

Health care is an ever-changing field. It is the responsibility of every professional in the field to ensure that all clinical skills and knowledge are up to date, since their own health and safety and the health and safety of co-workers and clients are at stake. Depending on the region, many postgraduate certificate courses and workshops may be available to support workers, such as advanced palliative care, conflict management courses, or medication management. Support workers also need to be aware of the scope of practice in their province or territory, since some, in particular situations, have been asked to perform tasks that are not within the support worker’s scope.

### The Supervision of Support Workers

In facilities, in the community, or in private homes, support workers may be supervised by a nurse or other health care provider. A registered nurse (RN) is licensed and regulated by the province or territory to maintain overall responsibility for the planning and provision of client care. Some RNs have university degrees and even postgraduate education. Others have community college diplomas. RNs assess clients, make nursing diagnoses, develop care plans, and implement and evaluate nursing care. They also carry out physicians’ orders. An RN is usually a team leader of the health care team, which includes RPNs or LPNs (see below), support workers, and other allied health care providers.

A licensed practical nurse (LPN), also known as a registered practical nurse (RPN), is licensed and regulated by the province or territory to carry out basic nursing procedures and provide client care. RPNs and LPNs have a community college diploma, although many also have university degrees and even postgraduate education. Like RNs, they must hold a current nursing registration in the province that they practise in. RPNs or LPNs function in a decision-making position when caring for stable clients with uncomplicated health issues, but when providing care to clients with serious and unstable health issues, they assist RNs and help with complex procedures. RPNs and LPNs often supervise support workers.

Some clients, particularly in a live-in environment, may be assigned to a primary care nurse, who is responsible for the ongoing management of the health of a client. Duties of this nurse include liaising with other health care team members, the client, and her or his family. In an acute-care environment, the primary care nurse may or may not be directly involved in providing the client care on a daily basis but will oversee the work of those who do, including the support worker.

In some situations, a support worker may be supervised by a professional other than an RN, RPN, or LPN. For example, in the recreation department
of a long-term care facility, support workers may report to a recreational therapist. In the community, a supervisor may be a social worker, a physiotherapist, or another health care provider.

## Being a Professional

Professionalism is an approach to work that demonstrates respect for others, commitment, competence, and appropriate behaviour. Being cheerful and friendly, keeping work schedules, performing tasks competently, and being helpful are all part of a professional approach. To be a true professional, you must demonstrate the following:

1. •
   Positive attitude.
    You need to show a good attitude about your job. The work you do is very important. People rely on you to give efficient care and support. You need to believe that you and your work are valuable and show that you enjoy your work. Be enthusiastic, considerate, courteous, honest, and cooperative. Speak in a professional manner, and avoid the use of slang terms or profane language. Always think before you speak, do not gossip, and do not complain. Your words reveal your attitude. [BOX 1.1](#b0010) lists some statements to avoid so as not to show a negative attitude and how they can be reworded.
2. •
   Sense of responsibility.
    Never blame others for your problems or mistakes at work. Admit your errors, accept constructive criticism, and learn from others. Always report to work when scheduled and on time. Everyone on the team, including the client, is affected when even one person is late. Have a plan ready for times when you are urgently needed at home or your transportation is unavailable. Inform your supervisor immediately if you will be late or unable to work. Also, be sure to finish assigned tasks before you leave for the day. The client’s care cannot be neglected for any reason. In the case that you cannot finish your assignment, promptly explain why to your supervisor.
3. •
   Professional appearance.
    A professional, appropriate appearance shows respect for the people in your care, your co-workers, and yourself. It indicates that you take your job seriously. Your appearance includes your clothes, grooming, and hygiene ([BOX 1.2](#b0015) and [FIGURE 1.3](#f0020)).
4. •
   Discretion about client information.
    Discretion means showing good judgement about what you say, how you say it, when you say it, and where you say it. You need to judge when information should be kept private and when it should be shared. Information about a client is confidential. Confidentiality means respecting and guarding personal and private information about another person. Information should be shared only among the health care team members involved in the client’s care. Information about your employer, your co-workers, and other clients is also private. For example, you must
   never talk with a client about another client, even if you avoid using names. Also, avoid talking about clients, co-workers, and your employer where you can be overheard; if you need to discuss a client’s care with team members, make sure that other clients, families, and visitors cannot hear you. People overhearing may think you are talking about them or their family members. This assumption can lead to misinformation and confusion, which can be distressing to those affected.

## The Goal of Support Work: Improved Quality of Life Through Client-Centred Care

Client-centred care (also known as compassionate or person-centred care) or caring means having concern for the dignity, independence, preferences, privacy, and safety of clients and their families at all times. It is important that any communication with or care given to a client be individualized for that client. The goal of support work is to demonstrate true client-centred care by following this principle and treating clients with kindness, honesty, sensitivity, comfort, discretion, respect, and understanding. Many people who require support are coping with serious illness, disability, personal issues, or challenges, and they depend on the care given to them for survival and improved quality of life. The acronym DIPPS is a reminder of the six principles of client-centred care—Dignity, Independence, Individualized care, Preferences, Privacy, and Safety—which is the goal of support work:

1. •
   To preserve their dignity. Dignity is the state of feeling worthy, valued, and respected. People need to feel dignified.
2. •
   To live independently.
    Clients need to do what they can for themselves.
3. •
   To have all care individualized for each client. No person is identical to another person, so care should be personalized to each client.
4. •
   To express their preferences.
    Clients need to make choices and explain how they want to have things done.
5. •
   To preserve their privacy.
    Clients need to know that their bodies and their affairs are treated respectfully and protected from public view ([FIGURE 1.4](#f0025)).
6. •
   To be safe from harm. Clients need to live in an environment that is as hazard free as possible. They also need to feel secure about the care provided.

When well and able-bodied, most people take the fulfillment of the six needs listed above for granted. When they are living with disabilities or suffer serious illnesses, however, these needs may be more difficult to fulfill. Those who rely on others for personal care may worry about losing their dignity, and they may not feel free to express their wishes. For example, clients living in long-term care facilities may have to eat what is provided and socialize only at prearranged times. They may share a room with another client and find that private moments are rare. Safety concerns are serious issues for clients with illnesses or disabilities. For example, they may worry about reaching the bathroom without falling.

Clients with illnesses or disabilities do not all have the same needs. However, most have at least some of the needs just discussed. To help you recognize clients’ needs, Providing Client-Centred Care boxes throughout the text discuss the goal of support work, which is to follow the principles of client-centred or compassionate care when performing procedures with clients ([BOX 1.4](#b0025)).

## Critical Thinking, Decision Making, and Problem-Solving

Support workers think critically and make many decisions in the course of a workday. For example, they estimate the time each task will take and plan the best way to complete their work on time, taking into account the various needs of the client. Many decisions involve critical thinking in order to solve problems.

When solving problems, consider the following:

1. •
   The goal of support work.
    Solutions to problems should not compromise the six principles of client-centred care: dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, and safety, or DIPPS.
2. •
   The client’s viewpoint.
    Involve clients in solving problems that concern them. Examine the problem from the client’s point of view.
3. •
   Scope of practice.
    Learn and observe the rules of your workplace. Know the limits of your role.
4. •
   Supervisor’s viewpoint.
    Decide if the problem is one that you can handle on your own or one that your supervisor should handle. Your supervisor should provide guidance about which problems you can deal with on your own.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• The ultimate goal of support work is to improve the client’s quality of life by following the principles of client-centred care (DIPPS). The support worker tends to the person’s needs, which include physical needs as well as helping to relieve loneliness, providing comfort, encouraging independence, and promoting the person’s self-respect. Care should always be individualized to each client. A support worker’s services to people in their homes help them remain independent and continue to live with their families. Support workers make a difference in people’s lives.

• Support workers can work in community-based or facility-based settings. Duties will vary according to the setting.

• Most of a support worker’s responsibilities can be grouped into six categories: (1) personal care, (2) support for nurses and other health care providers, (3) family support, (4) social support, (5) housekeeping or home management, and (6) documenting and reporting any basic assessments, observations, and care provided in the client’s record or chart.

• Depending on their setting, people receiving health care and support services are known by different terms, such as patient (in a hospital), resident (in a live-in facility), or client (in the community).

• The people supported can be grouped according to their problems, needs, and ages.

• At this point, support workers in most provinces and territories are still considered health care providers not regulated through legislation, or unregulated care providers (UCPs). Support workers are accountable to their supervisors, employers, and clients; they do not presently have an organization or college that governs their role. Regulated care providers are health care providers licensed and regulated by the province or territory to carry out care that is within their scope of practice.

• There are many members of the health care team, with the client always at the centre of care.

• It is important that support workers understand their scope of practice and never act beyond their role.

• In many settings, support workers are supervised by nurses or other professionals.

• Professionalism includes having a positive attitude, a sense of responsibility, a professional appearance, being discreet about client information, being a lifelong learner, advocating for the client, being discreet about personal matters, and using acceptable speech and language.

• While discussing the client with co-workers, support workers must use discretion at all times and honour the client’s right to confidentiality. Confidentiality means respecting and guarding personal and private information about another person.

• Part of being a professional, reflective practice reviews consist of a review and self-evaluation of any care provided and identification of ways to improve clinical performance.

• When thinking critically, making decisions, and solving problems, support workers must consider the principles of client-centred care (DIPPS), the client’s viewpoint, their scope of practice, and their supervisor’s viewpoint.

Most Canadians believe that quality health care should be available to all Canadian citizens, regardless of their ability to pay for services. Canada’s national health insurance system, known as medicare, which is envied throughout the world, was developed to achieve this goal. Medicare uses provincial or territorial taxes and federal taxes to pay for all medically necessary health services for all permanent residents.

As members of Canada’s changing health care system, support workers have an increasingly important role to play.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur21)

## Canada’s Current Health Care System

Canada’s publicly funded health care system is best described as an interlocking set of health insurance plans, with the federal government and the 10 provincial and 3 territorial governments sharing responsibilities within Canada’s health care system. The provincial and territorial governments fund health care services with assistance from the federal government. To receive their full share of federal funding for health care, provincial and territorial medicare plans must meet five criteria—comprehensiveness, universality, portability, accessibility, and public administration (see [BOX 2.1](#b0010) on page 22)—that are provided in the federal government’s Canada Health Act.

Medicare provides access to universal, comprehensive coverage for hospital and physician services that are medically necessary. These services are administered and delivered by the provincial and territorial governments to Canadian residents with
no payment necessary upon delivery of the service. However, each province or territory is responsible for developing its own health care policies, so some policy inconsistencies may exist from one province to another.

BOX 2.1 The Principles of Medicare, as Listed in the Canada Health Act (1984)

* 1. Comprehensiveness. The insurance plan must pay for all medically necessary services. In a hospital, all necessary drugs, supplies, and diagnostic tests are covered. A range of necessary services provided outside a hospital are also covered.
* 2. Universality. Every permanent resident of a province or territory is entitled to receive the insured health care services provided by the plan, on similar terms and conditions.
* 3. Portability. People can keep their health care coverage even if they are unemployed, change jobs, relocate between provinces and territories, or travel within Canada or abroad. However, if they are moving, it is their responsibility to inform their province or territory and to register with their new province or territory. During the transition, they will be covered by their previous jurisdiction’s health coverage for up to 3 months (see Supporting Mr. Wloch: An Example of Canada’s Health Insurance Portability box on page 31).
* 4. Accessibility. People can receive medically necessary services regardless of their income, age, health status, gender, or geographical location. Additional charges (privatization) for publicly insured services are not permitted.
* 5. Public administration. The insurance plan must be run by a public organization on a nonprofit basis. The public organization must be accountable to the citizens and the government of the province or territory.

The responsibility for Indigenous peoples’ health services is shared by federal, provincial, and territorial governments and Indigenous organizations. Together with these organizations and communities, Health Canada carries out many activities to help people of all ages stay healthy and to prevent persistent and contagious diseases. Improvements have occurred as a result of efforts to extend lifespan and prevent infant deaths. However, gaps still remain in the overall health status of Indigenous peoples compared with that of other Canadians. For example, the occurrence of suicide, injury, and diabetes is higher among First Nations, Métis, and Inuit people than the Canadian average.[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur22),[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur23),[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur24)

Many other organizations and groups, including health care providers’ professional associations as well as organizations involved with accreditation, education, research, and voluntary assistance, contribute to health care delivery in Canada.

Public health, too, is a shared responsibility. While public health services are generally delivered at the provincial, territorial, and municipal levels, the federal Public Health Agency of Canada acts as a focal point for disease prevention and control and for emergency response to outbreaks of infectious diseases.

### The Evolution of Canada’s Health Care System

In the first part of the twentieth century, individuals in Canada were expected to pay the entire amount of their doctors’ bills and hospital fees. Often, there were no “set fees” for services; that is, for the same treatment, a physician could charge one patient a certain amount and another patient an entirely different fee, depending on what the physician thought the patient could afford to pay. Those who could not afford to pay sometimes had to access charity through community agencies such as the Victorian Order of Nurses, the Red Cross, and local churches ([FIGURE 2.1](#f0010)), and some people just went without health care altogether.

The Great Depression of the 1930s had a dramatic effect on Canada’s health care system. Families struggled to feed, clothe, and house their members and could not possibly pay their medical bills. A serious illness or stay in a hospital caused financial disaster for many. The cost of care prevented others from even seeking medical treatment, so those who had illnesses and disabilities would instead depend on family members and neighbours to provide care.

These hardships inspired Canadians to create a prepaid medical and hospitalization insurance plan.
In 1947, under Premier Tommy Douglas, Saskatchewan became the first province to introduce a public insurance plan that covered the costs of hospital services ([BOX 2.2](#b0015)). By 1972, all provinces and territories had extended their insurance plans to also cover medical services provided outside hospitals. Again, provincial and territorial governments and the federal government shared the health care expenses roughly equally. Modern medicare began that year, giving all permanent residents free access to the same quality of hospital and medical care, regardless of their personal financial status.

### The Federal Role in Health Care

The federal government is responsible for the following:

1. • Administering the Canada Health Act and providing provincial funding
2. • Providing direct delivery of health care services to specific groups, such as First Nations people living on reserves; Inuit peoples; serving members of the Canadian Forces and the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP); eligible veterans; and inmates of federal penitentiaries[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur25)
3. • Developing and carrying out government policy and programs that promote health and prevent disease; for example, the federal government approves drugs, assesses health risks posed by environmental hazards, and provides funds to support public health programs such as prenatal health education[6](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur26)
4. • Transferring tax money to the provinces and territories to share the cost of medically necessary health care services
5. • Prohibiting service providers (such as physicians) from billing clients extra charges and user fees and
   ensuring that all the provinces and territories provide the same quality and type of care.

#### Indigenous Health Care Practices and Universal Health Care: A Call to Action

The Canadian government has recognized that there have been numerous challenges to delivering health care to Indigenous peoples across Canada. Challenges such as geography, lack of organization, language or cultural barriers, and socioeconomic status have resulted in universal health care access only slowly becoming available to Indigenous people (see [BOX 2.3](#b0020) on this page).

However, when health care has been made available, Indigenous health practices have usually been overlooked in favour of non-Indigenous medically focused practices, especially for the prevention and treatment of chronic conditions and promotion of health and wellness. This refusal to accept traditional health practices as being legitimate is a symptom of a bigger problem that has happened across Canada. There are generations of Indigenous people who have suffered physically and mentally as a result of a historical policy of separating children from their families and schooling them in live-in facilities. These children were forced to forget their Indigenous language, beliefs, and practices. In an important document issued in 2015, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada: Calls to Action, seven Health Calls to Action were identified as being necessary to redress this legacy of residential schools for Indigenous Canadians (see [BOX 2.4](#b0025) on page 25).

Mainstream Western medical practices are now evolving to include forms of medicinal health philosophies and treatments that were once considered to be “alternative” (see “Alternative Health Practices” on page 31). Indigenous health and wellness is based on a holistic model of health. An integral focus of Indigenous healing and wellness is through the balance and interrelationships of the physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual aspects of a being ([FIGURE 2.2](#f0015)). This model of health is very similar to current mainstream models of health throughout the world.[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur27)

Traditional healing refers to health practices, approaches, knowledge, and beliefs that incorporate
Indigenous healing and wellness while using ceremonies; plant, animal, or mineral-based medicines; energetic therapies; or physical/hands-on techniques (see [Chapters 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml), 44, and [45](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml)).[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur27) New government strategies are now encouraging access to health services for Indigenous people that are culturally sensitive and inclusive.[8](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur28)

### Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Health Care Delivery

In the past, most health care funding went to hospitals and equipment, and the focus of health care was to make people better after they became ill. The Canadian government has recognized that secondary or tertiary health care delivery, while still important, can be shortened or prevented altogether with adequate preventive measures taken in the primary health care delivery stage. Every province and territory provides primary, secondary, and tertiary health care ([FIGURE 2.3](#f0020)).

Primary health care delivery is the first point of contact that most people have with the health care system (e.g., through a doctor, a nurse, or another health care provider, or perhaps through
phone-based or computer-based services). Primary health care delivery usually takes place in the community, but it can also take place within a hospital. Primary health care delivery offers a wide range of services, focusing mostly on health promotion, early diagnosis of disease or disability, and disease prevention.[9](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur29)

Secondary health care delivery includes assessment, diagnosis, treatment, and preventive services that are associated with more complex medical issues. An example of secondary health care delivery would be a referral from a family doctor or nurse practitioner to a medical specialist for a specific medical issue that may require diagnosis and treatment. Tertiary health care delivery is a specialized, highly technical level of health care that takes place in large research and teaching hospitals. Tertiary health care delivery usually takes place within specialized care units such as a critical care unit and requires advanced diagnostic and treatment support services as well as highly specialized personnel. procedures. Because it is so highly specialized, not every health care centre will offer quaternary care.

#### Home Care

Over the years, the Canadian health care system has seen a shift in focus from hospital care to home care. Traditionally, people entered the health care system through hospitals. However, over the past two decades, the role and structure of hospitals have changed dramatically. Operating a hospital requires a tremendous amount of money. Over a third of all health care spending goes into running hospitals. Therefore, in order to cut costs, most provincial and territorial governments have reduced the number of hospitals: hundreds of hospitals have closed, merged, or been converted into other types of health care facilities.

Partly to save money and partly as a result of technological advances, and also to reduce infection rates, clients are now sent home sooner after hospital procedures than they used to be. Each year, fewer clients stay in hospital overnight, and if they do stay overnight, they stay for shorter time periods than they would have in the past. In order to support patients who leave hospitals early, governments have gradually increased spending on home care. Home care is health care and support services provided to people in their places of residence, including private homes, licensed live-in care facilities, and assisted-living facilities (see [Chapter 3](../B9780323709392000031/B9780323709392000031.xhtml)). Home care is the most common of the community-based services, and support workers are needed and are actively recruited to work in all home care areas.

Home care was first created to provide care for people who needed at-home assistance after hospital discharge. Today, home care provides community care and support to a wide range of people. Clients include older persons; families with children; people who are living with mental, physical, or developmental disabilities; people with short-term and long-term medical conditions; and people in the recovery, rehabilitative, or final stages of a life-ending disease. Home care services provide assistance to families who need help with a new baby. They enable people with disabilities to get up in the morning and get ready for school or work. They help people adjust to a disability or recover from an illness ([FIGURE 2.4](#f0025)). They enable people who are dying to remain at home rather than be admitted to a hospital.

One major focus of home care is to enable people to remain in their homes, so they can be as healthy as possible and remain independent for as long as possible. For some people, home care replaces hospital or other facility care. For others, home care allows them to maintain their health and independence, thus delaying or preventing admission to a facility.

##### Home Care Services and Funding

Support workers provide most home care support services. In most provinces and territories, support services are provided by both public and private agencies that can be either for-profit or not-for-profit organizations. Every province and territory has a publicly funded home care program. In addition, Health Canada’s Indigenous Services Canada uses contribution agreements with the Canadian government to provide funding for health programs and services for First Nations people on reserves and Inuit people in the North.[10](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur30)

The funding for the specific type of care that a client receives depends on their province’s funding policies. Because the Canada Health Act does not say what services must be provided, each province and territory has defined and funded its own home care system; therefore, the services offered and how they are provided vary across the country ([BOX 2.5](#b0030)).

All provinces and territories, however, offer the following:

3. • Nursing services

## The Provincial or Territorial Role in Health Care

Each province and territory is responsible for developing and administering its own health care insurance plan. The provincial or territorial government finances and plans its health care services, following the five basic principles outlined in the Canada Health Act. For example, provincial and territorial governments decide where hospitals or long-term care facilities will be located and organized; how many physicians, nurses, and other service providers will be needed; and how much money to spend on health care services. Provincial and territorial health insurance plans ([TABLE 2.1](#t0010)) pay for hospital and physician costs.

BOX 2.5 How Home Care Is Governed and Delivered

How home care is governed and delivered differs across the provinces and territories. In all jurisdictions, the ministries or departments of health and social or community services are responsible for home care services. These departments monitor the services and decide on budgets, policies, and standards of care.

Service delivery involves the following:

1. • Assessing clients’ needs
2. • Determining clients’ eligibility for government-sponsored professional and support services
3. • Coordinating and monitoring home care services provided by private or not-for-profit agencies
4. • Providing information and referrals to other long-term care services (e.g., volunteer-based community services such as Meals on Wheels), some of which charge user fees to the client
5. • Providing placement services to assisted-living facilities and extended care facilities (known in some provinces as long-term care facilities)

### Additional (Supplementary) Services

Provinces and territories provide coverage to certain people (e.g., older persons, children, and recipients of social assistance) for services that are not generally covered under the publicly funded health care system. These supplementary health benefits often include prescription drugs, dental care, vision care, medical equipment and appliances (e.g., prostheses, wheelchairs), independent-living assistance, and the services of other health care providers such as chiropractors. The level of coverage varies across the country.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur25) Additional health insurance, sometimes referred to as benefits, covers services that are not government funded, such as some of the costs of rehabilitation and extended care services.

#### Private Health Insurance

Those who do not qualify for supplementary benefits under government plans pay for these services with individual, out-of-pocket payments or through private health insurance plans. Many Canadians are covered by private health insurance, paid for by their employers or themselves. The level of service provided varies according to the plan purchased. Each company may provide its own unique plan. Exactly what is covered and by how much (e.g., ambulance services, drugs, home care) will differ according to the plan.

To help pay for services not covered by provincial or territorial insurance, people can buy extra health insurance policies. Some private insurance coverage is comprehensive; others cover very few services or only a percentage of the costs of services. Some people do not have any private insurance, so they receive no funding other than what is provided by their province or territory.

### Health Care Trends

To reduce some of the pressures placed on our health care system, new ways of providing care have been introduced to Canadians, with the intent of providing quality care while avoiding needless spending. Instead of Canadians’ relying on hospitals and doctors, there is a trend toward providing alternative care in clinics, in health care centres, and through home care. Many families now seek medical services not only from doctors but also from nurse practitioners. Medical advances have led to more surgical procedures being done through day surgery on an outpatient basis. Some procedures (such as elective cosmetic surgeries) are offered in private clinics. Post-acute services and hospital-alternative services are now more frequently provided in the home and community.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur25)

As a result of health care delivery changes and redistribution of health care funding, the number of acute-care hospital beds has decreased. This decrease, however, has led to hospital overcrowding in some communities where there are not yet sufficient community services.[13](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur33)

Most provinces and territories have tried to control costs and improve delivery by decentralizing decision making on health care delivery to the regional or local board level. Such regional authorities are managed by elected and appointed members who oversee hospitals, long-term care homes, home care, and public health services in their areas. As part of these
reforms, provincial and territorial governments are now focused on two areas:

1. • Health promotion and disease prevention
2. • Home care

#### Alternative Health Practices

Alternative health practices are health treatments that have not been taught or practised in traditional medical communities and offer therapies that differ from standard medical practice (see [Chapters 23](../B9780323709392000237/B9780323709392000237.xhtml), [44](../B9780323709392000444/B9780323709392000444.xhtml), and [45](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml)). They include massage therapies, homeopathy, herbal medicines (including the use of cannabis), and acupuncture. These practices are gaining widespread acceptance by the same medical community (also known as Western medicine) that once disregarded them and are now often ordered by physicians and used in conjunction with traditional treatments. Many of these services are also now covered by some provincial and territorial health care plans.

### Health Promotion and Disease Prevention

Traditionally, the purpose of a health care system has been to diagnose, treat, and cure illnesses. A more recent approach to health care, however, involves developing ways to promote health and prevent diseases from occurring. Preventing illness and injury, while keeping people healthy, is more effective and cheaper than providing treatments in hospitals. Health promotion refers to strategies that improve or maintain health and independence. Disease prevention refers to strategies that prevent the occurrence of disease or injury. Health promotion and disease prevention are now important functions of Canada’s health care system.

Some sectors of government and industry have policies to promote health and prevent illness by improving the quality of people’s lives. Examples of such policies include the following:

1. • Immunization programs
2. • Prenatal and parenting classes
3. • Information campaigns to reduce drinking during pregnancy, unsafe sex, and tobacco use and to encourage healthy eating and physical activity
4. • Efforts to improve housing, decrease poverty, monitor drinking water for safety, and protect the environment

Support workers contribute to health promotion and disease prevention and are needed now more than ever because they provide nonmedical care and services that can help prevent major health problems. Take, for example, Mr. Lukovic, who has been on bedrest for a long time. He is at risk for pressure ulcers, pneumonia, and blood clots. To prevent these complications, you, as his support worker, help him keep his skin clean and dry, change his position in bed frequently, and help him perform range-of-motion exercises. By doing these important things for Mr. Lukovic, you can help improve his quality of life now and prevent him from developing illness or disability in the future.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Canada’s publicly funded health care system is best described as an interlocking set of 10 provincial and 3 territorial health insurance plans.

• Medicare provides access to universal, comprehensive coverage for medically necessary hospital and physician services. These services are administered and delivered by the provincial and territorial governments and are provided at no additional cost to the client.

• To receive their full share of federal funding for health care, the provincial and territorial health insurance plans must meet the five criteria of the Canada Health Act—comprehensiveness, universality, portability, accessibility, and public administration.

• The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada: Call to Action (2015) document identified seven Calls to Action for health care that are necessary to redress the legacy of residential schools for Indigenous Canadians.

• Health care delivery is divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary delivery categories. Primary care is aimed at preventing illness whenever possible. Tertiary health care delivery is the most expensive to deliver.

• Many factors challenge and stress the Canadian health care system. These factors include (1) severe shortages of physicians, nurses, and other health care workers in rural areas; (2) the aging of the baby boomer generation; (3) long waiting lists; and (4) the steadily rising cost of care and new technology, which is the greatest challenge.

• Supplementary health benefits often include prescription drugs, dental care, vision care, medical equipment and appliances, independent-living assistance, and the services of other health care providers, such as chiropractors. The level of coverage varies across the country.

• Residents may have private insurance plans that pay for these supplementary services.

• New trends in health care include (1) alternative care in clinics, (2) health care centres, and (3) home care programs.

• To control costs, provincial and territorial governments are focusing on (1) health promotion and disease prevention and (2) home care.

• Support workers provide most home care support services.

• In most provinces and territories, support services are provided by for-profit and not-for-profit public and private agencies. Every province and territory has a publicly funded home care program, but the funding per client will vary depending on the province’s funding policies.

• Home care services are classified by (1) personal care services, (2) home support services, (3) nursing and professional services, and (4) support for IADLs.

This chapter describes common community-based and facility-based workplace settings for support workers. It also explores benefits and challenges you may encounter in these settings. Wherever you work, you provide people with vital services that enable them to be as safe, comfortable, dignified, and independent as possible.
In any work environment, you should be familiar with the scope of practice for support workers in your province, your municipality, or your agency, and you should always stay within this scope. Agreeing to perform tasks that are beyond your support worker role—such as performing delegated tasks without proper instruction and supervision—can possibly risk legal action against you or your employer.

## Workplace Settings and Services Provided

Support workers provide care in many settings. Each setting has different goals and services. Each employer should have a specific and formalized job description for its support workers, and any task the support workers do should fall within this job description.

1. • Acute care—health care that is provided for a relatively short time (usually days to weeks) and is intended to diagnose and treat an immediate health issue. It is provided mainly in hospitals. An acute illness appears suddenly and lasts a short time, usually less than 3 months. Symptoms can be severe. Examples of acute illnesses are pneumonia and influenza.
2. • Subacute care (also known as convalescent care or complex continuing care)—health care or rehabilitation for people recovering from surgery or injury or being stabilized after a serious illness or health challenge. A client in subacute care is in stable condition but still needs care requiring complex equipment and procedures. Many hospitals provide subacute care. After a hip replacement, for example, a client may not be ready to go home because they need frequent physiotherapy and dressing changes. Some hospitals have wards that are dedicated to providing subacute care. Eventually, the client is discharged home or to another level of care.
3. • Long-term care—health and support services provided over the course of months or years to people who cannot care for themselves. Many people who require long-term care are living with chronic illnesses. A chronic illness (also known as persistent illness) is an ongoing illness, slow or gradual in onset, that may or may not grow worse over time. Examples of chronic illnesses are diabetes, multiple sclerosis, and Alzheimer’s disease. Because chronic illnesses cannot be cured, the focus of care is on preventing complications of the illness. These illnesses can sometimes be managed, and complications prevented. In some cases, long-term care is provided for the remainder of the client’s life. The goal of long-term care is to help the resident cope with the challenges of living with a long-term illness or disability while maintaining a good quality of life. Some hospitals provide long-term care, but more often, long-term care is provided in a live-in care setting, such as a long-term care facility, or through home care services.
4. • Respite care—temporary care of a person who requires a high level of support, care, and supervision. Respite care gives the person’s caregivers a break from their duties. Respite care is often provided by support workers in the client’s home. However, many hospitals and other facilities also offer respite care.
5. • Rehabilitation services and
   restorative care
   —therapies and educational programs designed to restore or improve the client’s independence and functional abilities. These services are for people who are or have been ill, injured, or disabled. Hospitals, live-in facilities, and clinics offer rehabilitation services. Services may include life skills training, occupational therapy and rehabilitation services, behavioural management, speech therapy, physiotherapy, job coaching, and family counselling. Support workers may assist the client with personal care or activities of daily living (ADLs) or assist with program delivery.
6. • Indigenous or First Nations services—services aimed at supporting and empowering Indigenous peoples to independently improve access to and deliver services, and address the socioeconomic conditions in their communities.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur41) These services are used throughout Canada, and especially in rural and northern communities. Support workers may be employed by a First Nation (self-governing or otherwise) to provide care to their Indigenous
   clients. The job descriptions for support workers may vary between employers. Some employers expect support workers to provide mainly personal care, while other employer expectations include home management as well as personal care.
7. • Palliative care—services for clients (and their families) living with an illness that cannot be cured, with a goal of improving the client’s quality of life, by managing pain and other distressing symptoms. Palliative care is an approach to care that emphasizes client goals, relief of pain and suffering, and quality of life.
8. • End-of-life care—the physical, emotional, social, and spiritual care and support given to people who have stopped treatment to cure or control their disease as well as support to their families. End-of-life care may include palliative care, supportive care, and hospice care (below), which often work together to help the client and the family during the client’s journey near their end of life. End-of-life care is provided through an interdisciplinary approach.
9. • Hospice—an element of palliative care that provides home, live-in, or inpatient care to a client who has a terminal diagnosis and is no longer seeking life-prolonging care. The philosophy of hospice is to provide support for the client’s emotional, social, and spiritual needs, as well as medical needs, as part of treating the whole person. Hospice workers try to make the client’s last days as painless, comfortable, and dignified as possible. Support workers assist with personal care and ADLs, as well as provide emotional support and encouragement to the client and family (see [Chapter 47](../B978032370939200047X/B978032370939200047X.xhtml)).
10. • Mental health care services (also known as psychiatric care services)—services for people with mental health disorders (such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and addictions). Entire facilities, health care centres, and hospital units are devoted to caring for people with mental health disorders and offer both inpatient and outpatient services. Clients are usually encouraged to return to the community rather than stay in a hospital. Assessment and treatment programs enable these clients to function as independently as possible within the community, where they have access to community-based care and support services.

## Working in Community-Based Settings

Community-based settings are places within the community that provide health care and support services. Examples of community-based settings include clients’ homes, group homes, and retirement homes in which clients live as independently as possible. The most common community setting is the client’s home ([FIGURE 3.1](#f0010)). Support workers sometimes assist their clients with social integration, and they may teach their clients important skills such as doing laundry, shopping for groceries, managing their money, doing their own banking, taking a bus, or applying for a job. In many instances, if a client must receive around-the-clock or respite care, or when family members are unable to be there with clients, support workers are hired to provide care to clients on evening, night, or weekend shifts.

As discussed in [Chapter 2](../B978032370939200002X/B978032370939200002X.xhtml), the current trend within the Canadian health care system is to decrease hospital costs and to increase resources offered through community-based services. Community-based services include health care and support services provided in community settings, such as schools, community health centres, and doctors’ offices. As part of this trend, the health ministries of all the provinces and territories have initiated more community-based programs to support clients
in living longer and avoiding being hospitalized. The increase in home-based and community-based programs has created an ever-growing demand for support workers; in fact, community-based service providers are hiring more support workers than ever before. Home care agencies, live-in facilities, group homes and retirement residences, day programs, and school boards are all community-based services that hire support workers.

### Home Care

Home care is a vital part of Canada’s health care system (see [Chapter 2](../B978032370939200002X/B978032370939200002X.xhtml)). Support workers have a central role within home care. It has been estimated that support workers are responsible for 80% of the total hours worked by all home care workers.[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur43) Support workers are responsible for providing a range of home care services, including assisting with personal care ([FIGURE 3.2](#f0015)), ADLs, child care, transportation, and home management.

Support workers providing home care services are hired on a full-time, part-time, or casual basis and must follow their agency’s policies and procedures. Some agencies offer further education, and many offer in-servicing, to keep staff up to date with new procedures. ## Working in Facility-Based Settings

Facility-based settings are workplaces that provide clients with accommodations, health care, and support services. Several types of facilities, including hospitals and long-term care facilities, employ support workers. A hospital is a facility for clients with acute illnesses or injuries who require admission and care on a relatively short-term basis. Some clients require admission for procedures or rehabilitation on an outpatient basis only; these clients would generally not be required to stay overnight for care and observation. A chronic care, continuing care, or long-term care facility ([FIGURE 3.3](#f0020)) is home to people who are not able to live independently or in their own homes and who require the availability of 24-hour nursing services to meet their personal care needs but do not need hospital care. In some provinces, long-term care facilities are called complex care facilities or nursing homes. Support workers also may be employed in mental health facilities, which admit and treat clients with acute or long-term mental health disorders.

#### Group Homes

Group homes also offer assisted-living services. A group home is a live-in facility in which a small number of people with physical or mental disabilities live together and receive supervision, care, and support services. Rather than having their own apartments, residents share a house in a live-in neighbourhood ([FIGURE 3.5](#f0030)). Usually, residents have their own bedrooms but share bathrooms and living and dining areas. They receive 24-hour supervision, meals, housekeeping and laundry services, and assistance with personal care and ADLs.

Residents of group homes are often adolescents or young adults living with disabilities, behavioural or conduct disorders, or mental impairment; older persons needing care; women leaving abusive situations; or people with substance abuse issues. #### Retirement Residences

A retirement residence (or retirement home) is a facility that provides accommodation and supervision for older persons. Residents have their own bedrooms and bathrooms but share common living and dining areas ([FIGURE 3.6](#f0035)). They may need help with housekeeping but limited supervision and little to no assistance with personal care. The goal of a retirement residence is to allow older people to live as independently as possible while providing security, support services, and varying degrees of care, as needed.

Regulations governing retirement residences vary. In some provinces and territories, they are usually privately operated and not financed by the government. In this case, residents are required to pay the full cost. Standards, prices, and services differ from one facility to another. These facilities may be small, converted houses or high-rise apartment buildings.

Support workers are almost always hired directly by the facility or the resident. Support workers hired by a resident provide care for only that person. They perform functions such as running errands, providing transportation, assisting with activities or social and recreational events, and helping with tasks such as unpacking suitcases or arranging bedrooms. In some provinces, support workers may even be required to assist the residents with taking their medications, including insulin injections.

Residents in retirement homes do not have illnesses or disabilities that prevent them from meeting their own personal care needs, so personal care services are limited to a few simple tasks. For example, a support worker may help a client get in and out of a bathtub. Residents needing more than minimal daily care have to move to a long-term care facility. Some facilities house both a retirement residence and a long-term care facility; residents, therefore, do not have far to move once they need full-time access to nursing and personal care services.

### Community Day Programs

An adult community day program (also called adult day centre or adult day program) is a daytime program for people living with physical or mental health issues or for older persons who need assistance. Day programs aim to meet the client’s needs and provide a break for family caregivers. These programs may be held in hospitals, nursing homes, community and recreational centres, adult day centres, church basements, or other settings. In some provinces, support workers are hired by school boards to assist with clients who are attending school instead of a day program.

Each day program is unique. Some programs offer rehabilitation to people with disabilities. Others offer counselling to people with mental impairment. Many day programs offer recreational activities ([FIGURE 3.7](#f0040)), such as arts and crafts, social events, films, and board or card games.

Some provinces hire support workers to work in community day programs, whereas other provinces do not. Support workers in a community day program provide personal care and assistance to people attending the program and may assist with hosting the recreational and social activities. As always, if you work with a community day program, make sure that you follow all employer policies and procedures. [BOX 3.2](#b0015) lists common benefits and challenges associated with working in a community day program.

BOX 3.2 Benefits and Challenges Associated With Working in a Community Day Program

1. •
   Working closely with a team and a supervisor.
    In most community day programs, support workers work closely with a supervisor and other team members. Teamwork can be a success if team members share a common goal and work well together. It can be difficult, though, if conflicts occur within the team. Effective communication skills are necessary (see [Chapters 9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)and [10](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)).
2. •
   Working in a structured environment.
    Many day programs have highly structured environments, particularly programs that provide rehabilitation. People living with conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease usually benefit from a predictable routine. Many support workers enjoy working in a structured environment, as it develops strong organizational skills and sensitivity to clients’ needs.
3. •
   Meeting multiple needs. Support workers may have to attend to the needs of many people. They must be able to focus on each person and quickly decide whose needs to address first. Good judgement and time management are essential.

### Working Directly for Clients

A support worker may be hired by, supervised by, and work directly for a client or the client’s family. Clients may hire their own support workers if they need a service that is not provided by local agencies or if their province provides funding assistance directly to them rather than to an agency for their care. [BOX 3.3](#b0020) describes benefits and challenges associated with working directly for clients.

## Working in a Facility

A health care facility is a building designed or established for the delivery of specific care, treatment, and support services. Such facilities provide a range of services.

### Hospitals and Other Facility-Based Care

Clients in hospitals usually have acute, often serious illnesses or injuries that require treatment in a timely manner, skilled professional care, and complex equipment. Not all hospitals hire support workers, but in those that do, support workers usually report to and are supervised by a nurse.

Depending on the region and the hospital’s hiring policies, support workers may be employed in any unit in a hospital, including the critical care unit (CCU) and the emergency department (ED), pediatrics, hemodialysis, and maternity units. Their role may be to provide basic care such as feeding, transporting people, taking specimens to the lab, or measuring vital signs. In some hospitals, they may assist nurses before, during, or after surgical or medical procedures. Support workers may perform other tasks, if requested and supervised by the nurse. They usually do not provide care to clients in unstable conditions but may assist the nurse in moving, turning, or bathing a client in such areas as the CCU or the ED.

Health care services are offered both to inpatients (patients who are assigned a bed and admitted to stay in the facility overnight or longer) and to outpatients (patients who do not stay overnight in the facility).

Hospitals provide a variety of services, including acute care, subacute care, complex continuing care, respite care, rehabilitation services, palliative care, and mental health services. Not all hospitals provide all these services. In some cities and towns in Canada, these services are accessed through separate, specialized facilities. You may work in a hospital or facility that offers specialized care. Some of these facilities may require, and also provide, further education.

### Long-Term Care Facilities

Long-term care facilities (also called nursing homes, homes for the aged, long-term care homes, and special care homes) offer higher levels of care than do retirement residences and assisted-living facilities. They provide accommodations, 24-hour professional nursing care, and support services to clients who cannot care for themselves at home but do not need hospital care. Most residents are frail older persons with many health issues. Some residents, though, are young or middle-aged adults who are living with severe,
chronic health conditions or disabilities. The goals of these facilities are to maintain the residents’ health and independence to the greatest extent possible and to meet their physical, emotional, social, intellectual, and spiritual needs.

BOX 3.3 Benefits and Challenges Associated With Working Directly for Clients

1. •
   Clarifying the terms of employment.
    Some clients who employ a support worker may want to have a contract signed. Always read the contract carefully before signing it. Hours and pay for individual clients may change from week to week. Make sure you understand how many hours you are expected to work and what pay you can expect. If you are hired directly, your employer may be required to pay benefits, such as unemployment insurance.
2. •
   Establishing work limits.
    Before you begin working for the client, find out what exactly is expected of you and how your performance will be evaluated. Ask for this information in writing. Find out as much as you can about the client’s preferences and standards.
3. •
   Knowing scope-of-practice limits and your role and responsibilities. A private employer may ask you to do something that is beyond what you were educated for or is not identified by the support worker educational standards that your province or territory follows (see [Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml)). In this event, you may feel uneasy but may not know who to contact to confirm what you are allowed to do. It is therefore important that you inform your private employer of the scope of your role and that you know someone (a former teacher or employer, for example) who can answer your questions if such a situation ever arises.
4. •
   Dealing with people in distress.
    As in all other settings, support workers must support clients and their families who show signs of intense emotional or physical distress. They may be in pain, afraid, upset, or angry and not cooperate with their care providers. You need to remain calm and professional, no matter how clients express themselves. Also, be sensitive to their feelings and try to imagine how they are feeling. Sometimes you can provide emotional support just by holding the client’s hand or by listening.
5. •
   Needing to carry liability insurance.
    When you are self-employed, you are legally responsible for any injury or workplace mishap that might occur while you are providing care. In addition, without health insurance, you would not be compensated for lost wages if you were to become ill and miss time from work.

Residents stay on a ward or in private or semi-private bedrooms. Usually, each room has a bathroom with a toilet and a sink. Tubs and showers are located in the common bathrooms. Besides nursing care, these facilities offer access to medical and rehabilitative care. They also provide assistance with personal care and ADLs, meals, laundry service, and recreational and social activities.

Long-term care facilities are licensed, regulated, and funded by the province or territory in which they are located. Medicare covers some costs, with the balance paid through a monthly fee by residents. Residents also must pay for personal clothing, toiletries, hairdresser services, special nail care services, and other incidentals. Government or charitable organizations operate some facilities on a not-for-profit basis. Private facilities operate on a for-profit basis. Each facility hires its own staff.

Most long-term care facilities serve many residents living with various physical or other disabilities. Therefore, to function efficiently, these facilities maintain highly structured work environments. Support workers are members of the health care team in a long-term facility and report to a nurse. Nurses plan and coordinate resident care. Support workers provide personal care and assist clients with various ADLs ([FIGURE 3.8](#f0045)). They make important contributions to the clients’ care plans, and during discussions in family conferences, they provide valuable feedback based on their observations of the client.

Some long-term care facilities have subacute care units where a client may be admitted to recover after surgery if not yet able to go home.
Some facilities also have special care units for residents with specific disabilities. For example, a facility may have a dementia care unit for people living with Alzheimer’s disease or other dementias. Respite care and palliative care units are also part of some long-term care facilities. Support workers may work in any of these units but may require extra training to do so.

BOX 3.4 Benefits and Challenges Associated With Working in a Facility

1. •
   Working in a structured team environment.
    Support workers work on a team with highly skilled professionals. As part of that team, you are a valuable team member and have much to contribute at team meetings. In live-in facilities, support workers usually spend more time with the clients than the nurses and physicians do. You have valuable insights and observations about the client’s daily needs and possible changes in health.
2. •
   Meeting multiple needs and demands.
    Support workers are required to respond to the many needs and demands of clients. It may not be possible to respond immediately to all demands, so, as a support worker, you should be able to prioritize clients’ needs and manage your time. You must be flexible, diplomatic, and consistent. Effective organizational and communication skills are essential.
3. •
   Dealing with people in distress.
    Clients admitted to facilities and clients’ families may show signs of intense emotional or physical distress. Clients may be in pain, afraid, upset, or angry and not cooperate with their care providers. It is important that you remain calm and professional, no matter how these clients express themselves. Also, be sensitive to their feelings and try to imagine how they are feeling. As in a home setting, when palliative care is required, you need to be strong and supportive in the presence of intense suffering and emotions.
4. •
   Doing many tasks within a short period. Support workers must provide thorough, competent, and respectful care within a short time. This ability requires self-discipline, dedication, and efficiency.
5. •
   Respecting your role and scope of practice.
    Support workers work closely with nurses and may become familiar with many nursing procedures. You might sometimes be asked to perform a procedure that is beyond your scope of practice or a procedure that should be delegated to you ([Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml)). Never attempt any procedure that you have not been taught to perform, or if you are uncertain about how to perform it safely. Perform only those procedures that you are permitted to do according to your facility’s policy. Also, never perform a procedure unless your supervisor is allowed to train you on that procedure and has given you sufficient training so that you are comfortable doing it. The facility will have written policies to guide you.
7. •
   Working in shifts.
    Most facilities are staffed through the entire day. You may have to work evening and night shifts, and your shifts may be 8 to 12 hours long, depending on the agency. Many support workers love the variety of working different shifts, whereas some have difficulty adjusting to the changes in sleep and lifestyle habits demanded by shift work. Those who have families may enjoy having a schedule that permits them time to participate in their children’s activities.

Especially in Live-in Facilities

1. •
   Making the facility feel like a home.
    A live-in facility is, primarily, the resident’s home. Treat the setting with as much respect as you would your own home. Be careful with the client’s personal possessions. Make all areas of the facility cheerful, comfortable places. Every staff member must contribute to creating a positive, homelike environment.
2. •
   Respecting the client’s privacy and dignity.
    In any work setting, support workers must respect their clients’ privacy and dignity. Lack of privacy can result in loss of self-esteem, particularly during personal care. Remember to knock on the door and announce your presence before entering a client’s room. Carefully screen off the area, and cover the client. This step may seem obvious, but sometimes it is easy to focus more on getting the job done than on respecting the client’s need for privacy. As in other care situations, respecting privacy also includes keeping discussions with co-workers about the client confidential and professional, respecting the client’s property, and recognizing the client’s right to express their preferences.
3. •
   Maintaining professional boundaries.
    Support workers may provide care to residents who have no close personal relationships. Working closely with clients may foster strong attachments. This aspect of the job may be one of the main attractions of support work. However, do not become too personally involved with clients and their families. Always be caring and supportive, but remember that your main responsibility is providing care and maintaining a professional attitude.

 Case Study

Choosing a Place to Work

Therese LeCroix is a new support worker who has just graduated from a reputable support worker program. She was a highly motivated and interested student who enjoyed all of the placements that she was given during her support worker program. She is now looking for a permanent position but does not know where to begin. She has seen ads listed online for jobs available in her town for support workers.

Therese has decided to ask her classmates where they are going to apply for jobs. She is amazed that many of them are fairly certain of what type of support work they prefer. Some love providing home care, and some others are interested in working in long-term care facilities. Other classmates have decided to apply for positions at the local hospitals that hire support workers. Finally, many of Therese’s classmates say that they would love working in the community for agencies that offer day programs for special groups of clients.

Therese finally begins to understand why it is so difficult for her to decide: There are many opportunities for support workers in a variety of settings. She then decides that she should keep an open mind and apply to different agencies and settings that might best work for her, based on her family commitments, interests, and location in the city. Therese realizes that she is lucky to be in a line of work that offers such a variety of opportunities and in which the workers are so much in demand!
