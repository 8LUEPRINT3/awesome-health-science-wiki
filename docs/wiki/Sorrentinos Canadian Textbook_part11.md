A growth spurt occurs during adolescence. Boys grow about 10 to 41 cm (4 to 16 in.) and gain 7 to 27 kg (15 to 60 lb.) in weight. They usually stop growing between the ages of 18 and 21 years. Some continue to grow until about age 25 years. Girls grow
about 5 to 23 cm (2 to 9 in.) and gain between 7 and 23 kg (15 and 50 lb.) in weight. They usually stop growing between the ages of 17 and 18 years, but some girls continue to grow until about age 21 years.

BOX 31.1 Some Facts About Adolescent Sexual Orientation

1. • Sexual orientation refers to whether a person’s physical and emotional arousal is in response to people of the same or opposite sex, or both.
2. • One does not have to be sexually active to have a sexual orientation.
3. • Teens who will eventually identify as gay, lesbian, or bisexual do not always do so during their adolescence.
4. • Having had sex with someone of the same gender does not always mean the teen is gay, and many gay teens have never had sex with someone of the same gender.
5. • The process of declaring a non-heterosexual sexual identity is often referred to as “coming out.”
6. • “Coming out” can start with gay or lesbian fantasies or dreams, with the realization that one is attracted to people of the same gender, with a feeling that one is different from one’s peers, or even with a sexual experience.
7. • Identity confusion about sexual orientation is influenced by the stigma attached to gay, lesbian, or bisexual sexuality, inaccurate knowledge, lack of role models, and minimal opportunity to socialize with other youth who are having similar feelings.
8. • Some teens experience both same-sex and opposite-sex attractions.
9. • It can be a challenge for gay and lesbian youth to develop a healthy and integrated identity in the context of negative stereotypes and prejudice, and often without family or societal support.
10. • Gay and lesbian adults often describe their adolescence as a time when they felt isolated, ashamed, and afraid of being discovered that they were different—all of which can have an impact on self-esteem and identity formation. This isolation can lead to thoughts of suicide.
11. • Studies have shown that almost one-half of gay men and one-fifth of lesbians were verbally or physically assaulted in high school because of their orientation (or perceived orientation).
12. • Gay teens can also be harassed at home, and are more likely to be kicked out of their homes and live on the streets than their heterosexual peers.
13. • Gay sexuality may be more stigmatized within some ethnic communities.
14. • High school students who say they are gay, lesbian, or bisexual, or who say they are attracted to people of the same sex or have sex with people of the same sex, are two to seven times more likely to attempt suicide.

Adolescence is described as the “awkward stage.” Awkwardness and clumsiness are caused by the uneven growth of muscles and bones. Coordination and graceful body movements eventually develop as muscle growth and bone growth even out. Gay, lesbian, bisexual, and transgender youth have the same diversity of health care needs as heterosexual adolescents, including chronic illness, disabilities, sports injuries, and even contraception.[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur303)

Children at this stage often find changes in physical appearance hard to accept. Some girls are embarrassed about breast development, especially about very large or small breast size, and some boys may be concerned about genital size. Height may cause concern in both boys and girls—boys do not like being much shorter than their peers, and tall girls may feel embarrassed about being taller than other girls and boys. Many adolescents, especially girls, worry about their weight, and thinking that they weigh too much, some try to diet and may develop eating disorders.

Teenagers often feel intense emotions, and their emotional conditions vary from high to low. Adolescents can be happy one moment and sad the next, so predicting their reaction to a comment or event can be difficult. Teenagers can control their emotions better later in this stage. Older adolescents (15- to 18-year-olds) can still become sad and depressed, but they can better control the time and place of their emotional reactions.

Adolescents need to become independent of adults, especially their parents. They must learn to function, make decisions, and act in a responsible manner without adult supervision. Many teenagers work toward developing this independence by looking for part-time jobs, babysitting, going to dances and parties, dating, taking part in school clubs and organizations, shopping without an adult, and staying home alone ([FIGURE 31.10](#f0055)).

Adolescents become increasingly able to use logic and reasoning, and they often form passionate opinions on many issues. However, their judgement and reasoning are not always sound. Guidance, discipline, and emotional and financial support from parents are still needed. Disagreements with parents are common, especially about behaviour and activity restrictions and limits. Adolescents would rather be with peers than do things with parents and other family members. Similarly, they tend to confide in and seek advice from peers or from adults other than their parents.

Teenage interests and activities reflect the need to become independent and to develop intimate relationships. All genders are often interested in parties, dances, and other social activities. Clothing, makeup, and hairstyles are often important to teenagers of all genders because their appearance enables them to define their identity and fit in with a group. Parents and teenagers often disagree about clothing and hairstyles. Teenagers may spend a lot of time with friends or converse with them through email, text messaging, or social media or on the phone. They also spend time participating in sports or in club activities, listening to music, or playing video games. Dating begins during adolescence, although the age of its onset varies from individual to individual.

Difficult decisions and conflicts result as the adolescent matures physically, psychologically, and emotionally. Parents and teenagers often disagree about dating because parents worry that dating may lead to sexual activities, pregnancy, and sexually transmitted infections. Teenagers usually do not understand or appreciate these concerns. Dating one person exclusively helps the teenager meet the needs for security, love and belonging, self-esteem, and, for some, gender identification or exploration (see [BOX 31.1](#b0010)). Teenagers sometimes have difficulty controlling sexual urges and considering the consequences of sexual activity (including “cyber-dating,” in which they send pictures of themselves to other people). Unfortunately, when images such as these are sent through an electronic communication network, there is a high likelihood for those images to be misused (see [Chapter 12](../B9780323709392000122/B9780323709392000122.xhtml)).

Adolescents begin to think about careers and what to do after high school graduation. Interests, skills, and talents are some of the factors that influence the choice of further education or a job. Adolescents also need to develop morals, values, and attitudes for living in society. They need to develop a sense about what is good and bad, right and wrong, and important and unimportant. Parents, peers, culture, religion, television, school, and movies are some of the factors that influence teenagers. Drug abuse, unwanted pregnancy, alcoholism, suicidal ideation, and criminal acts (see [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml)) are common difficulties among adolescents who are troubled.

### Young Adulthood (18 to 40 Years)

Psychological and social development continues during young adulthood. There is little physical growth because adult height has already been reached by this time, and body systems are fully developed. Developmental tasks of young adulthood include the following:

1. • Choosing further education and a career
2. • Selecting a partner
3. • Learning to live with a partner
4. • Becoming a parent and raising children
5. • Developing a satisfactory sex life

Education and occupation are so closely related that they can rarely be separated. Most jobs require specific knowledge and skills, so the amount and kind of education needed depend on a person’s career choice. Most adults find that their job choices are greater with adequate educational preparation and that employment is necessary for economic independence and for supporting a family.

At this stage of development, young adults may choose to marry their partner, remain single, or live in a common-law relationship. Some of the reasons for marriage or common-law relationship are being in love, wanting emotional security, wanting a family, having a sexual relationship, wanting to leave an unhappy home life, looking for a certain social status, wanting companionship, and sharing financial security. Some marry to feel wanted, needed, and desirable. Factors such as age, religion, interests, education, race, personality, and love affect the selection of a spouse. Some marriages are happy and successful, whereas some others are not. There are no guarantees that a relationship will work, so the couple must work together to build a marriage or partnership based on trust, respect, caring, and friendship.

Partners (married or unmarried) must learn to live with each other, which may require habits, routines, household management, meal preparation, and leisure activities to be changed or adjusted to “fit” each other’s needs. They must learn to solve problems, make decisions together, and work toward the same goals. Open and honest communication is essential to a successful partnership ([FIGURE 31.11](#f0060)).

Young adults also need to develop a satisfactory sex life. Frequency of sex, desires, practices, and preferences vary among couples. Understanding and accepting a partner’s needs are necessary for a satisfying and intimate relationship.

Statistically, most heterosexual couples decide to have children, and modern birth control methods allow them to plan how many children they want to have and when to have them. However, some pregnancies are unplanned. Many couples have a child during the first few years of marriage or partnership; some wait several years before starting a family. Some couples decide not to have children at all. Today, couples of any gender who wish to have children but may be unable to become pregnant or who experience difficulty having children may choose to adopt a child, have in vitro (medically assisted) fertilization (sometimes with donor sperm), or arrange to have a surrogate carry the child. The availability of surrogacy and evolving adoption laws have enabled couples to choose the option that best fits their family. In these cases, there may be costly legal costs involved. Those couples deciding to have children must agree on methods of child-rearing and discipline and must adjust to their roles in raising children and to the children’s needs for time, energy, and parental attention.

### Middle Adulthood (40 to 65 Years)

For some adults, middle adulthood is a stable and comfortable stage of development, a time when children have grown up and moved away and partners are able to spend time alone with each other, enjoy financial security, and have fewer worries about children. However, some adults in this stage have to deal with divorce, ailing parents, children moving back home or not moving out at all, and caring for grandchildren. The developmental tasks of middle adulthood include the following:

1. • Adjusting to physical changes
2. • Having grown-up children
3. • Developing leisure activities
4. • Relating to and perhaps eventually caring for aging parents

Of the physical changes that occur in this period, several are gradual and go unnoticed, whereas some others become obvious early in this stage (see

[Chapter 43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml)). People in their early 40s may feel energetic and able to function as they did in their 20s. However, energy and endurance begin to decrease. People in this age group can develop any number of diseases and illnesses, which may be chronic or life-threatening. Weight control becomes a problem as metabolism and physical activities slow down. Facial wrinkles and grey hair appear (although grey hair can also naturally occur in people in their 20s). The need for eyeglasses is common at this stage, and there may be hearing impairment or deterioration. In individuals with female genitalia, menstruation stops between the ages of 42 and 55, as menopause sets in. The ovaries stop producing eggs and secreting hormones, meaning the individual can no longer become pregnant.

Children leave home to pursue careers and higher education, marry, move to homes of their own, and start their own families. Middle-aged adults have to cope with letting children go, being in-laws, and becoming grandparents. Parents must let their adult children lead their own lives while also remaining available to their children for emotional support in times of need.

Middle-aged adults often discover that they have a lot more spare time once the demands of parenthood have decreased significantly. Hobbies and leisure activities such as gardening, fishing, painting, golfing, volunteer work, and membership in clubs and organizations can be sources of pleasure ([FIGURE 31.12](#f0065)). These activities become even more important after retirement and during late adulthood.

Some middle-aged adults have parents who are very old and experiencing poor health; they take on the responsibility for their parents’ care and often have to deal with the death of parents.

### Late Adulthood (65 Years and Older)

Certain physical changes are a normal part of aging and occur in everyone in late adulthood (see [BOX 43.1](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml) on page 1026). The rate and degree of change vary with each person. Some of these changes are a result of lifestyle choices, including diet, general health, exercise, stress, environment, and heredity. Normal aging does not mean loss of health, so quality of life does not have to change with aging.

The developmental tasks of this stage are as follows:

1. • Adjusting to decreased physical strength and loss of health
2. • Adjusting to retirement and reduced income
3. • Coping with the death of one’s partner
4. • Developing new friends and relationships
5. • Preparing for one’s own death

See [Chapter 43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml) for a detailed discussion of the numerous physical, psychological, and social changes that occur during late adulthood, as well as the specific care that older persons may need.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Growth and development are ongoing from the moment of fertilization until death. They occur in a sequence, order, and pattern. Certain developmental tasks must be completed during each stage, and no stage can be skipped, as each stage lays the foundation for the next stage.

• Psychosocial health is well-being in the social, emotional, intellectual, and spiritual dimensions of one’s life.

• Factors that influence psychosocial health include personality, family background, environment, and life’s circumstances.

• Two common theories that address a client’s psychosocial health are Erikson’s theory of psychosocial development and Maslow’s hierarchy of needs.

• Infant stage (birth to 1 year): Rapid physical, psychological, and social growth and development occur. Birth weight usually doubles by the age of 5 to 6 months and triples by the first birthday. Newborns have certain reflexes (Moro, rooting, sucking, and grasping) that are present at birth and disappear as the central nervous system develops. Developmental tasks for infancy are learning to walk, talk, and communicate with others.

• Toddlers (1 to 3 years): Physical growth during the second year of life is not as rapid as during infancy. Developmental tasks for toddlers are tolerating separation from the primary caregiver, gaining control of bowel and bladder functions, using words to communicate with others, and becoming less dependent on the primary caregiver.

• Preschool (3 to 5 years): Children grow taller but gain little weight. They are more coordinated than toddlers. Developmental tasks for toddlers are improving the ability to communicate with and understand others, performing self-care activities, learning about the differences between the genders and identifying with a gender, distinguishing right from wrong, learning to play with others, and developing family relationships.

• Middle childhood (6 to 8 years): Children usually grow 5 cm each year during this stage; weight gain will vary, depending on such factors as level of activity. Developmental tasks include acquiring the social and physical skills needed for physical games; developing a conscience and morals; having a good feeling and attitude about self; developing the ability to get along with other children; learning behaviours and attitudes considered normative to one’s gender; and acquiring basic reading, writing, and arithmetic skills.

• Late childhood (9 to 12 years): Boys grow about 2.5 cm and gain 1.8 kg of weight per year. Girls grow about 5 cm and gain 2.3 kg of weight per year. Body changes begin to occur. Developmental tasks are developing moral and ethical behaviour; developing and keeping friendships; becoming independent of adults and learning to depend on oneself; understanding the physical, psychological, and social roles of one’s identified gender; learning how to study; and developing greater muscular strength, coordination, and balance.

• Adolescence (12 to 18 years): Rapid growth and psychological and social maturing occur. Boys grow about 10 to 41 cm and gain 7 to 27 kg in weight. Girls grow about 5 to 23 cm and gain 7 to 23 kg in weight. Developmental tasks are accepting changes in one’s body and appearance; developing relationships with others of the same age; accepting the role considered normative for one’s age and identified gender; becoming independent from parents and adults; and developing morals, attitudes, and values needed for functioning in society.

• Young adulthood (18 to 40 years): Adult height has been reached, and body systems are fully developed. Developmental tasks include choosing further education and a career; selecting a partner; becoming a parent and raising children; and developing a satisfactory sex life. Individuals will make choices depending on their goals.

• Middle adulthood (40 to 65 years): Of the physical changes that occur, some are gradual and go unnoticed at first, whereas others may be more obvious—for example, grey hair, wrinkles, and a decrease in energy. Developmental tasks include adjusting to physical changes, having grown-up children, developing leisure activities, and relating to aging parents.

• Late adulthood (65 years and older): Developmental tasks are adjusting to decreased physical strength and loss of health, adjusting to retirement and reduced income, coping with the death of one’s partner, developing new friends and relationships, and preparing for one’s own death.

## Cells, Tissues, Organs, Body Cavities, and Organ Systems

### Cells

The basic functional unit of body structure is the cell. Each cell has the same basic structure, but the function, size, and shape of cells may be different. Cells are so small that you need a microscope to see them. Cells need nutrients, water, and oxygen to live and to perform their functions.

The solution of 0.09% NaCl (sodium chloride or salt) and water which makes up all the entire body’s cells and bodily fluids is an isotonic solution and is compatible with all cells and their surrounding bodily fluids (see [Chapter 28](../B9780323709392000286/B9780323709392000286.xhtml)).

The cell and its basic structures are shown in [FIGURE 32.2](#f0015). The cell membrane is the outer covering that encloses the cell and helps it hold its shape. The cell membrane also controls the movement of substances in and out of the cell. The nucleus—the control centre of the cell—directs the cell’s activities and contains the cell’s chromosomes (DNA, or deoxyribonucleic acid). The cytoplasm is a gel-like fluid that supports the cell’s internal structures, including the nucleus. There are small structures within the cell’s cytoplasm called organelles, which work together to perform cell functions.

Chromosomes are threadlike structures within the nucleus that carry the cell’s “instructions” that direct all cell processes. Each cell has 46 chromosomes that contain genes, which are passed on to new cells during reproduction. Genes determine the physical and chemical traits inherited by children from their parents. Inherited traits include height, eye colour, skin colour, and many other characteristics.

Besides controlling the cell’s activities, the nucleus is responsible for cell reproduction. Cells reproduce by dividing in half and each half becoming a new cell. The process of cell division, called mitosis, is needed for the growth and repair of body tissues. The chromosomes replicate (or duplicate) and are pulled apart, and then the cell divides so that each new cell is identical, each with 46 chromosomes ([FIGURE 32.3](#f0020)).

### Tissues

The cells are the body’s building blocks. Tissue is a group of cells that are similar to each other in structure and function. The body has four basic types of tissue:

1. •
   Epithelial tissue
    covers internal and external body surfaces. Epithelial tissue is classified according to the shape of the cells and the number of layers within each. The shape of epithelial cells is described as squamous (thin and flat, like fish scales), cuboidal (cube-shaped, like dice), or columnar (tall and narrow, like columns). Epithelial tissue forms the thin outer layer of the skin that lines the cavities that open to the outside in the nose, mouth, respiratory tract, stomach, urethra, vagina, and intestines. Many glands are also epithelial tissue. The function of epithelial tissue is to aid in protection, absorption, secretion, and filtration. Secretion is the release of a substance either into or out of the body after normal body functions. Epithelial tissue has no blood supply of its own, so it depends on the blood supply of the underlying connective tissue for nourishment. Because it is composed almost entirely of cells and because it is so well nourished from the underlying connective tissue, it can repair or replace itself quickly. Epithelial tissue does contain sensory cells, so it is able to perceive sensations such as pain and pressure.
2. ![Diagram of a cell shows labels for nucleus, cell membrane, and cytoplasm.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-02-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 32.2 Parts of a cell.
3. ![Cyclic flow diagram shows six steps of cell division as follows: 1. Cell grows and prepares to divide; 2. Mitosis begins; 3. Chromosomes align; 4. Chromosomes split in half and separate; 5. Cell membrane separates the cell; 6. Two identical cells are formed.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-03-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 32.3 Cell division.
4. •
   Connective tissue
    anchors, connects, and supports other body tissues. Connective tissue is found in every part of the body. The types of connective tissue are connective tissue proper, made up of loose irregular cells in a viscous (thickened) fluid base or of dense irregular tissues, such as in joint capsules, and specialized connective tissue, which includes the following:
5. * 1. Adipose tissue (fat), which supplies energy to surrounding cells, aids in body temperature regulation, and acts both as a cushion and a support to surrounding structures
   * 2. Dense fibrous connective tissue, such as tendons (which attach muscles to bones) and ligaments (which connect bones and support joints)
   * 3. Cartilage connective tissue, which supports, protects, and cushions joints and provides framework
   * 4. Bone tissue, which can protect organs such as the brain and supports the weight of the body for moving
   * 5. Blood, which transports nutrients, hormones, and respiratory gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide and waste materials
   * 6. Lymph, which drains interstitial fluids and is involved in the body’s immune response
6. •
   Muscle tissue
    is responsible for almost all body movements through contraction. There are three types of muscle tissue (see page 728).
7. •
   Nerve tissue
    is the major tissue of the nervous system. It is the main component of the brain, spinal cord, and all the nerves in the body. It is responsible for relaying information to and from the brain and throughout the body.

### Organs and Body Cavities

Organs are structures made of different tissues that are arranged to perform a specialized function. An organ performs one or more functions. Examples of organs are the heart, brain, liver, lungs, and kidneys.

Organs are usually located within body cavities, but skin is also considered an organ. The two major cavities where organs are located are the dorsal cavity and the ventral cavity. The dorsal cavity has two cavities—the cranial cavity and the spinal cavity. The ventral cavity consists of the thoracic cavity, the abdominal cavity, and the pelvic cavity ([FIGURE 32.4](#f0025)).

### Organ Systems

Organ systems are formed by organs that work together to perform special functions ([FIGURE 32.5](#f0030)). Homeostasis is a steady state or stable internal environment maintained when all of the organ systems in the human body work together. For example, if the external temperature drops, our bodies start to shiver, and this action creates warmth and helps keep our internal temperature at the normal level.

We will discuss the major organ systems in this chapter: integumentary, musculoskeletal, nervous (including the senses), endocrine, circulatory (containing the cardiovascular and lymphatic), respiratory, immune, digestive, urinary, and reproductive.

## The Integumentary System

The integumentary system consists of skin and its appendages—hair, nails, and sweat and oil glands. (A gland is a group of cells that produces and secretes a substance. The glands in skin produce and secrete sweat and oil.) The integumentary system is the largest system of the body since the skin is considered to be the largest organ by mass.

Integument means “covering.” Skin is the body’s natural covering, and it consists of all four types of tissue working together. There are two skin layers—the epidermis and the dermis ([FIGURE 32.6](#f0035)). The dermis is the inner layer of skin and is made up of connective tissue. Blood vessels, nerves, sweat and oil glands, and hair roots are found in the dermis. The epidermis is the outer layer and contains living and dead cells arranged in specific layers. As the cells divide, creating newer cells, the older cells are pushed toward the surface, where they eventually die. Dead cells constantly flake off and are replaced by living cells. The living cells of the epidermis contain pigment, which gives skin its colour. The epidermis has no blood vessels, so it depends on blood vessels in the dermis for its nutrients and oxygen, but it does contain sensory cells and a few nerve endings, which perceive sensations like pain and pressure. Skin has four important functions:

* 1. It is the protective covering of the body. It prevents bacteria and other substances from entering the body, prevents excessive amounts of water from leaving the body, and protects organs from injury. The glands in the skin also produce and secrete substances that provide chemical protection against bacteria, viruses, and other potentially dangerous substances.
* 2. The nerve endings in skin protect the body by sensing heat, cold, pain, touch, and pressure stimulations. There are nerve endings over the entire body, and they are able to detect both pleasant and unpleasant stimulations.
* 3. Skin helps regulate body temperature. Blood vessels dilate (widen) when the temperature outside the body is high or when the temperature inside the body is high—for example, during a fever. More blood is brought to the body surface to be cooled during evaporation of sweat. During exercise, blood vessels dilate so that more blood reaches the skin to be cooled so as to cool the body. When we are cold and our body temperature is in danger of dropping, blood vessels constrict (narrow), and the body retains heat because less blood reaches the skin.
* 4. Like the urinary bladder and large intestine, the skin is an organ of elimination. Through perspiration, the skin eliminates cellular metabolism waste materials such as urea and other unwanted chemicals that may have accumulated in the perspiration.

Observing the condition of the client’s skin can tell you several things about the client. The colour of skin can indicate a physical condition. For example, your client’s skin or nails having a bluish (or grey–blue) tone could indicate a circulation condition. The client’s skin being very dry could indicate dehydration.

![Cross-section of skins shows the three layers of the skin, marked from top to bottom as: • Epidermis: Openings of sweat ducts. • Dermis: Pigment layer, sensory nerve ending for touch, oil gland, hair follicle, and hair shaft. • Sub-cutaneous fatty tissue: Sweat gland.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-06-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 32.6 Layers of skin.

The entire body, except the palms of the hands and the soles of the feet, is covered with hair. Hair in the nose, eyes, and ears protects these organs from dust, insects, and other foreign objects. Hair also helps retain heat, especially on body parts where it is plentiful (such as the head).

Nails protect the tips of fingers and toes and help fingers pick up and handle small objects. A client’s general state of health and nutritional status can be reflected by the condition of the nails. Nail beds are generally pink, so a bluish tinge to the nail beds can indicate poor oxygenation (the amount of oxygen in tissues). Clubbing of the fingernails—the fingertips’ being enlarged and the nails’ curving down—can be caused by chronic lung disease and heart disease.

Sweat glands help the body regulate temperature. Sweat consists of water, salt, and a small amount of waste. Sweat is secreted through the pores in skin, and the body is cooled as sweat evaporates. Oil glands lie near hair shafts, and they secrete an oily substance into the space near the hair shaft. Oil travels to the skin’s surface, helping keep hair and skin soft and shiny and also inhibiting the growth of bacteria on the surface of skin.

## The Musculoskeletal System

The musculoskeletal system provides the framework for the body and allows the body to move. This system also protects the body and gives it shape. Besides bones and muscles, the system consists of ligaments, tendons, and cartilage.

### Bones

Bones are hard, rigid structures that are made up of living cells surrounded by minerals that have been deposited by the living cells. A thin but strong membrane, called the periosteum, covers the entire surface of every bone in the body and merges with the ligaments and tendons that attach to the bones. Since the bones are living tissue, they require blood vessels to provide oxygen and nutrients; these vessels are found within the structure of the bones. The hard, dense layer of bone that forms the surface of every bone is called compact bone. It surrounds a softer, less dense form of bone called spongy bone (trabecular bone), which has many tiny hollow spaces (like a sponge). Inside these hollow spaces is a substance called bone marrow. Blood cells are manufactured in the bone marrow.

The human body has 206 bones ([FIGURE 32.7](#f0040)), consisting of five types:

* 1.
  Long bones
   are hard and dense bones that provide strength, structure, and mobility. They are longer than they are wide. Long bone structure is most recognizable in the bones of the arms (humerus, radius, and ulna) and legs (femur, tibia, and fibula) but also includes the metacarpals of the palms, the metatarsals of the feet, and the phalanges of the fingers and toes.
* 2.
  Short bones
   allow skill and ease in movement. The bones in the wrists and ankles are short bones. Short bones are cube-shaped.

  FIGURE 32.7 Bones of the body.

* 3.
  Flat bones
   are thin, flat, and curved bones that protect the organs. Such bones include the ribs and sternum (breastbone), the skull, pelvic bones, and scapulae (shoulder blades).
* 4.
  Irregular bones
   are irregularly shaped bones with various bony projections and features that interact with other bones. These bones provide sites for muscle attachment and allow various degrees of movement and flexibility, along with some support. They include the hip bones, vertebrae in the spinal column, and various bones in the skull.
* 5.
  Sesamoid bones
   are usually short or irregular bones that are imbedded in a tendon and protect the tendon. These bones include the patella (knee cap), which is connected to the quadriceps tendon.

### Joints

A joint is the point at which two or more bones meet ([FIGURE 32.8](#f0045)). Joints allow movement (see [Chapter 14](../B9780323709392000146/B9780323709392000146.xhtml)). Cartilage is connective tissue that cushions the joint so that bone ends do not rub together. The

synovial membrane lines the joint and secretes synovial fluid, which acts as a lubricant so that the joint can move smoothly. Bones are held together at the joint by strong bands of connective tissue called ligaments.

![Diagram shows labels for ball-and-socket joint at the shoulder, hinge joint in elbow, and pivot joint in the neck.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-08-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 32.8 Examples of joints and their movements.

Joints can be classified into three groups according to the amount of movement: fibrous, or immovable (such as the sutures in the skull bound by fibrous connective tissue); cartilaginous, or slightly movable (such as the movement achieved in the spinal column between the cartilaginous intervertebral discs); and synovial, or freely movable (which provides much more flexibility and movement than the other two types of joints). A typical synovial joint is more complex than the other types of joints and includes the following structures:

1. •
   Articular cartilage
   —which lines the articulating surface of each of the two bones, forming a smooth surface within the joint
2. ![Diagram shows labels for ball-and-socket joint at the shoulder, hinge joint in elbow, and pivot joint in the neck.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-09-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 32.9 Types of joints. A, Shoulder and elbow; neck. B, Hip.

   Source [A]: Herlihy, B. [2018]. The human body in health and illness [6th ed.]. Elsevier.
3. •
   Joint capsule
   —made up of fibrous connective tissue, enclosing the joint in a strong sleevelike covering
4. •
   Synovial membrane
   —lines the joint capsule and secretes lubricating synovial fluid into the joint cavity
5. •
   Synovial fluid
   —lubricates the bones in the joint and decreases the friction within the joint
6. •
   Bursae
    (singular: bursa)—small sacs of synovial fluid that function as a cushion and gliding surface between the joint and the tendons that cross over the joint (excessive use of the joint may cause a painful inflammation of the bursae, known as bursitis)

#### Moving Synovial Joints

The body contains many types of freely movable synovial joints. The types of motion and the degree of flexibility vary with each type of joint. There are six types of movable synovial joints, classified by the type of movement allowed by the joint ([FIGURE 32.9](#f0050)).

1. • Ball-and-socket joint—allows movement in all directions. It is made up of the rounded end of one bone and a hollow cavity or depression in another bone. The rounded end of one fits into the cavity of the other. The joints of the hips and shoulders are ball-and-socket joints. Although the ball-and-socket joint allows for a wide range of movement, it also predisposes the joint to easy displacement. For example, a fall can cause the shoulder to dislocate.
2. • Hinge joint—allows movement in one direction around a single axis. The structure of these joints is very similar to a hinge that you might find on a door. Hinge joints include elbows, knees, and fingers.
3. • Pivot joint—consists of a rounded projection on one bone fitting into a depression on another bone, which allows turning from side to side. The skull is connected to the spine by a pivot joint.
4. • Saddle joint—allows for a wide range of motion between two articulating bones that are saddle-shaped. The base of the thumb has a saddle joint known as a carpometacarpal joint, which gives the thumb a wide range of motion (opposable thumb) and gives us the ability to firmly grasp objects.
5. • Gliding joint—a joint between two corresponding flat bones held in place by ligaments that glide past one another in any direction along the plane of the joint—up and down, left and right, or diagonally. This movement ensures the bones have limited but complex gliding movements. Gliding joints are found in the wrist (intercarpal joints), ankles (intertarsal joints), and the vertebral column.
6. • Condyloid joint—is formed when the oval articular surface of one bone fits into the oval depression of the second articulating bone. The wrist
   (radiocarpal joint) and the knuckles at the base of the fingers (metacarpophalangeal joints) are examples.

### Muscles

There are more than 600 muscles in the human body (FIGURES [32.10](#f0055) through [32.12](#f0065)). Muscle tissue is categorized as forming skeletal (striated) muscles, smooth muscles, or cardiac muscles.

Skeletal muscles, the muscles attached to bones, are striated—that is, they look striped or streaked. They are also voluntary, meaning they can be consciously controlled. Arm muscles or leg muscles do not work unless you move your arm or your leg.

Smooth muscles can be identified by their smooth appearance (they are not streaked or striped). Smooth muscles are involuntary, which means they work automatically and cannot be consciously controlled. Smooth muscles are present in the stomach, intestines, blood vessels, and other body organs.

Cardiac muscle is found only in the heart. Although it appears striated like skeletal muscle, it is involuntary muscle.

Muscles can do only two things: contract and relax. Most muscles contract and relax quickly. Muscles perform three important body functions:

* 1. Movement of body parts
* 2. Maintenance of posture
* 3. Production of body heat

Strong, tough connective tissues called tendons connect muscles to bones. When muscles contract (shorten), tendons at each end of the muscle cause the bone to move. The body has numerous tendons; the Achilles tendon is shown in [FIGURE 32.12](#f0065).

Some muscles constantly contract to maintain the body’s posture. When muscles contract, they must
metabolize nutrients to generate energy, resulting in the production of heat. The greater the muscular activity, the greater the amount of heat produced in the body. As a result, we get warm when we exercise. Shivering is a way the body produces heat when exposed to cold. The shivering sensation arises from rapid, general muscle contractions.

## The Nervous System

The nervous system controls, directs, and coordinates body functions. The two main divisions of the nervous system are the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral nervous system (PNS). The CNS consists of the brain and the spinal cord ([FIGURE 32.13](#f0070)), and the PNS consists of the nerves throughout the body ([FIGURE 32.14](#f0075)), which carry messages or impulses to and from the brain. All the nerves in the body are connected to either the spinal cord (through the spinal nerves) or the brain and brain stem (through cranial nerves). Nerve tissue is composed of two types of cells:

* 1. Neuroglia, which nourish, protect, and insulate neurons
* 2. Neurons, which transmit information in the form of electrical nerve impulses

![Posterior view of human model shows labels for muscles, marked from top to bottom as: Trapezius, Deltoid, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, Teres major, Triceps brachii, Latissimus dorsi, Gluteus medius, Gluteus maximus, Adductor magnus, Gracilis, Hamstring group (including Biceps femoris, Semitendinosus, Semimembranosus, Gastrocnemius, Soleus, and Achilles tendon (calcaneal tendon).](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-12-9780323711654.jpg)

From Herlihy, B. [2018]. The human body in health and illness [6th ed., Fig. 9.7B]. Elsevier.

A nerve impulse is a signal that is transmitted along the membrane of the neuron, starting at the point where the impulse was applied. Nerve impulses pass, chain-like, from one cell to another.

Each neuron is composed of three parts ([FIGURE 32.15](#f0080)), through which these electrical nerve impulses pass:

* 1. The dendrite, which contains receptors that receive stimuli directly for sensations such as pain, cold, pressure, and heat. The dendrite transmits this information to the cell body from within the terminal ends of sensory neuron dendrites. Dendrites usually (although not always) branch from the neuron cell body.
* 2. The cell body, which contains the organelles (smaller parts), including the nucleus. The electrical nerve impulse passes through the cell body.
* 3. The axon, which carries information away from the cell to the axon terminals (ends).

Nerve impulses have both electrical and chemical traits. Electrical impulses transmit a signal within a neuron, whereas chemical processes transmit the signal from one neuron to another or to a muscle cell ([FIGURE 32.16](#f0085)). The electrical impulse may then be transmitted across the synapse (the gap between neurons) to the next dendrite. When a nerve signal, or
impulse, reaches the ends of its axon, it has travelled as an action potential (a pulse of electricity, or temporary reversal of the polarity of the cell).[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur321)

While there is no cellular continuity between neurons because of the synapses, neurons form a network of electrical activities and thus have to be interconnected in some way. A chemical process of interaction between neurons and effector (target) cells occurs at the end of the axon in the synapse.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur321) The axon terminals produce and store chemical substances called neurotransmitters. The neurotransmitters are released by the presynaptic (before the synapse) membrane and seep across the gap to receptors on the receiving neuron’s postsynaptic (after the synapse) membrane. The neurotransmitters bind to these receptors, causing changes to the membrane that can slow or speed up the impulse and also allows ions (charged particles) to pass in and out of the receiving cell.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur321)

Nervous tissue is easily damaged and has a limited capacity for regeneration. Some nerve fibres have a protective covering called a myelin sheath, which also insulates the nerve fibre. Nerve fibres covered with myelin can conduct impulses faster than those fibres without the protective covering.

### The Central Nervous System

The CNS consists of the brain and the spinal cord. The brain is contained in and protected by the skull. The three main parts of the brain are the cerebrum, the cerebellum, and the brain stem (see [FIGURE 32.13](#f0070)).

The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain and is the centre of thought and intelligence. The cerebrum is divided into two halves called the right and left hemispheres. The right hemisphere controls movement and activities on the body’s left side. The
left hemisphere controls the body’s right side. The outermost layer of the cerebrum is called the cerebral cortex, and it consists of billions of unmyelinated neurons (grey matter) ([FIGURE 32.17](#f0090)). The cerebral cortex controls the highest functions of the brain: reasoning, memory, consciousness, speech, voluntary muscle movement, vision, hearing, sensation, and other activities.

![Illustration shows the structure of a neuron with parts labeled as cell body, axon, dendrites, axon terminals, nodes of ranvier, neurilemma, and myelin sheath.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-15-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 32.15 The structure of a neuron.
From Herlihy, B. [2018]. The human body in health and illness [6th ed., Fig. 10.4]. Elsevier.

The cerebellum regulates and coordinates body movements. The smooth movements of voluntary muscles and the body’s balance are possible because of their control by the cerebellum. Injury to the cerebellum results in jerky movements, loss of coordination, and muscle weakness.

The brain stem connects the cerebrum to the spinal cord. Within the brain stem are important structures including the midbrain, the pons, and the medulla. The midbrain and the pons relay messages between the medulla and the cerebrum. The medulla is directly below the pons. The medulla controls heart rate, breathing, blood vessel size, swallowing, coughing, and vomiting. The brain connects to the spinal cord at the lower end of the medulla.

The spinal cord lies within and is protected by the spinal column. The cord is about 45 cm (18 in.) long. It contains pathways that conduct messages to and from the brain and the spinal nerves.

The brain and the spinal cord are covered and protected by three layers of connective tissue called meninges. The outer layer, a tough covering called the dura mater, lies next to the skull and partially protects the brain from movement. The middle layer is called the arachnoid, and the inner layer is the pia mater, which adheres to the brain directly and contains the blood vessels surrounding the brain. The space between the middle and inner layers is the arachnoid space, and this space is filled with a fluid called cerebrospinal fluid that circulates around the
brain and the spinal cord. The cerebrospinal fluid protects the CNS and cushions shocks that could easily injure the structures of the brain and the spinal cord.

### The Peripheral Nervous System

The PNS has 12 pairs of cranial nerves and 31 pairs of spinal nerves. Cranial nerves conduct impulses between the brain and the head, neck, chest, and abdomen and thus the impulses for smell, vision, hearing, pain, touch, temperature, pressure, and voluntary and involuntary muscle control. The vagus nerve is the only cranial nerve that has extensive association with the entire body. Sensations including touch, pain, temperature, pressure, as well as the control of muscle activity are handled by the various spinal nerves associated with each region of the body. Spinal nerves carry impulses from skin, extremities (a limb of the body, especially a hand or a foot), and the internal body structures not supplied by cranial nerves.

Peripheral nerves with special functions form the autonomic nervous system. This system controls involuntary muscles and certain body functions that occur “automatically,” including heartbeat, blood pressure, intestinal contractions, and glandular secretions.

The autonomic nervous system is divided into the sympathetic nervous system and the parasympathetic nervous system. These divisions balance each other. The sympathetic nervous system is responsible for what we refer to as the “fight or flight” response and generally (but not always) tends to speed up functions. The parasympathetic nervous system is responsible for maintaining our normal or “relaxed” condition with respect to body function and generally makes processes such as digestion and normal cell metabolism a priority (energy conservation). When you are angry, frightened, or excited, the sympathetic nervous system is stimulated. The parasympathetic system is more active during the remainder of the time or when the sympathetic system has been stimulated for too long.

## The Sense Organs

The five senses are sight, hearing, taste, smell, and touch. Receptors for taste are located in the tongue and are called taste buds. Receptors for smell are located in the nose. Touch receptors are found in the dermis of skin in varying numbers throughout the body, with particularly large numbers in the fingertips, toes, and lips.

### The Eye and the Sense of Sight

Receptors for vision are located in the eyes. The eye is easily injured. Bones of the skull, eyelids, eyelashes, and tears all protect the eyes from injury. Eye structures are shown in [FIGURE 32.18](#f0095). The eye has three layers:

1. • The sclera, the white of the eye, is the outer layer except for its anterior one sixth, which is the cornea. The sclera is made of tough connective tissue.
2. • The choroid is the second layer. Blood vessels, the ciliary muscle, and the iris make up the choroid. The iris gives the eye its colour. The opening in the middle of the iris is the pupil. Pupil size varies with the amount of light entering the eye. The pupil contracts (narrows) in bright light and dilates (widens) in dim or dark places.
3. • The retina is the inner layer of the eye. It contains receptors for vision and the nerve fibres of the optic nerve.

Light enters the eye through the cornea. The cornea is the transparent part of the outer layer that lies over the eye. Light rays pass to the lens, which lies behind the pupil. The light is then reflected to the retina and carried to the brain by the optic nerve for its interpretation of what is being seen.

The aqueous chamber separates the cornea from the lens. The chamber is filled with a fluid called aqueous humor. The fluid helps the cornea keep its shape and position. The vitreous humor is found behind the lens. The vitreous body is a gelatin-like substance that supports the retina and maintains the eye’s shape.

### The Ear and the Sense of Hearing

The ear is a sense organ that functions for hearing and balance. The ear is divided into the external ear, middle ear, and inner ear. Ear structures are shown in [FIGURE 32.19](#f0100).

The external ear (outer part) is called the pinna or auricle. Sound waves are guided through the external ear into the auditory canal. Glands in the auditory canal secrete a waxy substance called cerumen, which catches particles that enter the ear. The auditory canal extends about 2.5 cm (1 in.) to the eardrum. The eardrum (tympanic membrane) separates the external ear and the middle ear.

The Eustachian tube (also called the auditory tube), which opens into the middle ear, connects the middle ear and the throat. Air enters the Eustachian tube from the throat so that the pressure is equal on both sides of the eardrum. The middle ear is a small space that contains three small bones called ossicles. The ossicles amplify sound received from the eardrum and transmit the sound to the inner ear. The three ossicles are as follows:

* 1. The malleus, which looks like a hammer
* 2. The incus, which resembles an anvil
* 3. The stapes, which is shaped like a stirrup

The inner ear consists of the cochlea and semi-circular canals. The cochlea, which looks like a snail shell, contains fluid. The fluid carries sound waves received from the middle ear to the auditory nerve. The auditory nerve then carries the message to the brain for interpretation of what is being heard.

The three semi-circular canals are involved in maintaining balance. They sense the head’s position and any changes in position and send messages to the brain.

### The Tongue and the Sense of Taste

The taste buds contain the receptors for taste. They are located around the small structures on the upper surface of the tongue, the soft palate, the upper esophagus, and the epiglottis. They relay sensations of different tastes to the brain for interpretation. The four basic taste sensations are salty, sweet, sour, and bitter. The receptors for each sensation are located all over the tongue. The anterior two-thirds of the tongue is controlled by the facial nerve (cranial nerve [CN] VII). The posterior third of the tongue is controlled by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX).

### The Nose and the Sense of Smell

Receptors for smell are located on the roof of the nasal cavity. These are called olfactory receptors, and sense of smell is controlled by the olfactory nerve (CN I). These receptors are stimulated by the fluid in the mucous membrane in the nose. The impulses are sent to the brain, where they are interpreted as smell. Smell and taste are very connected. When we have a cold and our nose is congested (“stuffed”), we find that foods often do not taste the same.

## The Endocrine System

The endocrine system is made up of glands called endocrine glands ([FIGURE 32.20](#f0105)). The endocrine glands secrete chemical substances called hormones into the bloodstream. Hormones regulate the activities of other organs and glands in the body by binding to the receptor sites of their “target” tissue, similar to a key fitting in its corresponding lock. Hormones influence metabolic processes involving fat, proteins, and carbohydrates; water and electrolyte balance; cell growth and development; and regulation of the numerous chemical components in our cells and body fluids. Hormones play an important role in growth and reproduction.

The pituitary gland is also called the master gland. About the size of a cherry, it is located at the base of the brain behind the eyes. The pituitary gland is divided into the anterior pituitary lobe and the posterior pituitary lobe. The anterior pituitary lobe secretes the following:

1. •
   Growth hormone
   —needed for the growth of muscles, bones, and other organs. It is needed throughout life to maintain normal-size bones and muscles. Growth is stunted if a baby is born with deficient amounts of growth hormone. Too much of the hormone causes excessive growth. More information about this hormone will become available from research being conducted in Canada and elsewhere.
2. ![Diagram of endocrine system shows label for pineal gland, pituitary gland, thyroid gland, parathyroid glands, thymus gland, adrenal glands, pancreas, ovaries, and testes.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-20-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 32.20 The endocrine system.
3. •
   Thyroid-stimulating hormone
    (TSH)—needed for thyroid gland function
4. •
   Adrenocorticotropic hormone
    (ACTH)—stimulates the adrenal glands
5. •
   Reproductive hormones
   —regulate growth, development, and function of the male and female reproductive systems

The posterior pituitary lobe secretes antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and oxytocin. ADH prevents the kidneys from excreting excessive amounts of water. Oxytocin causes the uterine muscles to contract during childbirth. These two hormones are produced in a nearby structure in the brain called the hypothalamus. The hypothalamus is part of the brain, but it secretes several hormones, and so it is also considered part of the endocrine system. The hormones produced in the hypothalamus control or inhibit the release of other hormones.

The thyroid gland, shaped like a butterfly, is situated in the neck in front of the larynx and secretes
two types of thyroid hormones—thyroxine (T4) and tri-iodothyronine (T3)—as well as a hormone called calcitonin. Thyroid hormones regulate metabolism, which is the burning of nutrients by the cells to produce heat and energy. The thyroid hormones T3 and T4 are responsible for the proper functioning of all other hormones, the maturation of the nervous system, and normal growth and development. Calcitonin, a hormone produced in the C cells of the thyroid, helps to regulate calcium levels in the body by lowering the amount of calcium in the blood plasma and increasing bone calcium content.

Too little of the thyroid hormones T3 and T4 results in slowed body processes, slowed movements, and weight gain. Too much of them causes increased metabolism, excess energy, and weight loss. If a baby is born with deficient amounts of thyroid hormone, physical and mental growth will be stunted.

The parathyroid glands are four small collections of cells, two on each side of the thyroid gland, and they secrete parathormone. Parathormone also regulates the body’s use of calcium by increasing the amount of calcium available in the blood plasma. Calcium is needed for the proper functioning of nerves, contraction of muscles, bone development, and blood clotting. Insufficient amounts of calcium cause tetany—a state of severe muscle contraction and spasm—which, if untreated, can result in death.

There are two adrenal glands, one on the top of each kidney. The adrenal gland has two parts: the adrenal medulla and the adrenal cortex. The adrenal medulla secretes epinephrine and norepinephrine. These hormones stimulate the body to quickly produce energy during emergencies so that heart rate, blood pressure, muscle power, and energy all increase. The adrenal cortex secretes three groups of hormones that are essential for life: (1) glucocorticoids, which regulate the metabolism of carbohydrates and control the body’s response to stress and inflammation; (2) mineralocorticoids, which regulate the amount of salt and water absorbed and lost by the kidneys; and (3) small amounts of male and female sex hormones.

The pancreas secretes both insulin and glucagon, which regulate the amount of glucose (sugar) in the blood available for use by cells. Insulin is needed for sugar to enter cells. If there is too little insulin, sugar cannot enter cells, resulting in excess amounts of sugar building up in blood. Insulin and glucagon are antagonistic hormones, which means that they result in opposite actions occurring in the body and can therefore “balance” one another. The actions of hormones such as insulin and glucagon are good examples of negative feedback mechanisms in the body that help maintain homeostasis.

The gonads are the glands of human reproduction. Male sex glands (testes) secrete testosterone. Female sex glands (ovaries) secrete estrogen and progesterone (see page 747).

## The Circulatory System

The circulatory system is an organ system that transports and exchanges nutrients (amino acids, glucose, fatty acids), gases (oxygen and carbon dioxide), hormones, blood cells, and wastes to and from cells in the body to maintain normal cell processes and keep cells alive.

Two types of fluids move through the circulatory system: blood and lymph. Blood, the heart, and blood vessels form the cardiovascular system. The lymph, lymph nodes, and lymph vessels form the lymphatic system. The cardiovascular system and the lymphatic system together make up the circulatory system.

### The Cardiovascular System

The cardiovascular system is made up of blood, the heart, and blood vessels. The heart works as a pump to transport blood into two separate systems of blood vessels ([FIGURE 32.21](#f0110)). The right side of the heart pumps unoxygenated blood (normally shown as blue) to the lungs for oxygenation (pulmonary circulation). The left side of the heart receives oxygenated blood (normally shown as red) from the lungs and pumps it to all of the tissues and organs of the body (systemic circulation).

The cardiovascular system has numerous important functions:

1. • Blood carries nutrients, oxygen, and other substances to the cells throughout the body so that they can live and function.
2. • During exercise or physical activity, increased energy demands of organs and muscles are automatically met through increased blood flow.
3. • To help stop bleeding and to promote healing, the circulatory system sends clotting cells and proteins to the site of injury or bleeding.
4. ![Illustration depicts systematic and pulmonary circulation. In systemic circulation, there are capillary beds for all tissues of the body. Capillary beds of lungs are where gas exchange (between oxygen and carbon dioxide) occurs. Oxygenated blood leaves lungs through pulmonary vein to left ventricle, and left pump pushes it to aorta and branches, from where they reach capillary beds for all body tissues.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-21-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 32.21 Pulmonary and systemic circulations.

5. • Blood removes waste products from cells.
6. • Blood and blood vessels help regulate body temperature. Heat from muscle activity is carried by blood to other body parts. Blood vessels in skin dilate if the body needs to be cooled. They constrict if heat needs to be kept in the body.
7. • The cardiovascular system also produces and carries white blood cells and antibodies that defend the body from disease-causing microorganisms.

#### Blood

Blood consists of blood cells and a liquid portion called plasma, which is approximately 90% water. Blood composition is approximately 55% plasma and 45% cells. Plasma carries blood cells to other body cells. It also carries other substances needed by cells for proper functioning. Nutrients (proteins, fats, and carbohydrates), hormones, chemicals, and waste products are some of the substances carried in the plasma.

Red blood cells are called erythrocytes and they give blood its red colour because of a substance in the blood cell called hemoglobin. As red blood cells circulate through the capillaries in the lungs, hemoglobin picks up oxygen from inhaled air and carries it to cells. When hemoglobin in red blood cells is saturated (filled) with oxygen, the blood appears bright red. As blood circulates to the tissues and organs, oxygen is released from the hemoglobin and is given to cells. It is the lack of oxygen that makes the colour of the blood change from bright red to “bluish-red.” Cells release carbon dioxide (a waste product), which is picked up by the blood and transported back to the lungs, where it can be released and “exhaled” during ventilation.

There are about 25 trillion (25000000000000) red blood cells (erythrocytes) in the body. In a cubic millimetre of blood (the size of a tiny drop), there are about 4.5 to 5 million cells. These cells live for approximately 120 days, and as they wear out, they are destroyed by the liver and the spleen and replaced with new erythrocytes. Red bone marrow produces new red blood cells at a rate of about two million every second.

White blood cells, called leukocytes, are colourless. They protect the body against infection. There are 5000 to 10000 white blood cells in a cubic millimetre of blood. At the first sign of infection, white blood cells rush to the site of the infection and begin to multiply rapidly to kill the invading microorganisms to fight the infection. The number of white blood cells increases when there is an infection in the body. White blood cells are also produced by the bone marrow and live for approximately 9 days.

Platelets (thrombocytes) are necessary for the clotting of blood. They are also produced by the bone marrow. There are about 200000 to 400000 platelets in a cubic millimetre of blood. A platelet lives for approximately 4 days.

#### The Heart

The heart is a hollow, muscular organ that lies in the middle to lower part of the chest cavity and tilts slightly toward the left side ([FIGURE 32.22](#f0115)). The chambers, major vessels, valves, and circulation pathways of the heart are shown in [FIGURE 32.23](#f0120).

The heart has three layers:

* 1. The pericardium is the outer layer. It is a thin sac surrounding the heart that forms a fluid-filled (pericardial fluid) space around the heart called the pericardial space.
* 2. The myocardium is the second layer. This layer is the thick, muscular portion of the heart.
* 3. The endocardium is the inner layer. The endocardium is the membrane lining the inner chambers of the heart.

2. • The two lower chambers—called ventricles—pump blood out of the heart. The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs (pulmonary circulation), where it picks up oxygen, and the left ventricle pumps blood to all parts of the body (systemic circulation).

* 2. The mitral valve (bicuspid valve) is located between the left atrium and the left ventricle.

* 4. The aortic valve is located between the aorta and the left ventricle.

There are two phases of heart action. During diastole, the resting phase, heart chambers fill with blood. During systole, the working phase, the heart contracts. Blood is pumped through the major arteries exiting the heart to the blood vessels when the heart contracts.

The rate at which the heart beats (heart rate) can differ for various reasons. Generally, the larger the body size, the slower the rate is—men, therefore, have slower heart rates than women do. As well, heart rate generally slows with age. Disease, fever, exercise, and some medications can also influence heart rate. The pulse may be used to measure heart rate (refer to [Chapter 33](../B978032370939200033X/B978032370939200033X.xhtml) for more information on pulse rate).

#### Blood Vessels

Blood flows to body tissues and cells through blood vessels. Blood vessels vary in size, structure, and function, and there are several types, including arteries, arterioles, capillaries, veins, and venules. [FIGURE 32.24](#f0125) shows some of the major arteries and veins in the body.

Arteries carry blood away from the heart.

1. • Systemic arteries carry blood from the heart to the vessels that supply the tissues of the body. The aorta is the largest systemic artery and exits the heart directly from the left ventricle. The aorta branches into other arteries that carry oxygenated blood to all parts of the body, and these arteries, in turn, branch into various other vessels supplying all regions of the body.
2. • Pulmonary arteries carry blood from the heart to the vessels of the lungs, where gas exchange takes place. The pulmonary trunk is the largest pulmonary artery and exits the heart from the right ventricle. This pulmonary trunk branches into the right pulmonary artery, which goes to the right lung, and the left pulmonary artery, which goes to the left lung.

The smallest branch of an artery is an arteriole. Arterioles connect with blood vessels called capillaries, which are tiny, thin-walled vessels. Nutrients, oxygen, and other substances pass from capillaries into cells, and waste products, including carbon dioxide, pass from cells into the bloodstream.

Veins return blood to the heart.

### The Lymphatic System

The lymphatic system consists of lymphoid organs, lymph nodes, lymph ducts, lymphatic tissue, lymph capillaries, and lymph vessels. As blood circulates through capillaries, blood plasma leaks out into the spaces between cells. About 85% of the fluid is absorbed back into the blood capillaries. The other 15% drains into the lymphatic capillaries that surround the blood capillaries. This fluid is called lymph and is composed primarily of water, electrolytes, waste from metabolizing cells, and some protein. The lymphatic capillaries empty into larger lymph vessels.

Lymphatic vessels are distributed around the entire body and run beside veins. The lymph flows through larger and larger vessels until it reaches lymph ducts. These ducts drain into the veins below the collarbone, called subclavian veins.

Blood moves because it is pumped by the heart, but lymph depends on other means for movement. Lymph moves in response to contraction of skeletal muscles, the movement of the chest during respiration, and contraction of smooth muscles.

Lymphoid organs include lymph nodes, tonsils, the thymus gland, and the spleen.

Lymph nodes are small, pea-shaped patches of lymphatic tissue. Cervical lymph nodes drain and filter lymph from the head and neck area. Axillary lymph nodes drain and cleanse lymph from the upper extremities, shoulders, and breasts. Inguinal lymph nodes are located in the groin and drain and cleanse lymph from the lower extremities and external genitalia ([FIGURE 32.25](#f0130)).

Tonsils, which are located in the throat, filter tissue fluid contaminated by pathogens that enter the body through the nose, the mouth, or both.

The thymus gland is located in the thoracic cavity (see [FIGURE 32.25](#f0130)). It plays a crucial role in the development of the immune system before birth and in the first few months after birth. After puberty, the gland shrinks but remains active. It secretes a hormone that aids the immune system.

The spleen filters blood rather than lymph. It cleanses blood and destroys microorganisms. The spleen also produces disease-preventing cells such as lymphocytes and macrophages.

1. • It maintains the balance of fluid between the blood and tissues, known as fluid homeostasis.

3. • It facilitates absorption of fats and fat-soluble nutrients in the digestive system.

#### The Effects of Aging on the Lymphatic System

As people age, their immune system becomes less effective in the following ways:[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur322)

1. • Cells called macrophages (which ingest bacteria and other foreign cells) destroy bacteria, cancer cells, and other antigens more slowly. This slowdown may be one reason that cancer is more common among older people.
2. • The immune system becomes less able to distinguish self from non-self (that is, to identify foreign antigens). As a result, autoimmune disorders become more common.
3. • Fewer white blood cells are present to respond to new antigens. Thus, when older people encounter a new antigen, the body is less able to remember and defend against it.
4. • Older people do not produce the type of proteins needed to fight bacterial infections.
5. • Antibodies become less able to attach to the antigen, which may partly explain why pneumonia, influenza, infective endocarditis, and tetanus are more common among older people and result in death more often. These changes may also partly explain why vaccines are less effective in older people and thus why it is important for older people to get booster shots (which are available for some vaccines).

## The Respiratory System

Since oxygen is necessary for survival, every cell needs oxygen. Air contains about 21% oxygen—enough to meet the needs of the body under normal conditions. The respiratory system ([FIGURE 32.26](#f0135)) brings oxygen into the lungs and eliminates carbon dioxide. Respiration is the process of supplying oxygen to cells and removing carbon dioxide from them. It involves inhalation (breathing in) and exhalation (breathing out). The terms inspiration (breathing in) and expiration (breathing out) are also used; together, the process is referred to as ventilation. The respiratory system is divided into the upper respiratory tract and the lower respiratory tract. The upper respiratory tract includes the nose and nasal cavities, the pharynx, and the larynx. The lower respiratory tract includes the trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli.

![Diagram of human model shows labels for sinuses, pharynx, trachea, left main bronchus, right main bronchus, bronchiole, pleura, lungs, diaphragm, with an inset highlighting the alveoli transected showing walls and sac interiors, alveolus, alveolar sac, alveolar ducts, respiratory bronchus.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-26-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 32.26 The respiratory system.

Air enters the body through the nose and passes into the pharynx (throat), a tube-shaped passageway for both air and nutrients. It then passes from the pharynx into the larynx (which contains the vocal cords or “voice box”). A piece of cartilage called the epiglottis acts like a lid over the larynx and prevents food and liquids from entering the airway during swallowing. During inhalation, the epiglottis lifts up so that air passes over the larynx and into the trachea (the windpipe).

The trachea divides at its lower end into the right bronchus and the left bronchus. Each bronchus enters a lung and further divides several times into smaller branches called bronchioles. Eventually, the bronchioles subdivide and end in tiny air sacs called alveoli, which are composed of a very thin membrane, only one cell layer thick.

Alveoli look like small clusters of grapes. They are surrounded by large numbers of pulmonary capillaries. Oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged between alveoli and capillaries. Blood in capillaries picks up oxygen from alveoli and is returned to the left side of the heart and is then pumped to the rest of the body. Alveoli pick up carbon dioxide from capillaries, and the carbon dioxide is then breathed out.

Lungs are spongy tissues filled with alveoli, blood vessels, connective tissue, and a few nerves. Each lung is divided into sections called lobes. The right lung has three lobes; the left lung has two. The lungs and airways are located in the thoracic cavity, which is separated from the abdominal cavity by a muscle called the diaphragm. The lungs contain no actual skeletal muscle tissue and thus cannot move by themselves, so it is the movement of this diaphragm muscle that is the driving force for ventilation.

Each lung is covered by a two-layered sac called the pleura. One layer is attached to the lung, and the other to the chest wall. The pleura secretes a very thin fluid that fills the space between the layers. The fluid prevents the layers from rubbing together during inhalation and exhalation.

## The Immune System

The immune system defends the body against threats from inside and outside. Some of these threats include pollens, toxins, bacteria, viruses, and our own cells that have become abnormal (cancer). Immunity means that a person has protection against a disease or condition—the person will not contract or be affected by the disease. There are two types of defence mechanisms, or immunity, classified as nonspecific immunity and specific immunity.

Nonspecific immunity is the body’s reaction to anything it does not recognize as a normal body substance. The types of immunity are shown in [FIGURE 32.27](#f0140).

The first line of defence includes the following:

1. •
   Mechanical barriers
   —for example, intact skin and mucous membranes
2. •
   Chemical barriers
   —for example, tears, stomach acids, saliva, perspiration
3. •
   Reflexes
   —for example, coughing, sneezing, vomiting, and diarrhea

The second line of defence includes the following:

1. •
   Phagocytes
   —white blood cells that digest and destroy microorganisms and other unwanted substances
2. •
   Inflammation
   —the release of histamine to an injured or irritated area, causing blood vessels to
   dilate and bring more blood to the area (histamine causes redness, heat, and fluid leakage of the blood, which leads to swelling)
3. ![Flow chart shows types of immunity as induced immunity: using antibodies to resist specific diseases leading to active immunity: antibodies produced in the person’s body and passive immunity: antibodies from another organism enter the person’s body. The active immunity leads to natural: pathogens enter the body in a natural manner for example catching a cold and artificial: the pathogen is introduced into the body as a vaccine for example being immunized or vaccinated for polio. The passive immunity lead to natural: antibodies enter a person in a natural manner for example antibodies cross the placenta into the fetus and artificial: antibodies are injected into a person for example anti-tetanus injections.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-27-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 32.27 Types of immunity.
4. •
   Fever
   —stimulates phagocytosis (ingesting or engulfing other cells) and decreases the ability of certain pathogens to multiply

Specific immunity is the ability of the body to resist or overcome a certain disease or infection caused by a foreign agent such as a pathogen or antigenic substance. Special cells and substances provide immunity.

1. •
   Antibodies
   —normal body substances that recognize abnormal or unwanted substances and attack and destroy such substances
2. •
   Antigens
   —abnormal or unwanted substances (usually proteins) that cause the body to produce antibodies, which attack and destroy the antigens
3. •
   Lymphocytes
   —white blood cells that produce antibodies, the production of which increases as the body responds to an infection
4. •
   B lymphocytes (B cells)
   —cells that cause the production of antibodies that circulate in the plasma
5. •
   T lymphocytes (T cells)—
   cells that destroy invading cells (killer T cells produce toxic substances near the invading cells; some T cells attract other cells that destroy the invaders)

When the body senses an antigen (an unwanted substance), the immune system is activated, and phagocyte and lymphocyte production increases. Phagocytes destroy the invaders through digestion, and lymphocytes produce antibodies that attack and destroy unwanted substances. Antibodies, produced by B cells, react to specific antigens.

Immunity to diseases can be classified as genetic immunity and acquired immunity. Genetic immunity protects a member of one species from diseases that afflict other species; for example, your cat will not catch chickenpox from you.

A person can get acquired immunity either naturally or artificially. Natural immunity can be acquired in two ways:

* 1. Active immunity. By naturally acquiring an illness (such as chickenpox), your body develops the antibodies to protect you from getting it again.
* 2. Passive immunity. Passive immunity is short-lived and passed from mother to fetus through the placenta and from the mother to the baby through breast milk. This protection lasts for about the first 6 months after birth.

Artificially acquired immunity is achieved by injection of a vaccine or immune globulin (immunoglobulin). A vaccine is a small amount of an antigen-bearing substance (pathogen) that is injected into a person to stimulate the production of antibodies. Immune globulin is obtained from a donor who already has the antibodies and is injected into a person to provide passive immunity.

## The Digestive System

The digestive system, also called the gastrointestinal system (GI system), breaks down nutrients physically and chemically so that they can be absorbed for use
by cells—a process called digestion—and also eliminates solid wastes from the body.

![Diagram of human model for digestive system shows labels for pharynx, tongue, salivary glands, esophagus, liver, stomach, pancreas, gall bladder, small intestine (including duodenum, jejunum, and ileum), large intestine (including transverse colon, ascending colon, descending colon, appendix, sigmoid colon, and rectus), and anus.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-28-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 32.28 The digestive system.

The digestive system consists of the alimentary canal (GI tract) and the accessory organs of digestion ([FIGURE 32.28](#f0145)). The alimentary canal is a long tube extending from the mouth to the anus. Its major parts are the mouth, the pharynx, the esophagus, the stomach, the small intestine, and the large intestine. The accessory organs of digestion are the teeth, tongue, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas.

### Structure of the Digestive Tract Wall

Although modified for specific functions in different organs, the wall of the digestive tract has a similar structure throughout its length. The innermost layer of the digestive tract is the mucosa, which contains glands (which secrete mucus, digestive enzymes, and hormones) and ducts (which connect glands that empty into the digestive tract).

The middle layer of the GI tract is called the submucosa. It is composed of loose connective tissue containing blood vessels, nerves, glands, and lymphatic vessels.

The third and most superficial layer (the outermost layer) is called the muscularis or muscle layer and consists of two layers of smooth muscles. These muscles are responsible for two types of muscle movements in the digestive tract that aid in digestion. The first movement is a repeated relaxation and contraction of the stomach muscles, which mixes digestive juices with the food to digest the food mechanically in a “swishing” or back-and-forth motion. The second is called peristalsis, which is smooth-muscle (involuntary) contraction that pushes food forward in the digestive tract in one direction in a wavelike motion—similar to the way that toothpaste is squeezed through a tube.

### The Process of Digestion

Food enters the mouth—also called the oral cavity—which receives nutrients and prepares them for digestion. The first step of digestion is called mastication. During this process, food is bitten, positioned by the

tongue and cheeks, and then chewed, crushed, and ground by the teeth in order to increase the food’s surface area. Physically breaking down large food particles into smaller pieces is called mechanical digestion. Taste buds are on the tongue’s surface and on the soft palate, the upper esophagus, and the epiglottis; they contain nerve endings and allow sweet, sour, bitter, and salty tastes to be sensed (see page 732). Taste buds and olfactory nerve stimulation trigger saliva to be secreted from salivary glands in the mouth. Saliva is a substance composed mostly of water (99%), with the remainder consisting of digestive enzymes, mucus, electrolytes, glycoproteins, and antibacterial chemicals. During mastication, the saliva moistens the food and aids in mixing and swallowing it. The saliva also contains enzymes such as salivary amylase, an enzyme that breaks down the carbohydrates within the food and starts the chemical digestion process.

Chemical digestion is the chemical breakdown of food into small nutrient molecules that the cells can absorb and utilize. At this point, the food has been altered into a moist ball (called a bolus) and is then swallowed for further digestion to take place. During swallowing, the tongue pushes nutrients into the pharynx. The pharynx contracts, pushing nutrients into the esophagus. The esophagus, a muscular tube about 25 cm (10 in.) long, extends from the pharynx to the stomach. Peristalsis moves nutrients down the esophagus into the stomach through a muscular valve called the cardiac sphincter, which controls the entrance to the stomach.

The stomach is a muscular, pouchlike sac in the upper left portion of the abdominal cavity. Strong stomach muscles stir and churn nutrients to break them up into even smaller particles. The stomach is lined with a mucous membrane, which contains glands that secrete gastric juices. Gastric juices contain strong acids that kill most microorganisms in the nutrients that have been eaten. The partially digested food is mixed and churned with gastric juices to form a semi-liquid substance called chyme. In addition to the gastric juice, other cells secrete both thicker mucus and bicarbonate that adhere closely to the stomach and small intestine lining and prevent the acidic gastric juices from digesting the body’s own tissue! This chyme is very acidic, foul-smelling, and watery. Through peristalsis, the chyme is pushed from the stomach into the small intestine through another muscular valve called the pyloric sphincter.

The small intestine is about 6 m (20 ft.) long and has three parts. The first part is the duodenum, where more digestive juices are added to the chyme, along with a bicarbonate solution from the pancreas to reduce the acidity of the chyme. The enzymes in the digestive juices chemically break down the nutrients so that they can be absorbed. Peristalsis moves the chyme through the two remaining portions of the small intestine: the jejunum and the ileum. Tiny projections called villi (singular, villus) line the small intestine. Villi greatly increase the surface area of the small intestine so that digested nutrients can be efficiently absorbed into capillaries. Most of the absorption of nutrients takes place in the jejunum and the ileum.

One of the digestive juices is bile, which is a greenish liquid produced by the liver and stored in the gallbladder. Bile aids not only in the digestion of fat but also in the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins. It gives stool its brownish colour. The liver has important functions: (1) metabolizing fats, proteins, and carbohydrates; (2) detoxifying substances from blood; (3) storing fat-soluble vitamins; and (4) producing bile, cholesterol, and bilirubin.

The pancreas secretes the enzymes that are the most important of all the digestive enzymes because of their role in aiding digestion. Juices from the pancreas and the small intestine are added to the chyme.

The remaining chyme, along with any undigested nutrients and waste products, passes from the small intestine into the large intestine (large bowel or colon). The large intestine has four functions:

* 1. It absorbs much of the water and electrolytes from the chyme.
* 2. It contains certain beneficial bacteria that aid in the production and metabolism of substances such as vitamin K, vitamin B12, and thiamine (vitamin B1).
* 3. It temporarily stores feces (the remaining semi-solid material, consisting of water, solid wastes, and some mucus and microorganisms).
* 4. It aids in the elimination of feces through defecation (bowel movement).

Through peristalsis, feces pass through the colon into the rectum, where it can be temporarily stored. Defecation is the process of excreting feces from the rectum through the anus. The term stool refers to excreted feces.

## The Urinary System

Waste products are removed from the body through the respiratory system, the digestive system, the integumentary system (skin), and the urinary system. The digestive system rids the body of solid wastes; lungs rid the body of carbon dioxide; water and other substances are excreted in sweat through the skin. There are other waste products in blood as a result of body cells burning nutrients for energy, particularly proteins. The functions of the urinary system are to remove waste products from blood and to maintain water and electrolyte balance and acid–base balance within the body. The structures of the urinary system are shown in FIGURES [32.29](#f0150) and [32.30](#f0155). Note the relation of the bladder, urethra, and kidneys to the other pelvic structures, shown in FIGURES [32.31](#f0160) and [32.32](#f0165).

The kidneys are two bean-shaped organs located just posterior to the abdominal cavity. They lie against the muscles of the back on each side of the spine and are protected by the lower edge of the rib cage. The kidneys perform the following six tasks:

* 1. They act as a filtration system for blood by removing waste products such as urea, uric acid, ammonia, and creatinine (all waste products from protein metabolism) from the blood.
* 2. They regulate blood volume by determining the amount of water that is excreted. Excretion is the elimination of a waste substance from the body.
* 3. They help regulate the electrolyte content of the blood and body fluids.

* 6. They produce a hormone (erythropoietin) that stimulates the production and regulation of red blood cells.

Each kidney has about a million tiny nephrons, which are the filtering units of the kidney (see [FIGURE 32.30)](#f0155)
. The nephrons’ job is to regulate the concentration of water and soluble (dissolvable) substances by filtering the blood. Nephrons separate the nutrients and minerals in blood (water, sodium, amino acids, glucose) from toxins and waste products and produce urine as a waste product. The actual production of urine is a process known as diuresis, which consists of three major steps:

* 1. Glomular filtration. Every nephron has a renal (convoluted) tubule that ends in a Bowman’s capsule. A cluster of capillaries, called the glomerulus, is contained in this capsule. Blood moves through the capillaries of the glomerulus, and the liquid portion of the blood (mostly water) is filtered out into the Bowman’s capsule along with waste products as well as valuable nutrients, minerals, and any substances small enough to cross the membrane.
* 2. Tubular reabsorption. The filtered fluid runs through a series of tubules, where most of the water and other necessary substances are reabsorbed by blood and recirculated in the body.
* 3. Tubular secretion. Additional substances are secreted into the urine from the blood, making final small adjustments to the composition of the blood and urine. Toxins and waste products stay in the tubules and form urine. Urine flows through the tubule to a collecting tubule, which drains into the renal pelvis in the kidney.

Urine is transported from the kidneys to the bladder through the ureters by muscular peristalsis. The two ureters are narrow tubes, each about 25 to 30 cm (10 to 12 in.) long. They run from the renal pelvis of each kidney to the urinary bladder. The bladder is a hollow, muscular sac situated toward the front in the lower part of the abdominal cavity.

Urine is stored in the bladder until the urge to urinate is felt, usually when there is about 250 mL (8 oz, or a half-pint) of urine in the bladder. Urine passes from the bladder through the urethra. The opening at the end of the urethra is the meatus. Urine passes from
the body through the meatus. In the adult with male genitalia, the urethra is about 20 cm (8 in.) long and provides an exit for urine, as well as for semen during ejaculation. In the adult with female genitalia, the urethra is about 3 cm (approximately 1.2 in.); its only purpose is to provide a means of urine to pass from the bladder to the urinary meatus. The differences in the length of the urethra and the relative anatomical positions of the urethra between those with male and female genitalia explain why those with female genitalia are more prone to urinary tract infections than those with male genitalia (see [BOX 29.3](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml), on page 641).

Urine is a clear, yellowish fluid. The amount of urine eliminated over a 24-hour period should be close to the amount of fluid consumed. The average output of urine is 1 500 mL per 24 hours (see [Chapter 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml)).

## The Reproductive System

Human reproduction results from the union of a female sex cell and a male sex cell. The differing structures of the male reproductive system and the female reproductive system allow for the process of reproduction.

### The Male Reproductive System

The structures of the male reproductive system are shown in [FIGURE 32.31, A](#f0160). The testes (testicles) are the male sex glands, also called gonads. The two testes, oval- or almond-shaped glands, are where the male sex cells, called sperm, are produced. Testosterone, the male hormone, is also produced in the testes. This hormone is needed for the functioning of the reproductive organs and for the development of the male secondary sex characteristics (see [Chapter 31](../B9780323709392000316/B9780323709392000316.xhtml)). The testes are suspended between the thighs at the base of the penis in a sac called the scrotum, which is made of skin and muscle.

Sperm travel from each testis to the epididymis, a coiled tube (6 m or 20 ft. in length) on the top and to the side of the testis. From the epididymis, sperm travel through a tube called the vas deferens. Eventually, each vas deferens joins a seminal vesicle. The two seminal vesicles store sperm and produce semen, a fluid that carries sperm from the male reproductive tract. The ducts of the seminal vesicles unite to form the ejaculatory duct, which passes through the prostate gland.

The prostate gland, shaped like a doughnut, lies just below the bladder and secretes fluid into the semen. This fluid plays a role in increasing the mobility of sperm. As the ejaculatory ducts leave the prostate, they join into the urethra, which also runs through the prostate. The urethra, contained within the penis, is the outlet for both urine and semen.

The penis is outside of the body and contains erectile tissue. During sexual arousal, blood fills the erectile tissue, causing the penis to become enlarged, hard, and erect. The erect penis can enter the vagina of the female reproductive tract. The semen, which contains sperm, is then released into the vagina during the act of sexual intercourse.

### The Female Reproductive System

The structures of the female reproductive system are shown in [FIGURE 32.32](#f0165). The female gonads are two almond-shaped glands called ovaries. Ovaries are situated on each side of the uterus in the pelvic cavity and contain ova, or eggs, which are the female sex cells. During the reproductive years, one ovum (egg) is released monthly, which is called ovulation. The ovaries also secrete the female hormones estrogen and progesterone. These hormones are needed for the functioning of the reproductive system and the development of secondary sex characteristics in the individual with female genitalia (see [Chapter 31](../B9780323709392000316/B9780323709392000316.xhtml)).

When an ovum is released from an ovary, it travels through the fallopian tube, which is situated on either side of the uterus and attached to it at one end. The uterus is a hollow, muscular organ shaped like
a pear and located in the centre of the pelvic cavity behind the bladder and in front of the rectum. The main part of the uterus is the fundus. The neck, or narrow section, of the uterus is the cervix. Tissue lining the uterus is called the endometrium. There are numerous blood vessels in the endometrium. If the sex cells from the male and the female (semen and ovum) unite into one cell, that cell implants itself into the endometrium, where it develops into a fetus. The uterus serves as a place for the developing baby (fetus) to receive nourishment and grow.

![Diagram of external female genitalia shows label for mons pubis, clitoris, labia minora, urethra, labia majora, vagina, and anus.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000328/main.assets/f32-33-9780323711654.jpg)

The cervix of the uterus projects into a muscular canal called the vagina. The vagina opens to the outside of the body and is located just behind the urethra. The vagina receives the penis during sexual intercourse and also serves as part of the birth canal. Glands in the vaginal wall keep it moistened with secretions. In young girls, the external vaginal opening is partially closed by a membrane called the hymen, which can break for various reasons.

The external genitalia of the female are referred to as the vulva ([FIGURE 32.33](#f0170)). The mons pubis is a rounded, fatty pad over a bone called the symphysis pubis and is covered with hair in the adult female. The labia majora and the labia minora are two folds of tissue on either side of the vaginal opening. The clitoris is a small, sensitive organ composed of erectile tissue and becomes hard when sexually stimulated.

The mammary glands are contained in the female breasts and produce and secrete milk after childbirth. They are made up of glandular tissue and fat ([FIGURE 32.34](#f0175)). The milk drains into ducts that open into the nipple.

#### Menstruation

The endometrium is rich in blood to nourish the cell that grows into the fetus. If pregnancy does not occur, the endometrium breaks down and is discharged out of the body through the vagina. This process, called menstruation, occurs about every 28 days and is therefore called the menstrual cycle.

The first day of the cycle begins with menstruation. Blood flows out from the uterus through the vaginal opening, usually for 3 to 7 days. During the next phase—ovulation—which occurs usually on or about day 14 of the cycle, an ovum matures in an ovary and is released.

Meanwhile, estrogen and progesterone (the female hormones) are secreted by the ovaries. These hormones cause the endometrium to thicken in preparation for possible pregnancy. If pregnancy does not occur, the hormones decrease in amount, and, because of this, blood supply to the endometrium decreases. The endometrium then breaks down and is discharged through the vagina, and another menstrual cycle begins.

#### Fertilization

For reproduction to occur, a male sex cell (sperm) must unite with a female sex cell (ovum). The uniting of the sperm and the ovum into one cell is called fertilization. A sperm and an ovum have only 23 chromosomes each, which is exactly half the number that all other cells have. When the two sex cells unite, the fertilized cell has 46 chromosomes.

During intercourse, millions of sperm are deposited in the vagina. Sperm travel up the cervix, through the uterus, and into the fallopian tubes. If a sperm and an ovum unite in the fallopian tube, fertilization occurs and may result in pregnancy. The fertilized ovum then travels down the fallopian tube to the uterus. After a short time, the fertilized cell implants itself in the thick endometrium and grows during pregnancy.

## Chapter review

Key points

• The anatomical position is the body standing erect, with the feet shoulder-width apart, face forward, arms at the sides, and palms of the hands facing forward. In an illustration, it contains terms that are used in the study of anatomy related to the body.

• Cells are the basic unit of body structures. Groups of cells with similar functions combine to form tissues.

• Organs are groups of tissues that work together to perform a special function. Organ systems are formed by organs that work together to perform special functions.

• The major organ systems in the body include the following:

• The integumentary system, which contains skin and its appendages—hair, nails, and sweat and oil glands

• The musculoskeletal system, which provides the framework for the body and allows the body to move

• The nervous system, which controls, directs, and coordinates body functions, including the central nervous system, the peripheral nervous system, and the sense organs that control the five senses

• The endocrine system, which is made up of glands called endocrine glands, which secrete hormones into the bloodstream, to regulate the activities of other organs and glands in the body; the metabolic processes involving fats, proteins, and carbohydrates; water and electrolyte balance; cell growth and development; and numerous chemical components in cells and body fluids

• The circulatory system, which is made up of the cardiovascular system (consisting of blood, the heart, and blood vessels) and the lymphatic system (consisting of lymph, lymph nodes, and lymph vessels)

• The respiratory system, which brings air into the lungs by inspiration (inhalation), which is a process of ventilation, and allows the transfer of oxygen into the blood and the transfer of carbon dioxide out of the blood; the carbon dioxide is eliminated by expiration (exhalation), which is a process of ventilation

• The immune system, which defends the body against threats from inside and outside

• The digestive system, which breaks down food physically and chemically into basic nutrients so that they can be absorbed for use by cells

• The urinary system, which removes waste from blood and maintains water and electrolyte balance

• The reproductive system, which allows for the processes of development and reproduction

## Communicable Diseases

A pathogen is a microbe that can cause illness, infection, or disease (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)). A communicable disease or illness is caused when disease-causing pathogens are transmitted from one person to another by direct contact, indirect contact, airborne transmission, vehicle transmission, or vector transmission (see [TABLE 19.1](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)
: Modes of Transmission of Microorganisms on page 788) and usually spread easily. They are also known as communicable illnesses, infectious illnesses, or infectious diseases.

There are a number of communicable diseases that are more common in adults. Communicable diseases that are included in this textbook include COVID-19 (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)), H1N1, hepatitis, sexually transmitted infections (STIs) (see pages 764–766); influenza and tuberculosis (TB), which are discussed under Respiratory System Disorders (page 797); and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV and AIDS), which is discussed under Immune System Disorders

### Three Possible Outcomes of Exposure to a Pathogen

Being exposed to pathogens does not always result in an infection, because the body can protect itself from infection. There are three possible outcomes after exposure to a pathogen:

* 1.
  The immune system destroys the pathogen.
   The pathogen does not live and grow within the body, so an infection does not occur (see [Chapter 32](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml) to review discussion about the immune system). If the person has been infected by that pathogen previously, their body may have “immunological memory,” thereby fighting the infection without causing symptoms.
* 2.
  The immune system does not destroy the pathogen, and yet an infection does not develop.
   Some people remain healthy because of a strong immune response. They are healthy and have no other illnesses or infections to deal with, but the pathogen can still be present within the body and can be transmitted to others; these persons would be considered carriers. The pathogens are not harmful to these persons but might be harmful to others who have a weakened immune system for certain reasons, such as fatigue, stress, malnourishment, or other illnesses.
* 3.
  An infection develops sometime after exposure to the pathogen.
   The period between exposure to the pathogen and the onset (beginning) of illness is called the incubation period, which may be relatively short (hours to days) or may last for years. During the incubation period, the person can transfer the pathogen to others. The time during which a person is at risk of transferring pathogens to others (putting people at risk of “catching” the illness) is known as the communicable phase. The incubation period and length of time of the communicable phase vary from pathogen to pathogen.

### Vaccinations as a Preventative Measure

Vaccinations have been proven to prevent communicable (contagious) illnesses from developing into more serious illness for people who have been vaccinated against them. There are a number of common communicable childhood illnesses (such as measles, mumps, and chickenpox) that babies and toddlers can be vaccinated against. Updated immunization schedules are available from family doctors, local health units, or the Public Health Agency of Canada website (<http://www.phac-aspc.gc.ca/publicat/cig-gci/p01-12-eng.php#tab1>).

#### Pandemic Prevention

A pandemic occurs when a communicable disease or infection spreads quickly throughout the population of a country, or even throughout the world, as with COVID-19. Fortunately, it is believed that people who are exposed to different strains of the influenza (“the flu”) virus frequently during their lives (such as through the annual influenza vaccine) will develop some protection against future infections. This protection results because viruses usually mutate only some of the proteins on their surface each year. Most adults usually have memory cells that will recognize at least some of the proteins of future influenza strains, which allows people who have been vaccinated to recover quickly.

However, for unknown reasons, three to four times in a century, a radical change takes place in a virus (such as influenza A), causing a new strain to emerge.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur331) In other circumstances, an entirely new virus develops in the world. Unfortunately, we have no memory cells for any new virus antigen. This status can lead our immune systems to overreact when a new virus emerges, resulting in widespread illness and deaths, especially among the young, the ill, and the very old. This occurred with the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) epidemic in 2002 and 2003 and has occurred among people of all ages, particularly among the very old and persons with compromised immunity during the COVID-19 pandemic (see the Think About Safety: COVID-19 and Its Effect on the World box, on page 385). In such cases, even young, healthy people can die from influenza, as they did during the flu pandemic of 1918.

In hopes of preventing or minimizing a future influenza pandemic in Canada, the federal government, in cooperation with public health departments across Canada, provides widespread health teaching and influenza vaccinations on a yearly basis. In regard to the COVID-19 pandemic, there is a worldwide effort underway to develop a safe vaccine that can be distributed to all people. The influenza vaccine does not provide immunity from COVID-19.

#### Recommended or Required Vaccinations for Health Care Workers

Health care agencies require their workers to have all routine vaccinations up to date before they start work. There are a number of vaccinations that health care workers are recommended (and sometimes required) to get. They include:

1. •
   Yearly influenza vaccination.
    This may be required at many agencies. This vaccine is very important to protect both health care workers and clients from influenza, which can be very dangerous to older persons and people who have weakened immune systems.
2. •
   Hepatitis B vaccination.
    This vaccination is given in three injections over 6 months. It protects against getting hepatitis B from clients. The antibody levels can be checked periodically after vaccination to ensure immunity to hepatitis B.
3. •
   Hepatitis A vaccination. Some health care facilities recommend that all employees be vaccinated for hepatitis A (usually given along with hepatitis
   B vaccine), especially if the workers handle food. Check with your local health unit (or employer) for more information about this vaccine.
4. •
   Skin testing to check for exposure to tuberculosis (TB).
    TB is a highly infectious bacterial illness. As a support worker, it is very important that you inform your doctor if you think you may have been exposed to a client or family member suspected of having TB. Some older people now living in long-term care facilities might have been exposed to TB in their youth, when TB infection was much more common. New staff and residents of long-term care facilities are required to have a two-step TB skin test (or a chest X-ray, if the doctor thinks it is necessary) so that they do not risk contracting TB or spreading it to others.

### COVID-19

COVID-19 is a highly contagious, airbourne, communicable viral disease that is spread mainly through close contact from person to person, including between people who are physically near each other (within about 2 m [6 ft]). Symptoms may take up to 14 days after exposure to a person infected with COVID-19 to appear (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml) for a list of symptoms).[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur335) A person with the virus can infect others even if the infected person shows no symptoms. People who have not yet developed symptoms are considered presymptomatic, and those who never develop symptoms are considered asymptomatic.

How easily the virus spreads can vary from person to person:[6](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur336)

1. • People who are physically near (within 2 m [6 ft]) a person with COVID-19 or have direct contact with that person are at greatest risk of infection. Infection occurs mainly through exposure to respiratory droplets.
2. • When people with COVID-19 cough, sneeze, sing, talk, or simply breathe, they produce respiratory droplets. These droplets can range in size from larger droplets (some of which are visible) to smaller droplets. Small droplets can also form particles when they dry very quickly in the airstream.
3. • Respiratory droplets cause infection when they are inhaled or deposited on mucous membranes, such as those that line the inside of the nose and mouth.
4. • As the respiratory droplets travel further from the person with COVID-19, the concentration of these droplets decreases. Larger droplets fall out of the air due to gravity. Smaller droplets and particles spread apart in the air.
5. • With passing time, the amount of infectious virus in respiratory droplets also decreases.
6. • Some infections can be spread by exposure to virus in small droplets and particles that can linger in the air for minutes to hours. These viruses may be able to infect people who are further than 2 m (6 ft) away from the person who is infected or after that person has left the space.
7. • This kind of spread is referred to as airborne transmission and is an important way that infections like tuberculosis, measles, and chicken pox are spread.
8. • In some situations, people with COVID-19 have infected others who were more than 2 m (6 ft) away. These transmissions have tended to occur within enclosed spaces that had inadequate ventilation. Sometimes the infected person was breathing heavily, for example, while singing or exercising.
9. • Under these circumstances, the amount of infectious smaller droplets and particles produced by the person with COVID-19 became concentrated enough to spread the virus to other people. Those who became infected were in the same space with the person with COVID-19, either at the same time or shortly after the person with COVID-19 had left.
10. • Respiratory droplets can also land on surfaces and objects. It is possible that a person could get COVID-19 by touching a surface or object that has virus on it and then touching their own mouth, nose, or eyes, although spread from touching surfaces is not thought to be a common way that COVID-19 spreads.
11. • It appears that the virus that causes COVID-19 can spread from people to animals in some situations,
    though rarely. A small number of pets worldwide, including cats and dogs, have been reported to be infected with COVID-19, mostly after close contact with people with COVID-19.

Although its first patients were identified in 2019, COVID-19 was officially identified in 2020 as a new active virus, and researchers around the world are still studying the potential medium-term and long-term effects it can have on people who get sick from the virus. According to the Mayo Clinic, documented adverse effects of COVID-19 include the following:[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur337)

* 1.
  Organ damage to the heart, lungs, and brain
* 1. • Heart. Imaging tests taken months after recovery from COVID-19 have shown lasting damage to the heart muscle, even in people who experienced only mild COVID-19 symptoms. This may increase the risk of heart failure or other heart complications in the future.
  2. • Lungs. The type of pneumonia often associated with COVID-19 can cause long-standing damage to the tiny air sacs (alveoli) in the lungs. The resulting scar tissue can lead to long-term breathing problems.
  3. • Brain. Even in young people, COVID-19 can cause strokes, seizures, and Guillain-Barré syndrome—a condition that causes temporary paralysis. COVID-19 may also increase the risk of developing Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease.
* 2. Blood clots and blood vessel problems
* 1. • COVID-19 can make blood cells more likely to clump up and form clots. While large clots can cause heart attacks and strokes, much of the heart damage caused by COVID-19 is believed to stem from very small clots that block tiny blood vessels (capillaries) in the heart muscle.

* 3. Problems with mood and fatigue
* 1. • People who have severe symptoms of COVID-19 often have to be treated in a hospital’s critical care unit, with mechanical assistance such as ventilators to breathe. Simply surviving this experience can make a person more likely to later develop post-traumatic stress syndrome (PTSD; see [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml)), depression, and anxiety.

Also, because it is difficult to predict long-term outcomes from this new COVID-19 virus, scientists are looking at the long-term effects seen in related viruses, such as SARS.

Many people who have recovered from SARS have gone on to develop chronic fatigue syndrome, a complex disorder characterized by extreme fatigue that worsens with physical or mental activity but does not improve with rest. The same may be true for some people who have had COVID-19; such individuals are referred to as long-haulers. Much is still unknown about how COVID-19 will affect people over time. However, researchers recommend that doctors closely monitor people who have had COVID-19 to see how their organs are functioning after recovery.

### Hepatitis

Hepatitis is a disease that can cause inflammation (itis) of the liver (hepat). It may be caused by bacterial or viral infection, by parasitic infestation, through use of alcohol or drugs, exposure to toxins, transfusion of incompatible (not matching) blood type, by consuming contaminated water or food, using dirty needles or syringes, or practising unsafe sex. Scientists have identified six hepatitis viruses, but three—known as A, B, and C—cause about 90% of all acute hepatitis cases in Canada.

People infected with hepatitis can experience effects ranging from mild illness to serious liver damage. Many people recover completely from an infection, while some become carriers of the disease and can spread it to others unknowingly. It is especially important that women who are pregnant or are trying to become pregnant get tested for hepatitis.

When the viral infection of the liver first occurs, it is called acute hepatitis, which may or may not cause recognizable symptoms, such as nausea, vomiting, and pain in the abdomen. Acute hepatitis often lasts less than 1 or 2 months. After about 2 weeks, dark urine and jaundice (a yellowish colour in the skin and whites of the eyes) develop in some, but not all, clients. Some clients with hepatitis have light-coloured stools, muscle pain, drowsiness, irritability, and itching, as well as diarrhea and general ache in the joints accompanied by redness and swelling.

For mild cases of acute viral hepatitis, no medication or other treatment is available or necessary. The disease runs its course and resolves by itself. The primary goals for managing acute viral hepatitis are providing good nutrition, preventing additional damage to the liver, and preventing transmission of the infection to others (also see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)).

Some cases of acute hepatitis do not resolve. If hepatitis lasts longer than 6 months, it is called chronic hepatitis. Chronic hepatitis causes liver damage over a long period of time. The normal cells of the liver are eventually replaced with scar tissue—a condition known as cirrhosis, which can cause complete liver failure. When the liver fails, the client will need life-saving liver transplantation.

Hepatitis viruses A, B, and C can all be passed along from one person to another. Vaccines are now available for protection against hepatitis A and B, and a vaccine against hepatitis C is currently being developed.

#### Hepatitis A

Hepatitis A virus is spread by the fecal–oral route; that is, the virus is transmitted when traces of feces are ingested. Food, water, and drinking and eating vessels (cups, bowls, and cutlery) can be contaminated with feces. The virus is ingested when contaminated food or water is consumed or when a person eats or drinks from a contaminated vessel. Common food
sources of hepatitis A include contaminated water or ice cubes, raw or undercooked shellfish, or raw fruits and vegetables. Risk factors include crowded living conditions and poor sanitation and hygiene.

Clients who have fecal incontinence, confusion, or dementia can cause contamination. As a support worker caring for such clients, it is important to look carefully for contaminated items and areas.

People infected with hepatitis A can have a wide range of symptoms; 10 to 15% of people who do not show symptoms can still carry the disease for up to 6 months and spread it to other people, foods, or surfaces. Others may experience symptoms like fatigue and jaundice. If you think that you have been exposed to the hepatitis A virus, see your doctor immediately. Vaccination can prevent the onset of symptoms if it is given within 2 weeks of exposure.

Children often do not show symptoms of hepatitis (see the Focus on Children: Hepatitis box).

Hepatitis

Hepatitis A is more common among preschool and school-aged children than among adults because the virus spreads easily among children. Poor hygiene practices after defecation lead to contamination of eating and drinking vessels, toys, and other surfaces. Also, young children often put their hands, toys, and other items in their mouths.

Symptoms of hepatitis A are usually mild, and the illness usually lasts 1 to 2 weeks, although severe cases can last several months. Most people recover without treatment. It is rare for healthy adults to die from hepatitis A, and the illness does not become chronic. For pregnant women, hepatitis A is more serious and can be fatal, particularly for women in their third trimester. If you have been diagnosed with hepatitis A, or any other gastrointestinal illness, do not prepare food or pour water for other people.

There is currently an approved vaccine for hepatitis A in Canada. If you are immune to hepatitis A (because you already had the virus and your body now has a resistance to it, or you have been vaccinated), this immunity will last your entire life.

It is important to follow food safety standards and policies from Health Canada to help minimize the risk of foodborne illnesses such as hepatitis A.

#### Hepatitis B[8](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur338)

Hepatitis B is a liver disease caused by the hepatitis B virus (HBV). HBV is far more infectious than HIV and infection can be prevented by a vaccine. HBV is spread through contact with infected blood and body fluids, including semen and vaginal fluid.

People who have not been vaccinated may be at risk of getting infected. About 95% of adults will recover within 6 months of becoming infected (acute hepatitis B) and as a result will develop lifelong protection against it. The remaining 5% are unable to clear the virus and will become chronically infected. Chronic hepatitis B infection is treatable.

It is estimated that less than 1% of Canada’s population is infected with either acute or chronic HBV. People who are infected before the age of 7 years are at a higher risk of developing chronic infection. In 2011, the overall reported rate of acute HBV infection in Canada was 0.6 reported cases per 100 000 people living in Canada.

Symptoms of HBV infection can include some or all of the following: fatigue, loss of appetite, fever, nausea, vomiting, dark urine, pale stools, stomach pain, joint pain and jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes). About half of the people infected with HBV do not develop any symptoms until their liver has already been damaged. This is why it is important to take precautions against HBV infection and to get tested if you think you might be infected. Many people infected with HBV do not know they have the virus because symptoms can take 2 to 6 months to appear, and only about 50% of people develop symptoms. During this time, the infection can be spread to others. Many people are not aware they have this infection until liver damage has already occurred. Potential complications from chronic HBV infection include cirrhosis of the liver, liver failure, liver cancer, and premature death.

In Canada, all provinces and territories have free immunization programs for children and certain groups of adults which protect against hepatitis B infection. Women who are pregnant and infected with hepatitis B need to know that their infant is at a high risk of developing chronic HBV infection.
In Canada, it is recommended that infants born to infected mothers receive a special injection immediately after birth, as well as the first dose of vaccine within 12 hours of birth to help prevent infection.

#### Hepatitis C

Hepatitis C is a liver disease caused by the hepatitis C virus (HCV). It is spread through contact with infected blood and bodily fluids, such as semen and vaginal fluid. Infection can only take place if the virus enters a person’s bloodstream. In Canada, most people are infected by unsafe sexual practices; receiving contaminated blood transfusions or organs; using or sharing contaminated drug paraphernalia; receiving body services in which unclean tools or work practices were used, such as tattooing, acupuncture, or body piercing; or by sharing personal care items with an infected person, such as toothbrushes, razors or scissors.

HCV can be passed to the infant during pregnancy, childbirth, or through breastfeeding if the nipples are cracked and bleeding and the baby also has bleeding in or on the mouth.

Hepatitis C is not spread through breast milk, food, or water, or by casual contact such as kissing, hugging, or shaking hands. There is currently no vaccine that will protect against the transmission of HCV.

A list of safety precautions that can reduce the transmission of hepatitis is provided in [BOX 33.1](B978032370939200033X.xhtml#b0010).

### Herpes Simplex Virus

Although herpes simplex viruses (HSVs) are sometimes classified as an STI, they can also be spread without sexual activity ([BOX 33.2](B978032370939200033X.xhtml#b0015)). In that case, the viruses are spread by skin, saliva, or touch of the infected area to other parts of the host’s body or other
people. An infected person can shed the virus even if there is no sore present. If spread to another person, symptoms will occur approximately 3 days to 1 week after exposure. People experiencing a new HSV infection may develop a fever, tiredness, loss of appetite, and localized pain and/or burning at the site before any lesions appear.[10](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur340) People who experience recurrent HSV infections may experience swollen glands and develop sores that are painful and fluid-filled. The sores and surrounding skin may also itch, burn, or tingle.

Herpes simplex viruses (HSV) are the viruses that cause illnesses such as fever blisters or cold sores, as well as genital herpes (see page 764). Herpes varicella-zoster is a virus that causes both chicken pox and shingles (see pages 759 and 773)
.

Both the herpes simplex and herpes zoster viruses originate from the Herpesvirus family. The viruses can also become dormant (hibernate) for long periods of time, but they can become reactivated if the person becomes immunocompromised.

Because of their genetic similarities, the medical treatment for these viruses is similar. They can be treated with antiviral medications and medication for the discomfort they cause.

HSV can go into periods of dormancy (similar to hibernation). For many people, outbreaks (reappearance of the sores and other symptoms) can be brought on by the following conditions:

1. • General illness (from mild illnesses to serious conditions)
2. • Fatigue
3. • Physical or emotional stress
4. • Lowered immunity from AIDS or some medications (such as chemotherapy or steroids)
5. • Trauma to the affected area, including sexual activity
6. • Menstruation[12](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur342)

## Cancer

Throughout the body, cells reproduce for tissue growth and repair and usually divide in an orderly and controlled way. Cancer is defined as a group of diseases characterized by out-of-control cell division and growth leading to the development of a lump or mass of cells. Cancer can start in any body system.

A new growth of abnormal cells is called a tumour. Tumours can be benign (noncancerous) or malignant (cancerous). Benign tumours grow slowly and are contained in one area. They do not usually cause death. Malignant tumours grow rapidly, often invade other tissues, may go on to affect several body systems ([FIGURE 33.2](sec-s0080.xhtml#f0015)), and can cause the person to die.

![A. Benign tumours growing in limited area. B. Malignant tumours spreading in other tissues.](../../IMAGES/B978032370939200033X/main.assets/f33-02-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 33.2 A, Benign tumours grow within a localized area. B, Malignant tumours invade other tissues.

Metastasis is the spread of cancer to other body parts ([FIGURE 33.3](sec-s0080.xhtml#f0020)). Cancer cells break off from the tumour and travel to other body parts, where new tumours grow. Death occurs if the cancer cannot be treated or controlled.

Common cancer sites are the lungs, breasts, prostate, colon and rectum, uterus, urinary tract, and skin (see page 769 for description of skin cancers). Cancer is the second leading cause of death in Canada and occurs in all age groups.[13](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur343) In 2020, one out of every four cancers diagnosed in Canadian women is breast cancer, and 13% of all cancer deaths in women will be from breast cancer.[14](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur344) Prostate cancer is the most common cancer among Canadian men (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancer). It is the third leading cause of death from cancer in men in Canada.[15](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur345) One type of leukemia (acute lymphoblastic leukemia, a type of blood cancer) is the most common cancer in children.[16](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur346) The warning signs of cancer identified by the Canadian Cancer Society are listed in [BOX 33.3](sec-s0080.xhtml#b0020).

### Treatment

Treatment depends on the type of tumour, its location and size, and whether it has spread. Treatment is most successful when the cancer is detected as early as possible. One or many treatments may be used, with the following goals:

1. • Cure the cancer
2. • Keep the cancer from spreading
3. • Slow the cancer’s growth
4. • Relieve symptoms caused by the cancer

The three most common cancer treatments are surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. Stem cell transplantation has been used to treat some types of cancers, especially those affecting bone marrow.

Surgery to remove tumours is localized (contained to one area) and involves the removal of malignant tissue as well as surrounding tissue and lymph nodes that might contain cancer cells. If the tumour has not spread to other areas, surgery has a greater chance of success.

Radiation therapy destroys living cells and, like surgery, is used for treating localized cancers. Radiation therapy involves use of high-energy rays directed at the tumour to destroy cancer cells. It can be used alone or in combination with surgery and chemotherapy. Treatment is usually given over a period of weeks. During treatment, normal cells can be destroyed along with cancer cells. Adverse effects of radiation therapy include discomfort, nausea and vomiting, fatigue (tiredness), anorexia (loss of appetite), and diarrhea. Skin breakdown can occur in the area being radiated, so the physician may order special skin care procedures. The markings drawn on the body to show where the radiation will be administered should not be washed off.

Chemotherapy (also called as cytotoxic therapy) involves powerful drugs that enter the bloodstream where they can target and kill cancer cells throughout the body. These drugs can be administered orally or intravenously. Like radiation, chemotherapy affects normal cells as well as cancer cells, so adverse effects can be severe. The digestive tract may be irritated, and nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea may result. Some other adverse effects are inflammation of the mouth (stomatitis) and hair loss (alopecia), and because of decreased production of blood cells, the client may tire easily and is at risk for bruising, bleeding, and infection.

Stem cell transplantation involves infusion of healthy stem cells into the body. Stem cell transplantation may be necessary if bone marrow stops working and does not produce enough healthy stem cells. The procedure can help the body make enough healthy white blood cells, red blood cells, or platelets and reduce the risk of life-threatening infections, anemia, and bleeding. This procedure is also known as bone marrow transplantation or umbilical cord blood transplantation, depending on the source of the stem cells. Stem cell transplantation can use cells from the client’s own body (autologous stem cell transplantation) or stem cells from donors (allogenic stem cell transplantation). The procedure is not widely available across the country, but certain cancer centres are undertaking trial cases.

### Skin Cancer

The two most common types of skin cancer are basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma; both types are unlikely to spread to other parts of the body. The most dangerous type of skin cancer is malignant melanoma, which can be fatal if not treated early.

Basal cell carcinoma is the most common type of skin cancer, accounting for 90% of all skin cancers. It usually presents as a raised, smooth, pearly bump and occurs on an area that has been exposed to the sun, such as the neck or shoulders. Small blood vessels may be seen in the tumour. Frequently, crusting and bleeding occur in the centre of the tumour, so it can be mistaken for a sore that does not heal.[17](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur347)

Squamous cell carcinoma is the second most common type of skin cancer. It starts in the epidermis as a red, scaly, thickened patch on sun-exposed skin, eventually penetrating the underlying tissue if not treated. Bleeding and ulceration can occur. It is easily treated when found early. In a small percentage of cases or if it is not treated, this cancer spreads (metastasizes) to other parts of the body.

Malignant melanoma is a less common but highly dangerous form of skin cancer. It is the eighth most common form of cancer in Canada.[18](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur348) It is caused by excessive exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light from either the sun or tanning beds and from repeated sunburns. Prevention measures include avoiding overexposure to the sun and using a broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 15 or greater throughout the year when outdoors.

All melanomas look different. Signs of a melanoma include the following mole traits:

1. • More than one colour (may be shades of black, brown, or blue or sometimes white, grey, red, or pink)
2. • Asymmetrical
3. • Irregular border or jagged edges
4. • More than 6 mm in diameter
5. • Itchy
6. • Changes in texture
7. • Oozing
8. • Bleeding

Early detection is critical. The appearance of a new mole during adulthood should be checked by a physician.

Treatment of skin cancer usually includes removal of the lesion, ensuring that the edges are free of cancer cells. The lesion may be removed by surgery, radiation therapy, or cryotherapy (freezing off the lesion).

### Common Skin Disorders

Many skin disorders cause discomfort to clients (also refer to [Chapter 22](../B9780323709392000225/B9780323709392000225.xhtml)). Treatments for skin disorders may seem very easy to do, and you may have done the treatment at home on your own family, but remember that you cannot perform any treatment that is not within your scope of practice. [FIGURE 33.5](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030) shows some common skin disorders, discussed in more detail next.

#### Athlete’s Foot

Athlete’s foot is a highly contagious fungal skin infection that causes the affected foot to peel, burn, itch, and turn red ([FIGURE 33.5, A](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). It is easily spread through contact in moist areas, such as locker rooms or showers, or by exchanging footwear. It can be treated by drying the foot thoroughly, including between the toes, and by using over-the-counter (OTC) medications or, in severe cases, prescription medications.

#### Boil

A furuncle, or boil, is a skin disorder caused by the infection of a hair follicle, usually by a type of bacterium (a disease-causing organism) called Staphylococcus. Boils appear as red, pus-filled lumps, which are painful ([FIGURE 33.5, B](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). The most common sites for boils are the back, underarms, shoulders, thighs, and buttocks. When the boil is ready to drain or discharge the pus, a yellow or white point will appear in its middle. The care plan may include applying warm compresses to the boil, a process referred to as “bringing it to a head.” You cannot predict when the boil may break, so use Standard Practices when providing care to the area of the boil. Your supervisor will inform you of the nursing care required when the boil drains.

#### Cyst

A cyst is an abnormal, closed sac that can occur anywhere in the body. The sac, which may contain air, fluids, or semi-solid material, has a distinct membrane enclosing it ([FIGURE 33.5, C](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). Once the cyst has formed, it will remain in the tissue unless it is
removed by surgery or by taking medication to dissolve the sac.

#### Dermatitis

Dermatitis is an inflammation of the skin caused by direct contact with an irritating or allergy-causing substance ([FIGURE 33.5, D](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). Contact dermatitis can be caused by exposure to a material that the person has become allergic to. Some other common allergens that can cause dermatitis are poison ivy, poison oak, latex, cosmetics, perfumes, and some plants. Symptoms can include itching, skin redness, skin tenderness, swelling, or warmth of the exposed area.

Irritant dermatitis can be caused by reaction to materials such as soaps, detergents, or chemicals and usually resembles a burn. Your role as the support worker will be to follow the care plan and ensure that further exposure to the irritant is prevented.

#### Eczema

Eczema is an inflammatory condition of the skin. Eczema may look different from person to person. In most cases, it is characterized by dry, red, and extremely itchy patches of skin ([FIGURE 33.5, E](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). In
other cases, it can involve papular (solid elevation of the skin) or vesicular (solid elevation of the skin containing fluid) lesions. Eczema occurs in both children and adults and often first appears in infancy. Areas of high perspiration and creases of the elbows and knees are often prone to skin breakdown from eczema. Treatment usually focuses on preventing scratching. The client’s care plan will tell you what treatments are to be carried out. One of the most common treatments is to apply lotions or creams to keep the skin as moist as possible. Eczema is a persistent condition, and research is ongoing to find medications to control outbreaks.

#### Impetigo

Impetigo is a contagious skin disorder that is caused by either Streptococcus or Staphylococcus bacteria. When a person scratches a cut or scratch that has become infected and touches another part of the body, the bacteria can spread. The infected area forms a crust, which then drains, thus also spreading the bacteria ([FIGURE 33.5, F](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). The usual treatment for small areas of infection is application of an antibiotic ointment. If the impetigo is widespread, an oral antibiotic may be prescribed.

Impetigo is commonly found on the hands and faces of children. The most common way of acquiring impetigo is by coming into contact with an infected person. If you are caring for a client with impetigo, use Standard Practices when washing around the area. Encourage the client to perform frequent hand hygiene. Washing the client’s linens and dishes separately from others’ will help avoid spreading pathogens.

#### Psoriasis

Psoriasis is a persistent skin disorder that causes red, scaly plaques (patches) on the skin and can be found anywhere on the body ([FIGURE 33.5, G](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). These scaly patches are areas of excessive skin production that accumulate and become inflamed. As the skin deposit accumulates, it takes on a silvery-white appearance. The cause of psoriasis has not yet been identified, but stress and excessive alcohol consumption can aggravate the condition. A client with psoriasis can suffer from physical discomfort, itching, and poor self-image because of the appearance of the skin. Treatments available are numerous and will be different for each client. You will need to follow the client’s care plan for treatment of the affected areas.

#### Scabies

Scabies is a highly contagious skin infection caused by a mite. The mite burrows under the skin to deposit its eggs, causing intense, itchy skin rashes ([FIGURE 33.5, H](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). Scabies is transmitted by skin-to-skin contact between people. Since it takes approximately 4 to 6 weeks for symptoms to develop after the initial infestation, a client can be contagious for at least a month before the condition is diagnosed. As a result, it can spread through a family or a facility. The mites and burrows are difficult to see, so scabies is frequently misdiagnosed as intense itching of the skin before the red papular eruptions form. Treatment usually involves a topical application to kill the mites. Some applications are repeated a week later. Ensure that you follow the manufacturer’s instructions if you are carrying out the treatment. After the treatment, thorough washing of all material that has been in contact with the infected client (such as bedding and clothing) in the past 4 days is recommended.

#### Scleroderma

Scleroderma is a rare, progressive autoimmune disease that involves the hardening (sclero) of the skin (derma) and connective tissue throughout the body. There are two main types of scleroderma:

* 1. Localized scleroderma is less severe and usually affects the skin. Muscles and bones may be affected, but internal organs are not. The lesions on the skin can appear either as hard, oval, white lesions with a purple ring around them or as hard, reddish scales.
* 2. Generalized scleroderma affects both the skin and the internal organs, causing damage to the organs.

There is no cure for scleroderma, and treatment usually involves medications to soften the skin and reduce inflammation. Clients may be self-conscious because of the appearance of their skin. As the support worker, it is important to treat them with
respect. Treatment for the affected internal organs varies, depending on the damage.

#### Seborrheic Keratosis

Seborrheic keratosis is a noncancerous growth that is common in older persons. These growths can appear on many areas of the skin, either alone or in groups. They may be dark or multicoloured, and they usually have a grainy surface, though they can be smooth and waxy ([FIGURE 33.5, I](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). They may be mistaken for moles or skin cancer.

#### Shingles

Shingles is a viral infection caused by a reactivation of the same virus, herpes varicella zoster, that causes chickenpox. It causes a painful rash of raised blisters, which can appear anywhere on the body, usually on one side ([FIGURE 33.5, J](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)), with the most common sites including the trunk, buttocks, and face. Any person who has had chickenpox is at risk of developing shingles. The blister outbreak lasts about 2 weeks. Shingles can cause extreme and sometimes debilitating pain, itching, or tingling along the affected nerve root, whether the rash is present or not. The pain may linger for weeks, months, or even years.

Once a person becomes infected with this virus, it never leaves that person’s body, but it exists in a dormant (inactive) state. Stress, illness, and a weakened immune system can reactivate the virus in the form of shingles, usually travelling along one of the nerve pathways in the body. If the shingles rash appears on the face and spreads to the eye, it can lead to cornea scarring and chronic tearing in the affected eye. Shingles can be spread through skin contact to people who have not acquired immunity to herpes varicella zoster.

Antiviral medications are available that can be prescribed, which are effective for reducing the length and severity of a shingle infection. In addition, there is a vaccine that can prevent a shingles episode.

#### Skin Tag

A skin tag is a small flap of flesh-coloured skin that looks like a droplet or “tag” of skin ([FIGURE 33.5, K](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). Skin tags are not painful but, depending on where they appear, can get caught in jewellery, zippers, or even bra hooks. They are most common on women and on older persons.

#### Urticaria

Urticaria (also known as hives) is a common skin condition characterized by raised, usually itchy wheals ([FIGURE 33.5, L](sec-s0100.xhtml#f0030)). It is commonly caused by an allergic reaction to food (such as shellfish), food additives, certain plants (like poison ivy), or drugs. A wheal is a circular, red, spongy lesion that evolves and changes over minutes to hours. It is usually surrounded by an area of redness called a flare. Urticaria can vary in size from a few millimetres to wheals covering a whole extremity and causing severe itching.

If you observe signs of urticaria on your client, report it immediately to your supervisor. Treatments may include antihistamines as well as topical lotions or creams to relieve the itching. It is important to record what your client has ingested (eaten) and any substances they have been in contact with, such as powders or creams. Urticaria usually disappears in a matter of hours after ingestion.

### Supporting a Client Who Is Living With an Integumentary System Disorder

Clients who are living with an integumentary disorder may feel embarrassed by the physical changes they have noticed. For those people with a contagious illness, they may feel embarrassment and anger that they “caught” something from someone. Besides the suggestions listed within this section, here are some other ways you can best support these clients:

1. •
   Protect yourself from the contagious illness or infection. Wear the proper PPE that is recommended in the client’s care plan. Follow Standard Practices (see [Chapter 19](../B9780323709392000195/B9780323709392000195.xhtml)) at all times. Change out of your uniform before leaving the agency or client’s home to avoid risk of spreading pathogens.
2. •
   Listen to the client and use empathy when speaking to them.
    This conveys respect for the client.
3. •
   Always obey rules of confidentiality. Never discuss the client or details about the client to anyone not involved in the client’s care.

### Fractures

A fracture is a break in the bone. Along with the break, the tissues around the fracture, including muscles, blood vessels, nerves, and tendons, are usually injured. Fractures are open or closed ([FIGURE 33.6](sec-s0185.xhtml#f0035)). A closed fracture (simple fracture) means that the bone is broken but the skin is intact. An open fracture (compound fracture) means that the broken bone has come through the skin.

Fractures are usually caused by falls and other accidents but can also result when bones are weakened by diseases such as cancer, alcoholism, and osteoporosis. Signs and symptoms of a fracture include the following:

1. • Limb appearing bent or out of position
2. • Pain
3. • Swelling
4. • Limited movement of limb or loss of function
5. • Bruising and colour changes in the skin at the fracture site
6. • Bleeding (internal or external)

To help the broken bone heal, the bone ends are brought into normal position—called reduction. Closed reduction involves moving the bone back into place without opening the skin. Open reduction involves surgery to expose the bone and bring it back into alignment. Nails, rods, pins, screws, plates, or wires are sometimes used to keep the bone in place. After reduction, the site of the fracture is immobilized; that is, movement of the bone ends is prevented by using a cast or traction (see the Focus on Children: Fractures box). The location and seriousness of the fracture will determine the type of immobilization used.

#### Casting and Cast Care

Casts are applied to immobilize a fracture for an extended length of time in order for it to heal in its correct position. Casts that stay in place for the duration of the healing process are made of plaster, fibreglass, or plastic. Some casts, though, consist of removable plastic material. Before applying a plaster or fibreglass cast, the injured part is covered with a stockinette, which protects the skin, and then a layer of padding. Plaster casting material, which comes in rolls, is moistened and wrapped around the padding. Fibreglass casts dry quickly, usually in 20 to 30 minutes. A plaster cast dries in 24 to 48 hours. It is odourless, white, and shiny when dry. When wet, it is grey and cool and has a musty smell. Any client complaints about increasing pain or numbness and tingling in the casted limb should be reported immediately to the supervisor.

Proper care of a cast is important. As a support worker, you may assist with care of the cast. All casts will generate heat when they are drying, so it is important not to cover the cast during this period so as to keep it cool and allow it to dry. The client’s care plan may tell you to elevate the client’s casted limb. Elevating the limb reduces swelling and pain in the injured area. Removable plastic casts can be inflatable to compress the injury or fracture. The benefits of this type of cast are that it can be removed for physiotherapy or hygiene and it can reduce ankle stiffness and muscle atrophy as well as decrease healing time ([BOX 33.4](sec-s0185.xhtml#b0025)).

#### Traction

Traction is rarely used anymore to treat fractures and bone dislocation. However, two main types of traction are still in use: skin traction and skeletal traction.

1. •
   Skin traction
   [19](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur349) is a temporary measure used before a surgical repair to help prevent displacement between fractured bone pieces. Weights are suspended at the end of the bed and are counterbalanced by the client’s own body weight. This traction is held in place either with wide strips of medical adhesive tapes attached to the skin of the limb segment below the fracture or with a foam boot that is securely fitted to the client’s foot. Positioning in a slight Trendelenburg position (see [Chapter 32](../B9780323709392000328/B9780323709392000328.xhtml)) will keep the client from sliding to the foot of the bed. This type of traction can cause damage to the client’s skin, so usually no more than 10% of the client’s body weight (to a maximum of 5 kg) is recommended for the traction weights. If heavier weights are used, the superficial skin layers of the leg can be irritated or even pulled away from underlying layers, and the risk for friction injury in the client’s coccyx region increases (see [Chapter 22](../B9780323709392000225/B9780323709392000225.xhtml)).
2. •
   Skeletal traction
   [19](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur349) is most commonly used for femur fractures. A surgical pin with weights attached to it is placed through a bone farthest from the fracture. An apparatus to facilitate traction is then applied to the client ([FIGURE 33.7](sec-s0185.xhtml#f0040)).

#### Hip Fractures

Fractured hips are common in older people, especially in older women. Two signs of a fractured hip are shortening of the leg and outward rotation of the leg. Fractured hips in older people are a serious concern because they heal more slowly. The person is also put at risk for life-threatening postoperative complications such as pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and thrombi in the leg veins, as well as for pressure ulcers, constipation, and confusion.

The fractured hip is fixed in position with a pin, nail, plate, screw, or artificial hip joint. The client with a hip fracture needs preoperative and postoperative care (see [Chapter 46](../B9780323709392000468/B9780323709392000468.xhtml)), usually including rehabilitation after surgery. If home care is not possible, the client will need subacute or long-term care. Unless complications develop, most clients return home after successful rehabilitation (see the Focus on Home Care: Clients Recovering From Hip Fractures box). [BOX 33.5](sec-s0185.xhtml#b0030) describes the postoperative support care these clients require.

### Osteomyelitis

Osteomyelitis is inflammation or infection of the bone marrow, often caused by Staphylococcus bacteria. It can be a complication of a bone fracture or of orthopedic surgery and often occurs in the femur and tibia. If not recognized early, it can be very difficult to cure and can lead to persistent pain, loss of function, constant drainage, and even death. Osteomyelitis
occurs more frequently in males, possibly because they suffer a higher number of trauma injuries.

### Amputation of a Limb

An amputation is the removal of all or part of an extremity. A traumatic amputation occurs by accident, whereas surgical amputation is performed when an extremity has been so severely damaged in an accident or through disease that it has to be removed, when amputation is necessary to treat or prevent disease (such as cancer), or if gangrene has occurred. Gangrene—a condition in which tissue death occurs—is caused by infection, frostbite, burns, injuries, and circulatory disorders, all of which interfere with blood flow, thus depriving tissue of oxygen and nutrients. Poisonous substances and waste products build up in tissue and, as a result, the affected tissue dies and becomes black, cold, and shrivelled ([FIGURE 33.9](sec-s0185.xhtml#f0050)). If untreated, gangrene can spread through the body and cause death. The most common cause of amputation is complications from diabetes.

Amputation of all or part of an upper extremity—including fingers, hand, forearm, or entire arm—may
be necessary. Similarly, all or part of the lower extremity—including toes, foot, lower leg below or above the knee, or entire leg—may be amputated. The client may experience phantom limb pain, which is the perception that the limb is still part of the body and that it is in pain. The client may also feel burning or tingling that seems to stem from the amputated limb. The dominant theory for the cause of this pain is the irritation of the nerve endings in the stump (the remaining part of the limb), which is a normal reaction that may last only for a short time after surgery or for many years.

#### Prostheses

Most clients with amputations are fitted with prostheses (prosthetic devices; singular, prosthesis)—artificial replacements for missing body parts ([FIGURE 33.10](sec-s0185.xhtml#f0055)). A prosthesis can be made for any body part—for example, the hand, arm, leg, eye, or breast.

To fit an arm or leg prosthesis, the stump is first conditioned, which involves shrinking and shaping the stump into a cone shape using an elastic stocking or bandage ([FIGURE 33.11](sec-s0185.xhtml#f0060)). It is important to wash the elastic stocking, as normal moisture could accumulate and cause the stocking to break down, which in turn would increase the possibility of breakdown of the skin or healed incision. The client needs to exercise to strengthen the other limbs, and physiotherapists and occupational therapists will help the client learn how to use the prosthesis. Research is being done on prosthetic devices that will enable more mobility for clients. Currently available prostheses allow for limited movement and can be cumbersome.

#### Prostheses and Skin Care

Proper skin care is important for clients with prostheses. For those with leg or arm prostheses, the stump is confined in the prosthesis. Since the skin in this area is not exposed to air, it may become hot and moist, which can cause irritation, blisters, and skin infections.

It is important to follow the client’s care plan. The skin at the prosthesis site requires daily care. Damp skin inside the prosthesis can become irritated, so the skin usually needs to be cleaned at the end of the day to
keep it thoroughly dry. Wash the skin with warm water and soap—sometimes with medicated soap, depending on the client’s needs. Rinse the skin thoroughly with warm water to remove any soap residue, and towel-dry gently. The client’s care plan may call for skin lotion or cream to be applied to the entire stump area in order to keep the skin soft and prevent skin damage.

Notify your supervisor at once if the client complains of pain at the prosthesis site. Also, report any signs of redness, swelling, or drainage at the site.

Most prostheses are washed every day with warm, soapy water, rinsed well, and towel-dried. Prostheses are very valuable, so do not handle them unless instructed to in the care plan. Keep the prosthesis in its box or container when not in use.

### Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a bone (osteo) disorder in which the bone becomes porous and brittle (porosis), meaning it can break easily. Bones of the spine, hips, and wrists are affected most often. Osteoporosis is common in older people and in postmenopausal women. After menopause, ovaries stop producing the hormone estrogen, resulting in bone changes. Lack of dietary calcium is also a major cause of osteoporosis.

Smoking, high alcohol intake, lack of exercise, prolonged bed rest, and immobility are risk factors for osteoporosis. For bone to form properly, it must continually bear weight. If not, calcium is not absorbed, and the bone becomes porous and brittle. Back pain, gradual loss of height, and a stooped posture occur. Fractures are a great risk if the client falls or has an accident. Sometimes, bones are so brittle that the slightest activity—for example, turning in bed or getting up from a chair—can cause a fracture. Always keep this risk in mind when moving your clients.

Because there is no cure for osteoporosis, prevention is important. The diet must contain enough calcium and vitamins. Estrogen is prescribed for some women after menopause but is not used as much as in the past. Other medications prescribed are bisphosphonates, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), calcium, and vitamin D, and more medications to prevent osteoporosis or decrease its progression are being developed. Weight-bearing exercises—for example, strength training (lifting weights), walking, jogging, dancing, and stair climbing—may also prevent osteoporosis.

Some clients with osteoporosis wear a back brace or corset or use walking aids. While providing support care to a client, protect the client from falls and accidents. Transfer, turn, and reposition the client gently.

### Arthritis

Arthritis—which means joint (arthr) inflammation (itis)—is the most common joint disease. Inflammation means swelling, redness, heat, and pain. Pain and decreased mobility occur in the affected joints. Arthritis occurs in persons of all ages. Clients with severe arthritis may require arthroplasty—surgical replacement (plasty) of a joint (arthro) to relieve pain and restore joint motion. Ankle, knee, hip, shoulder, wrist, finger, and toe joints can be removed and replaced with an artificial joint.

There are different types of arthritis. The two that are most common are osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.

#### Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common form of arthritis. It causes the cartilage that occupies the joints of the body to wear away, causing pain in and disfigurement of the joints. Obesity and a sedentary lifestyle are the main contributors to OA. It affects 13.6% of adults in Canada.[20](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur350) It tends to occur in people over 40 and becomes more common with increasing age. Approximately 80% of Canadians are affected by osteoarthritis by age 75.[21](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur351)

OA usually affects the weight-bearing joints—the hips, knees, ankles, and spine. However, joints in the fingers and thumbs can also be affected. In OA, cartilage (the material that cushions the ends of the bones) gradually breaks down, and eventually the involved bones rub together, causing pain, especially when the joint is moved. In OA, joints become swollen, stiff, and painful.

Pain is often less severe in the morning and worsens during the day. Pain occurs with weight-bearing and joint motion, and severe pain can interfere with rest and sleep. The joints become stiff after the client has not moved for a period, and cold weather and dampness can increase the symptoms.

Bones can also thicken and form growths called spurs, which change the shape of the bone and joint.
Spurs called Heberden’s nodes are common in the fingers ([FIGURE 33.12](sec-s0185.xhtml#f0065)).

OA has no cure. Treatment involves relieving pain and stiffness, and physicians often prescribe Tylenol or special arthritis medications as well as heat or cold applications. Treatment for obese clients often includes weight loss through a low-fat, low-calorie diet. During the advanced stage of OA, the client may need a cane or walker. Measures to prevent falls are important, and assistance with activities of daily living (ADLs) is given as needed. Elevated toilet seats are helpful for clients with limited range of motion (ROM) in the hips and knees.

#### Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a persistent and progressive disease that is predicted to affect one in six Canadians aged 16 or older.[22](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur352) It usually occurs in people between the ages of 25 and 50. However, RA can affect people of all ages, from toddlers to older persons, and is found to be more common in women than in men (see the Focus on Children: Arthritis box).

RA affects connective tissue throughout the body. The immune system does not recognize the connective tissue as “normal” and attacks and destroys it. The disease can destroy connective tissues of the heart, lungs, eyes, kidneys, and skin, but it affects mainly the joints, which become painful, swollen, and stiff. RA usually occurs on both sides of the body, but not always. Pain and stiffness are usually worst when the client wakes in the morning but gradually decrease during the day. RA usually starts with the smaller joints in the fingers, hands, and feet and moves on to the larger joints (wrists, elbows, and shoulders; ankles, knees, and hips). Eventually, the normal tissue is replaced by scar tissue, and deformities in the joints can develop ([FIGURE 33.13](sec-s0185.xhtml#f0070)). In time, the organs in the body also become affected by RA. Fatigue and fever are common.

![Close-up of hands with all fingers turned outward.](../../IMAGES/B978032370939200033X/main.assets/f33-13-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 33.13 Deformities caused by rheumatoid arthritis.
From Swartz, M. H. [2009]. Textbook of physical diagnosis [6th ed., p. 625]. Saunders.

Treatment goals are to maintain joint motion, control pain, and prevent deformities. Rest is balanced with exercise. Adequate sleep—8 to 10 hours—is necessary each night, along with morning and afternoon rest periods. Bed rest is needed if several joints are involved and if fever is present.

Clients with RA benefit greatly from ROM exercises, and some may need walking aids or splints. Taking safety measures to prevent falls is important. Physicians or nurse practitioners may prescribe medications as well as heat or cold applications for relief of pain and inflammation. Some clients may need joint replacement surgery.

### Gout

Gout, also called metabolic arthritis, is a disease caused by the accumulation of uric acid in the cartilage of a joint, especially in the tendons. It usually
affects the big toe first but can affect other joints as well. The signs and symptoms of gout include severe and sudden pain, swelling, redness, warmth, and stiffness of the joint and possibly a low-grade fever. Even simply placing a blanket over the affected area may cause severe pain to the client with gout. Treatment involves pain medication and anti-inflammatory drugs. Applying ice packs may help relieve pain. Follow the client’s care plan.

### Fibromyalgia

Fibromyalgia is a condition associated with aching, stiffness, and fatigue in muscles, ligaments, and tendons and often affects the neck, shoulders, upper back, lower back, hips, legs, knees, and feet. Because of the pain and stiffness in the affected parts, the client commonly experiences fatigue and sleep disturbances. Persistent fatigue makes even simple tasks difficult. Clients diagnosed with fibromyalgia have usually gone through many tests to rule out other disorders. The process can be very frustrating for them because there are, as yet, no specific tests to identify this condition. As a support worker, you need to empathize with affected clients and encourage them to accept the treatments indicated on their care plan.

There is no cure for fibromyalgia. Heat and cold applications, massage, and regular stretching and ROM exercises are helpful. Occupational therapy may be required, along with prescribed medications to relieve pain, relax muscles, and promote sleep.

Clients who are living with a musculoskeletal disorder may feel angry and frustrated if they are less active or have decreased mobility. Besides the suggestions listed within this section, here are some other ways you can best support them:

1. •
   Listen and use empathy when speaking to the client.
    This conveys respect for the client.
2. •
   Ensure the client is clean and dry at all times to prevent skin irritation. Skin that is soiled or moist can trap odour and irritation causing bacteria.
3. •

Ensure that they are sitting or lying in a clean, dry, wrinkle-free base at all times (see [Chapters 21](../B9780323709392000213/B9780323709392000213.xhtml) and [22](../B9780323709392000225/B9780323709392000225.xhtml)). 5. •

## Nervous System Disorders

The nervous system communicates signals between the brain and the body, so nervous system disorders affect physical and cognitive functions. Physical functions include tasks such as moving, touching, seeing, hearing, and controlling the bowel and bladder. Cognitive functions include tasks controlled by the mind (the word cognitive is related to knowledge), such as thinking, reasoning, understanding, remembering, learning, reading, and problem solving.

### Epilepsy

Epilepsy is a group of neurological disorders characterized by recurrent episodes of convulsive seizures, sensory disturbances, abnormal behaviour, loss of consciousness, or some or all of these features. Temporary changes occur in the brain because of a burst of electrical energy, which results in a seizure, affecting awareness, movement, or sensations. A seizure (or convulsion), in some people, can be a violent and sudden contraction or tremor of muscle groups, uncontrolled movements, and loss of consciousness; in others, the seizure may be so subtle that it can go unnoticed. Seizures can involve the entire brain or just one part of the brain. Some individuals have a warning of a seizure, or an aura, before it starts.

Some infants can have a single seizure caused by a very high fever. A single seizure does not mean epilepsy, however; in epilepsy, seizures recur. Known causes of epilepsy include the following:

1. • Brain injury before, during, or after birth
2. • Lack of oxygen before, during, or after birth
4. • Problems with brain development before birth
5. • An injury or infection during pregnancy
6. • Head trauma (accidents, gunshot wounds, sports injuries, falls, blows to the head)
7. • Chemical imbalance
8. • Poor nutrition
9. • Brain tumour
10. • Childhood fevers
11. • Poisons such as lead and alcohol
12. • Infections, such as meningitis and encephalitis
13. • Stroke

While children and young adults are commonly affected, epilepsy can develop at any time during a person’s life. It can occur because of any condition affecting the brain—for example, cerebral palsy, developmental disability, autism, and Alzheimer’s disease.

There is no cure for epilepsy at this time. It is normally controlled by medication. Surgery has been used for people suffering from severe, uncontrolled seizures. When controlled, epilepsy usually does not affect learning and ability to perform ADLs. Activity limitations occur only in severe cases. For example, if a client is at risk of having a seizure at any time, their job choices and activities such as driving may be limited. Each province and territory has regulations regarding driver’s licences for people with epilepsy.

If a client experiences an epileptic seizure, your responsibility is to prevent self-injury. Stay with the client and call for assistance. Lower the client to the floor, if possible, and move objects away from them. You should move the client away from sharp edges, place something soft under their head, and, if possible, move the client onto their side to avoid asphyxiation (choking on vomit). If it is not possible to move the client onto their side during the seizure, gently try to move their head to the side, but do not force any movement of body parts. After the seizure is over, put the client in the recovery (three-quarter prone) position, and check the client for injury. If the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes or if more seizures follow one after another, you should call emergency services immediately.

Never place anything in the client’s mouth during a seizure. Doing so can cause injury to you or your client. Clients will not swallow their tongues but may bite them during the seizure.

After a seizure, the client may be exhausted and confused and may not be aware that a seizure has occurred. Stay with the client until they return to their normal condition. You may need to help the client wash and change clothes, as some clients may vomit, void, or soil themselves during or after a seizure. Some clients may fall into a deep sleep, and some may experience a headache after a seizure.

You need to record your observations—duration of the seizure, parts of the body involved, any mannerisms noted, and your client’s behaviour after the seizure has stopped.

### Stroke

Stroke, or cerebral vascular accident (CVA), is the fourth leading cause of death in Canada and also the leading cause of nervous system disabilities in adults.

The Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada defines stroke as a sudden loss of brain function. Stroke is caused by one of the following:

1. • An interruption of blood flow to the brain, often caused by a blood clot
2. • The rupture of a blood vessel in the brain

When a vessel to the brain is blocked or bursts, blood supply to a part of the brain is suddenly obstructed, and the brain cells in the affected area do not get oxygen and nutrients. A brain injury results, and the functions controlled by that part of the brain are lost or impaired.

Temporary interruption of blood flow to the brain is called a transient ischemic attack (TIA). Even though a TIA results in no permanent brain injury, it is an important warning sign, as sometimes a TIA occurs before a stroke, so a client having a TIA needs immediate medical attention.

The risk for stroke increases with age—most strokes affect people aged 65 years or older. Men are at a slightly higher risk than women, but more women than men die from stroke. Risk factors other than gender include high blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, heart disease, high blood cholesterol, lack of exercise, and high alcohol intake.

Common warning signs of a stroke are listed in [BOX 33.6](sec-s0265.xhtml#b0035). A stroke is a medical emergency, so the
client needs immediate medical attention. Call emergency services.

2. • Trouble speaking—sudden difficulty speaking or understanding or sudden confusion, even if temporary

4. • Headache—sudden severe and unusual headache

If the client survives the stroke, some brain injury is likely. The injured area in the brain cannot send messages to control certain parts of the body, so normal function is affected, depending on the area of the brain injury ([FIGURE 33.14](sec-s0265.xhtml#f0075)). Early detection and treatment is of vital importance to reduce the risk of lasting effects. The effects of stroke include the following:

1. • Hemiplegia—paralysis (plegia) of one side (hemi) of the body; the right arm and leg or the left arm and leg could be affected
2. • Weakness on one side of the body
3. • Loss of facial muscle control
4. • Changing emotions (the person may cry easily, sometimes for no apparent reason)
5. • Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia)

7. • Loss of ability to speak or understand others; aphasia (see [Chapter 35](../B9780323709392000353/B9780323709392000353.xhtml))

9. • Impaired memory
10. • Urinary frequency, urgency, or incontinence
11. • Aphasia—loss of speech

Following a stroke, the client’s behaviour is usually affected. The client may forget about or ignore the affected side because of loss of movement and feeling on that side. If vision is affected, the client may not see one side of the visual field. This impairment can cause the client to eat food from only one side of the plate or to read only one side of a page.

After a stroke, clients may no longer recognize familiar objects or know how to use them. Telling time may be hard for these clients, and they may not recognize people. Memory impairment and difficulty learning and remembering new information are common. Clients may forget what to do or how to do it, or if they do know, their bodies may not be able to act, making it difficult for them to carry out ADLs (see [Chapter 38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml)).

Depression is a common after-effect of illness or disability. It is particularly common after a stroke, as a result of the brain injury. As a support worker, it is important to encourage your clients to share their feelings during this difficult period. Sometimes, their emotional responses may seem exaggerated or inappropriate. Outbursts of anger, moaning, laughing, or crying for little or no reason can often occur. Remember, these reactions are beyond clients’ control, so be patient and kind with them. Report any changes in the client’s behaviour or mood to your supervisor.

Rehabilitation usually starts immediately after a stroke, and many clients are able to improve or regain lost functions. Speech therapy, physiotherapy, and occupational therapy are usually ordered by the physician. In addition, a swallowing assessment may be done to provide guidelines within the care plan to reduce the risk for choking and aspiration. Self-help
devices may be necessary (see [Chapter 16](../B978032370939200016X/B978032370939200016X.xhtml)), and the client may depend partially or totally on others for care. Most stroke survivors return home and require home care, but some may need subacute or long-term care, depending on their needs and condition. Some clients require assistance with ADLs, but they are encouraged to do as much as possible for themselves. Clients who are immobile need skin care to prevent pressure ulcers from forming, and they also may require a bladder or bowel control training program and ROM exercises. Communication methods are established for clients with language impairments (see [Chapter 35](../B9780323709392000353/B9780323709392000353.xhtml)).

### Aneurysm

An aneurysm is a localized dilation (expansion, and thus weakness) of the wall of an artery or heart chamber. Aneurysms can form anywhere in the body. Aneurysms are common in the aorta but can also occur in peripheral vessels. In older people, the popliteal artery is a common site for an aneurysm. The effect of an aneurysm will depend on the area damaged.

Aneurysms throughout the body can be caused by atherosclerosis (see page 793) and hypertension (see page 792) or, less frequently, by trauma, infection, or a congenital weakness in the vessel wall. A person may inherit the tendency to form aneurysms, or aneurysms may develop because of hardening of the arteries (atherosclerosis) and aging.

Clients with aneurysms are usually asymptomatic (without symptoms), which explains the occurrence of sudden death when an aneurysm ruptures. Most aneurysms are discovered on routine physical examination or with X-rays. Once diagnosed, the primary goal is to prevent a rupture. If an aneurysm ruptures, surgical repair is needed to stop the bleeding, but this surgery can be very complicated, especially if the aneurysm is in an abdominal, thoracic, or brain artery.

#### Brain Aneurysm[23](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur353)

A brain (cerebral) aneurysm is a bulging, weak area in the wall of an artery that supplies blood to the brain. In most cases, a brain aneurysm causes no symptoms and goes unnoticed. In rare cases, however, the brain aneurysm can rupture, causing blood to escape into the brain and causing a stroke. This escaping blood is called a hemorrhage. Depending on the severity of the hemorrhage, brain damage or death may result. The most common location for brain aneurysms is in the network of blood vessels at the base of the brain.

Most brain aneurysms cause no symptoms and may only be discovered during tests for another, usually unrelated, condition. Some risk factors that can lead to brain aneurysms can be controlled, and others cannot. The risk factors of brain aneurysms include the following:

1. •
   Family history. People who have a family history of brain aneurysms are more likely to have an aneurysm than those who do not.
2. •
   Previous aneurysm. People who have had a brain aneurysm are more likely to have another.
3. •
   Gender. Women are more likely to develop a brain aneurysm or to suffer a subarachnoid hemorrhage.
4. •
   Ethnic background. People of African descent are more likely than Whites to have a subarachnoid (brain) hemorrhage.
5. •
   High blood pressure. The risk of subarachnoid hemorrhage is greater in people who have a history of high blood pressure.
6. •
   Smoking. In addition to being a cause of high blood pressure, the use of cigarettes may also greatly increase the chances of a brain aneurysm rupturing.

Symptoms of a ruptured brain aneurysm often come on suddenly. If you have any of the following symptoms or notice them in someone you know, call emergency services immediately:

1. • A sudden, severe headache that is different from past headaches
2. • Neck pain
3. • Nausea and vomiting
4. • Sensitivity to light
5. • Fainting or loss of consciousness
6. • Seizures

### Acquired Brain Injuries

An acquired brain injury (ABI) is any type of sudden injury or illness that causes temporary or permanent damage to the brain. It is also called traumatic brain injury. An ABI can be the result of a disease, a medical condition, trauma to the head caused by
a fall, collision, assault, concussion, stroke, tumour, or lack of oxygen, or of an illness such as meningitis. When the head is subjected to violent forces, the brain is bashed against the skull, resulting in bleeding, swelling, and bruising of brain tissue. Brain injury can be permanent. Most ABIs are caused by motor vehicle accidents (MVAs). Other common causes are falls, sports and recreational injuries, acts of violence, work-related accidents, and other nervous system injuries—for example, spinal cord injuries. As well, conditions during birth, near drowning, choking, suffocation, or stroke can cause ABIs. Brain injury can also occur without any outward signs of physical damage—for example, when babies are shaken violently (known as shaken baby syndrome). Some ABIs are caused by lack of oxygen to the brain due to accidents, diseases, and certain conditions. Lack of oxygen destroys brain cells and can cause permanent brain injury. ABI is the fourth leading cause of disability in Canada, and more than 160 000 Canadians sustain a brain injury each year.[24](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur354) Brain injury is the leading cause of death and disability among Canadians under the age of 40.[25](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur355)

An ABI affects both the client and the family. Family members of clients with ABI may be required to provide long-term care and may even need to alter their own retirement plans. Signs and symptoms depend on the severity and location of the injury and may include moodiness, irritability, or an inability to sit still. Clients with ABI may require ongoing physiotherapy, speech therapy, occupational therapy, or even psychotherapy, depending on the cause and extent of their injury.[26](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur356)

#### Symptoms of Acquired Brain Injury

Many people with ABI have difficulties associated with the following:[24](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur354)

1. • Attention and concentration
2. • Memory
3. • Orientation to person, place, or time
4. • Decision making
5. • Problem solving
6. • Learning new ideas

Physical symptoms of ABI may include the following:

1. • Headache
2. • Fatigue
3. • Dizziness
4. • Uneven gait
5. • Nausea
6. • Visual disturbances (such as blurred vision)
7. • Seizures
8. • Changes in sleep patterns
9. • Changes in eating patterns
10. • Impairment of one or more senses, such as sight or hearing

Clients with ABI may exhibit the following behavioural changes:

1. • Depression
2. • Anxiety
3. • Irritability
4. • Emotional or impulse-control difficulties
5. • Decreased initiative or motivation

See [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml) for ways that ABIs can affect a client’s mental health. Cognitive issues include difficulty making decisions, difficulties with attention or concentration, and difficulty recalling simple words. Severe brain injury can result in intellectual disability, speech challenges, breathing difficulties, and loss of bowel and bladder control. Rehabilitation (see [Chapter 16](../B978032370939200016X/B978032370939200016X.xhtml)) is required for these clients. Care depends on their needs and abilities.

### Parkinson’s Disease

Parkinson’s disease is a neurological disorder in which cells in certain parts of the brain are gradually destroyed. It is progressive and, as yet, there is no cure. The disease is usually seen in people over 50 years of age. Signs and symptoms become worse over time. They include the following:

1. •
   Tremors
   —often start in one finger and spread to the whole arm; pill-rolling movements (rubbing of the thumb and index finger) may occur and progress to tremors in legs, jaws, and face
2. •
   Stiff muscles
   —stiffness in arms, legs, neck, and trunk
3. •
   Masklike expression
   —inability to blink or smile; a fixed stare is common
4. •
   Slow movement
   —a slow, shuffling walk and inability to lift the feet
6. •
   Stooped posture and impaired balance
   —inability to walk, with increased risk for falls

Other signs and symptoms that can develop over time include difficulties swallowing and chewing, constipation, and bowel and bladder issues. Sleep disturbances and depression as well as dementia—memory loss, slow thinking, and emotional changes (see [Chapter 38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml))—can also occur. Speech changes include slurred speech, monotone, and soft speech. Some clients may talk too fast or repeat what they say. When caring for a client with Parkinson’s, allow them the time required to communicate.

Medications specific for Parkinson’s disease are prescribed, along with exercise and physiotherapy to help improve or maintain strength, posture, balance, and mobility. The client needs to have regular rest periods and avoid stress, as tiredness and stress can make the symptoms worse. The client may need help with ADLs. Measures to promote normal elimination are practised, and safety practices to prevent falls and choking are followed. As a support worker, it is important to treat the client with dignity and respect. Remember, the client’s masklike facial expression does not show the person’s true feelings.

### Huntington’s Disease

Huntington’s disease is an inherited neurological disorder. It destroys brain cells and causes uncontrolled movements, emotional disturbances, and cognitive losses. Signs and symptoms may appear any time between the ages of 20 and 60 years. They begin with twitching, fidgeting, and clumsiness, and over time, the client’s arms and legs constantly move, making walking impossible. The client may have difficulty eating and swallowing and be unable to perform many ADLs.

Clients with Huntington’s disease also may have slurred speech, depression, irritability, and apathy. Cognitive losses include loss of intellectual speed, attention, and short-term memory. Concentration on intellectual tasks becomes increasingly difficult as the disease progresses. Currently, there is no cure, and there are no treatments to prevent or control the disease.

Support care depends on the client’s needs. Safety practices should be followed to prevent falls, choking, and other accidents. Emotional support is necessary for all clients, but it is particularly important for these clients, as they are often relatively young and in long-term care facilities. Also, often, more than one member of the family has the disease.

### Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a persistent, slowly progressive disease in which the body’s immune system attacks the protective myelin sheaths that surround the nerve cells of the brain and spinal cord (a process called demyelination), resulting in damaged areas that are unable to transmit nerve impulses.

Canada has one of the highest rates of MS in the world. Symptoms usually start between the ages of 15 and 40 years, with women being affected more often than men.[27](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur357) The onset is gradual, and symptoms vary greatly from client to client. A client can also have different symptoms at different times, with conditions improving during periods of remission.

The common symptoms of MS are as follows:

1. • Blurred vision, double vision, or blindness
2. • Extreme fatigue
3. • Loss of balance, dizziness, difficulty walking, and clumsiness
4. • Muscle weakness and stiffness
5. • Tingling, numbness, or a burning feeling in one area of the body
6. • Sensitivity to heat
7. • Difficulty speaking
8. • Difficulty swallowing
9. • Bladder and bowel issues
10. • Impotence or diminished sexual arousal
11. • Short-term memory loss
12. • Difficulties concentrating
13. • Impaired judgement or reasoning

There is no cure as yet for MS, but research is ongoing and more progress is being made. A number of drugs used to treat MS are keeping clients in remission for longer periods. Some clients are seeking alternative methods of treatment, some of which involve travel to another country for procedures not yet available in Canada. Support care depends on the client’s needs and condition. At first, the client may need home care, especially help with housekeeping, to prevent fatigue. As mobility decreases, the client
will need to depend more on others, and measures have to be taken to prevent injury and to promote bowel and bladder elimination. Eventually, the client may require long-term care.

### Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as Lou Gehrig’s disease, is a degenerative disease characterized by loss of the motor neurons, with progressive weakness and atrophy of the muscles of the hands, forearms, and legs, eventually spreading to involve most of the body and face. In Canada, about 3 000 people are currently living with this disease, which occurs most commonly between the ages of 40 and 70 years.[28](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur358)

ALS is a progressive disease in which nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord are gradually destroyed. The rate of progress of the disease differs with each client. Usually, the first sign of the disease is difficulty using fingers and hands. The client may not be able to pick up a cup, lace shoes, pull up zippers, or do buttons. The client then has difficulty walking, so stumbling and falling are common. Eventually, all muscle control is lost, and the client is unable to speak, swallow, move, or breathe independently. Eventually death occurs.

Clients with ALS are alert and can think clearly through the course of the disease. Even if these clients cannot speak, remember that they can still understand what you and others say. The speech pathologist may recommend new communication methods. Follow the client’s care plan. Emotional support is very important, as these clients are aware of what is happening to their bodies, and the progression of the disease is quite distressing. They eventually become confined to bed and are usually placed in a side-lying position.

### Spinal Cord Injuries

The spinal cord is a pathway that allows communication between the brain and the rest of the body, so when it is damaged, the nerves cannot send messages between the brain and the different parts of the body, and partial or total paralysis may occur. Paralysis is the complete or partial loss of ability to move a body part or muscle group. Spinal cord injuries, most of which are permanent, are often caused by MVAs.

![(A) shows diagram of a human model with area below the waist shaded. (B) shows diagram of a human model with area below the neck shaded.](../../IMAGES/B978032370939200033X/main.assets/f33-15-9780323711654.jpg)

Which parts of the body are paralyzed depends on where the spinal cord was injured. The higher up the spine the injury occurs, the greater the loss of function is ([FIGURE 33.15](sec-s0265.xhtml#f0080)). Spinal cord injuries at the thoracic level (chest area) or lower may cause paraplegia—paralysis (plegia) from the waist down. Injuries in the cervical region (neck) may cause quadriplegia—paralysis (plegia) of all four (quad) limbs and the trunk; depending on the location and severity of the spinal cord injury, the client can be paralyzed from the neck down. These clients often need cervical traction and a special bed that keeps the spine straight at all times.

Clients who have had recent spinal cord injuries require rehabilitation (see [Chapter 16](../B978032370939200016X/B978032370939200016X.xhtml)), and the rehabilitation program depends on the client’s needs and remaining abilities. ## Endocrine System Disorders

The endocrine system is made up of glands that secrete chemical messengers called hormones. Hormones affect other organs and glands and are essential for body function. Endocrine disorders result in hormone levels that are either too high or too low. The most common endocrine disorder is diabetes.

### Diabetes

Diabetes is a disorder in which the body cannot produce or use insulin properly. Insulin is a hormone secreted by the pancreas and needed for the proper use of sugar (glucose). It helps the sugar obtained from foods to get into cells. Without enough insulin in the body, sugar builds up in blood—referred to as hyperglycemia, which means high (hyper) sugar (glyc) in blood (emia). When cells do not have sugar for energy, they cannot perform their functions. Left untreated, mild hyperglycemia can lead to long-term complications. Severe hyperglycemia can be life-threatening.

Today, more than nine million Canadians live with diabetes or pre-diabetes. Approximately 10% of people with diabetes have type 1 diabetes, and the number of people with type 2 diabetes is increasing dramatically. There are three types of diabetes:

1. •
   Type 1 diabetes
   —occurs most often in children and young adults but can occur at any age. The pancreas does not produce insulin, leading to severe hyperglycemia. These clients develop symptoms early in the disease and need treatment of daily insulin injections. It is sometimes called insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM).
2. •
   Type 2 diabetes
   —usually develops in adulthood but can occur in children as well. Type 2 is the most common type of diabetes. Either the pancreas does not produce enough insulin, or the body does not effectively use the insulin that is produced. Obesity and family history are risk factors. Risk also increases after age 40 years. Almost 80% of Canadians come from populations that are at higher risk for type 2 diabetes (e.g., Indigenous, Latin American, Asian, South Asian, or African descent).[29](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur359)

The hyperglycemia is often mild enough that the client may not notice the symptoms. Treatment often consists of diet and exercise or oral medications. Insulin is sometimes required. Type 2 diabetes is sometimes referred to as non–insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM), although this name is misleading since some clients must take insulin.

1. •
   Gestational diabetes
   —develops during pregnancy and usually disappears after the baby is born. However, the woman who experiences gestational diabetes is at risk for developing type 2 diabetes later in life.

Common signs and symptoms of diabetes include the following:

1. • Increased thirst
2. • Frequent urination
3. • Constant hunger
4. • Unusual weight loss
5. • Extreme fatigue
6. • Dry, itchy skin
7. • Blurred eyesight

It is important that diabetes, whether it is type 1 or type 2, be controlled. If left untreated or poorly managed (as often happens with type 2 diabetes), the high levels of blood sugar slowly damage both small and large blood vessels in the body, potentially causing numerous complications including blindness, kidney disease, nerve damage, sexual dysfunction, and circulatory disorders. Circulatory disorders can lead to stroke, heart attack, and slow wound healing. Foot and leg wounds have very serious consequences for people with diabetes, as infection and gangrene can occur and sometimes lead to amputation.

While there is no cure for diabetes, the disease can be managed to reduce or prevent long-term complications. All clients with diabetes must follow a careful diet, and overweight clients need to lose weight. Exercise is included as part of the treatment plan, as regular exercise helps lower blood sugar levels, promotes weight loss, and reduces stress. Oral medications may also be necessary. Regular and proper foot and nail care provided by a professional is extremely important, as corns, blisters, and calluses on the feet can lead to infection and require amputation. The client’s feet should be inspected daily, and any cuts, lesions, or nonintact skin surface should be reported immediately to the supervisor.

When you are caring for a client with diabetes, your reports and observations are extremely important, as you could be the first one to observe changes in your client’s condition that require medical attention.

Diabetes requires blood sugar monitoring—glucometers are now available to allow clients to do finger-prick blood tests themselves to determine the levels of sugar in blood and the amount of insulin needed. Clients with type 1 diabetes need to test their blood sugar level an average of three to four times a day before each insulin dose, and those with type 2 diabetes usually need to monitor their levels once daily. Blood sugar monitoring is a delegated act that you may be trained to do as part of your role as a support worker.

Clients with type 1 diabetes require insulin injections usually one to four times daily; some clients with type 2 diabetes also need insulin. Most clients administer their insulin themselves. The exact type and dose of insulin must be taken as ordered by the physician so as to prevent complications from insulin therapy. If too much insulin is taken, hypoglycemia occurs. Hypoglycemia means low (hypo) sugar (glyc) in the blood (emia). If not corrected (by intake of sugar), hypoglycemia can lead to coma or death. Immediately report signs and symptoms of hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia in clients to your supervisor.

As the support worker, you may prepare meals for adults and children with diabetes. Diet is a very important part of managing diabetes. Follow the client’s diet plan carefully, and serve meals and snacks on time. You need to record the amount of food your client consumes at every meal and snack. Calories not consumed at a meal or snack need to be replaced in the course of that day, or your client may experience a reaction. Refer to the client’s care plan. Signs and symptoms of hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia are described in [TABLE 33.3](sec-s0330.xhtml#t0020). The care plan will tell you what to do if your client is having a reaction.

### Hyperthyroidism

Hyperthyroidism is caused by an overactive thyroid gland, which results in too much thyroid hormone being produced. All of the body’s processes are regulated by thyroid hormone, so all of the body’s functions speed up when there is excess thyroid hormone. Since all body functions are affected, there can be different signs and symptoms, including weight loss, increased hunger, hyperactivity, palpitations, nausea, vomiting, irritability, and depression. Treatment may involve surgical removal of some or all of the thyroid gland, administration of one dose of radioactive iodine to destroy some of the thyroid cells, or medications that inhibit the production of thyroid hormone.

### Hypothyroidism

Hypothyroidism is caused by an underactive thyroid gland. With decreased production of thyroid hormone, all of the body processes are slowed down. Signs and symptoms of hypothyroidism are the opposite of signs and symptoms of hyperthyroidism: weight gain, impaired memory, fatigue, sluggishness, irritability, constipation, and slower heart rate. Treatment consists of a thyroid supplement.

## Cardiovascular System Disorders

The cardiovascular system involves the heart and blood vessels. The heart pumps blood continuously through the vessels, and blood delivers oxygen, nutrients, and other substances to the body’s cells and removes waste products. Cardiovascular disorders involve problems in the heart or in the blood vessels and are the leading causes of death in Canada.[30](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur360) Hypotension is a condition in which the systolic blood
pressure is below 90 mm Hg and the diastolic pressure is below 60 mm Hg (see [Chapter 24](../B9780323709392000249/B9780323709392000249.xhtml)).

Dehydration will be discussed in this section. Although it can be caused by many things, it can lead to severe hypotension and, in extreme cases, cardiac complications and even death.

### Dehydration

Every day, we lose water through natural bodily functions such as sweating, breathing, urination (see [Chapter 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml)), bowel movements and through tears and saliva. To maintain fluid balance in our bodies, our brain sends out a signal to tell us we are thirsty. By drinking fluids and eating foods that contain water, fluids are then replaced in our bodies. Dehydration is a decrease in the amount of water in body tissues, which occurs when fluid output exceeds fluid intake. Common causes are low fluid intake, vomiting, diarrhea, bleeding, excess sweating, increased urine production, excessive urination caused by health conditions such as diabetes, and taking the incorrect dose of diuretics (see [Chapter 45](../B9780323709392000456/B9780323709392000456.xhtml)).

Signs of mild or moderate dehydration include the following:

1. • Thirst
2. • Dry or sticky mouth
3. • Decreased urination
4. • Dark yellow urine
5. • Dry, cool skin
6. • Headache
7. • Muscle cramps

Signs of severe dehydration include the following:

1. • Lack of urine production (anuria) or having concentrated urine that is dark in colour
2. • Very dry skin
3. • Feeling dizzy
4. • Rapid heartbeat
5. • Rapid breathing
6. • Sunken eyes
7. • Sleepiness, lack of energy, confusion, or irritability
8. • Fainting

Older people, infants, and young children are particularly susceptible to dehydration (see the Focus on Children: Dehydration and the Focus on Older Persons: Dehydration boxes). Severe dehydration is a medical emergency and needs to be treated immediately.

In some emergency circumstances, certain people are at risk for developing not only severe hypotension from dehydration but also potentially lethal heart conditions due to loss of an essential electrolyte, potassium (K+). Potassium exits the body in vomitus and diarrhea feces. Potassium is one of the primary electrolytes that is concentrated within the cells of the body, and its function includes maintaining the electrical activity of the cells in the body, including muscles in the heart. Cells within the heart are particularly affected when potassium levels fall.

People who may be at risk of developing potentially lethal low potassium blood levels may include clients with severe cases of influenza who have experienced frequent diarrhea and vomiting for more than several days, are unable to tolerate drinking fluids, and are dehydrated. Other people at risk are those who have eaten tainted water or food, as well as individuals who live with an eating disorder such as bulimia nervosa and frequently purge or overuse laxatives (see [Chapter 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml)). In any situation in which the client has experienced a significant drop in blood potassium levels, the client requires hospital treatment that includes infusion of an intravenous (IV) solution containing potassium replacement (see [Chapter 28](../B9780323709392000286/B9780323709392000286.xhtml)).
