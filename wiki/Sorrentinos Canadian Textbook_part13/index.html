    Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a trauma and stressor-related disorder that can occur in people of all ages, ethnicities, cultures, and genders. Signs and symptoms include the following:

    1. • Vivid nightmares of the traumatic event(s)
    2. • Feeling of nervousness, edginess
    3. • Avoidance of triggers that recall memories of traumatic event
    4. • Difficulty concentrating or sleeping
    5. • Feelings of loss of control

    PTSD symptoms fall into three categories. People with PTSD will have at least one symptom in each of these categories:9

      1. Reliving or re-experiencing the trauma
      1. Attempts to avoid thoughts, situations, or people that are reminders of the trauma
      1. Increased anxiety or arousal, including being constantly on guard for danger, and being easily startled

    Survivors with PTSD are more likely to experience substance abuse, phobias, chronic pain, poor concentration, sleep disturbances, and extreme anxiety. Some survivors with PTSD might become easily angered, whereas others may feel overwhelming guilt because others have died and they have survived. The client in Supporting Mr. Awondo: How Past Traumatic Events Can Be Triggers fits the description of someone living with PTSD.

    Supporting Mr. Awondo: How Past Traumatic Events Can Be Triggers

    Jesus Awondo, 44, single, fled to Canada 20 years ago from a South American country. Two weeks ago, at work, he fell from a scaffold and broke numerous bones in his body. After being discharged from hospital, he was cared for at home by Bob, a support worker. Bob came to Mr. Awondo’s house every day to assist him with his activities of daily living.

    While caring for him, Bob noticed that Mr. Awondo usually fell asleep after his bed bath but never had a quiet sleep. Yesterday, while he was sleeping, Mr. Awondo started to scream, mumble loudly, and try to swing his cast-covered arms around in the air. When Mr. Awondo woke up, Bob told him about what he had observed.

    Mr. Awondo said that in his home country, he had watched his wife and child being killed with machetes by the local drug gangs. He had been bound, gagged, and placed in a cage, where rats were allowed to crawl on him. He told Bob that he had frequent nightmares about these events, and since his injury at work, his nightmares had increased in their duration and frequency. Bob reported the event to his supervisor, who arranged for Mr. Awondo’s doctor to see him in his outpatient clinic. Mr. Awondo’s medical treatment was then changed and resulted in Mr. Awondo sleeping more peacefully at night, in spite of an occasional nightmare.

    Supporting Clients With Anxiety Disorders

    1. • Avoid situations that are known to cause anxiety. For example, if a client is afraid of small, closed spaces, keep the person away from these.
    2. • Avoid discussing subjects that cause anxiety. Keep the conversation on other subjects.
    3. • Provide comfort during periods of anxiety. Stay with the client who is extremely anxious or is having a panic attack. Use touch to reassure the client, if appropriate. Offer limited, clear choices to make decision making easier so as not to induce further stress. Report the situation to your supervisor.

    Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in Children

    Children who have experienced traumatic events may develop fear of the dark, fear of being alone, separation anxiety, nightmares, stomach aches, or headaches. They are often afraid to talk to their parents about the events for fear of upsetting them.10

    Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in Older Persons

    The signs and symptoms of PTSD in older persons are often the same as those in children. Many older people may have been traumatized when they were much younger, and extreme changes in their lives may trigger PTSD symptoms—for example, deaths of friends and family members or sudden loss of daily social contact. In long-term care facilities, rough treatment or a feeling of a loss of control when receiving care may trigger symptoms of PTSD in clients.10

    Supporting Clients With Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder

    As a support worker, you should be aware of the following:

    1. • As with all clients, treat the client with PTSD according to the DIPPS principles. Personal care should be provided in a gentle manner that is respectful of the client’s right to privacy.
    2. • Clients should be given the opportunity to talk or reminisce about their experiences. Refrain from using statements such as “Everything will be fine.”
    3. • Clients may have witnessed traumatic events in their past, and PTSD may be triggered at any time .
    4. • Be aware of changes in the client’s willingness to complete activities of daily living (ADLs). Change in a client’s willingness to be toileted, dressed, undressed, bathed, or fed may be a clue that the client has PTSD. Such a reaction should be reported to the supervisor immediately and always be documented.

    Bipolar and Related Disorders

    Bipolar and related disorders, formerly known as manic-depressive illness, are a group of related brain disorders that cause unusual shifts in a person’s mood, energy, and ability to function, different from the normal ups and downs that everyone goes through. About 1% of Canadians will experience this disorder.1 Like diabetes or heart disease, bipolar and related disorders are long-term illnesses that must be carefully managed throughout a person’s life. A manic episode is diagnosed if elevated mood occurs with three or more of the other symptoms of mania most of the day, nearly every day, for 1 week or longer. If the person’s mood is irritable, four additional symptoms must be present for the diagnosis.

    A person with bipolar disorder has emotional lows (depression) and emotional highs (mania) and thoughts, moods, and behaviours that swing from normal to grandiose and then to depressed. These symptoms can result in damaged relationships, poor job or school performance, and even suicide. Bipolar disorder is treatable, however, and with proper treatment, individuals with this disorder can lead full and productive lives.

    Bipolar disorder tends to run in families. Signs and symptoms can range from mild to severe and typically develop in late adolescence or early adulthood. However, some people exhibit the first symptoms during childhood, and others, late in life. It is often not recognized as an illness, and some affected people may suffer for years before it is properly diagnosed and treated. Children and adolescents can also develop bipolar disorder; it is more likely to occur in children of parents with the disorder. Unlike many adults with bipolar disorder, whose episodes tend to be more clearly defined, children and young adolescents with the disorder often experience very fast mood swings between depression and mania many times within a day. Children with mania are more likely to be irritable and prone to destructive tantrums than to be overly happy and elated. Mixed symptoms also are common in youths with bipolar disorder. Older adolescents who develop the illness may have the more classic, adult-type episodes and symptoms.

    Bipolar disorder in children and adolescents can be hard to distinguish from other issues, such as drug abuse, that occur in these age groups and have similar symptoms. As with any illness, effective treatment depends on accurate diagnosis. Children or adolescents with emotional and behavioural symptoms should be carefully evaluated by a mental health professional. Most people with bipolar disorder—even those with the most severe forms—can achieve substantial stabilization of their mood swings and related symptoms with proper treatment. Because bipolar disorder is a recurring illness, those affected need long-term preventive treatment.

    The symptoms of bipolar and related disorders usually occur in three phases: (1) the acute phase (when the symptoms are escalating), (2) the continuation phase (when the symptoms are still visible and the client is usually being treated), and (3) the maintenance phase (when the client’s acute symptoms have subsided). In most cases, the disorder is much better controlled if treatment is continuous than if it is periodical. However, even when there are no breaks in treatment, mood changes can occur and should be reported immediately to the doctor, who may be able to prevent a full-blown episode by making timely adjustments to the treatment plan. Working closely with the doctor and communicating openly about treatment concerns and options can contribute significantly to treatment effectiveness.

    Signs and Symptoms of Mania (or a Manic Episode)

    1. • Increased energy, activity, and restlessness
    2. • Excessive “high”; overly good, euphoric mood
    3. • Extreme irritability
    4. • Racing thoughts and very fast talking, jumping from one idea to another
    5. • Distractibility, inability to concentrate
    6. • Little need for sleep
    7. • Unrealistic beliefs in one’s abilities and powers
    8. • Poor judgement
    9. • Spending sprees
    10. • A prolonged period of behaviour that is different from usual
    11. • Increased libido (sex drive)
    12. • Abuse of drugs, particularly cocaine, alcohol, and sleep medications
    13. • Provocative, intrusive, or aggressive behaviour
    14. • Denial that anything is wrong

    Depressive Disorders

    Depressive disorders are a group of disorders in which the client has sad, empty, or irritable moods accompanied by physical changes and thought changes that are severe enough to affect the client’s ability to function. These disorders include major depressive disorder, persistent depressive disorder, premenstrual dysphoric disorder, substance/medication-induced depressive disorder, and depressive disorder due to another medical condition.

    Most people feel depressed at one time or another—for example, after the loss of a loved one, the loss of a job, or any other major negative event in life. Grief is a normal and natural feeling that all people experience during their lives (see Chapter 47 for a discussion of the normal stages of grief and loss). Feelings of loss and sadness are normal reactions to recent experiences and are sometimes referred to as reactive depression. This sort of depression is different from a major depressive disorder (which is sometimes called clinical depression).

    Major Depressive Disorder

    Major depressive disorder consists of severe feelings of worthlessness, self-blame, sadness, disappointment, and emptiness that last weeks. The physical and emotional effects of depression interfere with the person’s ability to perform the ADLs. It is sometimes called reactive depression, although this term is no longer used by physicians. Sleeping, eating, working, studying, and emotional affect (expression) are affected. A person with depression may think about or attempt suicide. Major depressive disorder may occur only once, brought on by a stressful event, such as the death of a loved one, a divorce, or a job loss, or it may recur throughout a person’s life and at any age. Once depression is recognized, medical intervention can make a difference for 80% of people who are affected, allowing them to get back to their regular activities.1 Depression is, unfortunately, common among older people (see the Focus on Older Persons: Depression box). For signs and symptoms of depression, see BOX 37.3.

    Major depressive disorder is usually treated by any (or a combination) of the following:

    1. • Psychotherapy —a form of therapy in which a person explores thoughts, feelings, and behaviours with the help and guidance from a mental health specialist.
    2. • Drug therapy —medications prescribed by a doctor. Street drugs, abuse of over-the-counter (OTC) medication, and alcohol interfere with the helping action of many medicines and, therefore, will usually be discouraged.
    3. • Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)— a short-term treatment that alters brain chemicals. Before ECT, clients are administered anaesthesia to put them in a dreamlike state and medications to relax their muscles. An electrical current is then briefly sent to the brain through electrodes placed on the client’s temples, which causes a seizure. It is not known exactly how ECT works, but it is believed that it alters brain chemicals, serotonin, endorphins, and adrenalin.

    Seasonal Affective Disorder

    Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is a type of depression that occurs each year at the same time, usually starting in fall or winter and ending in spring or early summer. It is common in colder regions, where people tend to spend the majority of their winter months indoors. Signs and symptoms may be mild to severe (see BOX 37.3), and the condition is often mistaken for major depression. The treatment, however, may be different—SAD is usually treated with phototherapy, which involves exposure to light from a box containing white fluorescent light tubes.

    Focus on Older Persons

    Depression

    Depression is common among older people, in part because they experience many losses—the death of family members and friends, diagnoses of medical conditions that lead to health impairment, loss of body functions, and loss of independence. Other factors that may trigger depression in older persons include the following:

    1. • Loneliness
    2. • Loss of a beloved pet, friend, and/or partner
    3. • Poor nutrition
    4. • Overuse, underuse, or misuse of prescribed medications
    5. • Adverse effects of some medications
    6. • Loss of control over finances
    7. • Memory impairment
    8. • Recent transition from independent living to a long-term care setting

    Depression in older persons is often overlooked or misdiagnosed. Many individuals will not admit to feeling depressed but will instead complain of having many aches and pains; others may be agitated and irritable. An older client with depression may be thought to have dementia (or delirium; see Chapter 38), so the depression goes untreated. Ways to reduce the likelihood of depression in older clients include the following:

    1. • Ensuring the client has adequate nourishing food and water
    2. • Ensuring the client has regular bowel movements
    3. • Ensuring the client has regular medical checkups by a family physician or nurse practitioner
    4. • Ensuring the client takes medications correctly
    5. • Ensuring the client avoids excessive use of OTC medications or alcohol
    6. • Providing support and encouraging involvement with others
    7. • Providing opportunities for physical activity
    8. • Providing opportunities to be outside in the fresh air and sunlight
    9. • Providing opportunities to interact with pets
    10. • Providing opportunities for stimulation of the senses
    11. • Providing opportunities to interact with reminiscence groups
    12. • Providing situations that allow the client to feel wanted and needed

    Other Types of Depressive Disorders

    The following disorders do not fit within the categories above but may affect some clients:

    1. • Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD). Much worse than temper tantrums, DMDD begins in children under the age of 10 years of age and is characterized by severe and recurrent temper outbursts that are exceedingly out of proportion in length and severity for the situation.
    2. • Persistent depressive disorder. Persistent depressive disorder is diagnosed when a client has a depressed mood most of the day for at least 2 years plus at least four of the following symptoms: poor eating (or overeating), insomnia or sleeping for long periods of time, low energy or fatigue, low self-esteem, poor concentration, feelings of hopelessness.
    3. • Premenstrual dysphoric disorder. At least five of the following symptoms appear in the week prior to each menstrual period and then are minimized or absent in the week after each period: mood swings, irritability, depressed moods, anxiety or tension, decreased interest in usual activities, difficulty concentrating, and a sense of feeling out of control.
    4. • Substance/medication-induced depressive disorder. This disorder is characterized by unexplained depressed mood or extreme lack of interest in all or most activities after becoming intoxicated, after withdrawing from intoxication, or after exposure to a medication.

    Supporting Clients With a Depressive Disorder

    1. • Show that you enjoy being with the client. Listen to your client. Show an interest in the client’s life.
    2. • Know that most clients who have never been diagnosed with depression do not know that they are depressed. Many clients will not say they are depressed because they are not familiar with the symptoms.
    3. • Do not minimize the client’s mood. Do not say, “Cheer up” or “Snap out of it.” Such statements suggest that the client is exaggerating the condition and has control over it.
    4. • Be positive. One positive experience may encourage further positive experiences.
    5. • Encourage rest. It can refresh the client and improve energy.
    6. • Encourage proper nutrition. Maintaining normal blood sugar and energy levels is important.
    7. • Encourage activity and social interactions. Participation in such activities can improve the client’s outlook and sense of well-being. However, do not tire the client with activities.
    8. • Be alert for warning signals of suicidal intent (BOX 37.4). Report any signals to your supervisor at once (see Suicidal Behaviour Disorders, on page 885).
    9. • Provide a safe, secure, stable environment. Clients with depression may feel less control over their environment but will be comforted by a feeling of safety.
    10. • Follow the care plan. The care plan may state, for example, that the client may not have access to medication bottles but may take medications from a dosette or blister-packaged container only (see Chapter 45).

    BOX 37.4 Warning Signs of Suicidal Intent

    It is important to understand that suicidal feelings and actions are symptoms of an illness that can be treated. While some suicide attempts are carefully planned over time, others are impulsive acts that have not been thought through.

    Warnings signs of suicidal intent include the following:

    1. • Signs and symptoms of depression
    2. • Repeated expressions of hopelessness, helplessness, or desperation
    3. • Expressions of interest in killing oneself
    4. • Having a plan for suicide, such as taking pills or hanging oneself at a specific place and time
    5. • Loss of interest in friends, hobbies, or previously enjoyed activities
    6. • Giving away prized possessions or putting personal affairs in order
    7. • Telling someone else about final wishes
    8. • A change in personality or mood—for example, sudden and unusually happy behaviour after a period of depression
    9. • A change in appearance—for example, a person who is usually well groomed is untidy and unwashed
    10. • Failure to recover from a loss or crisis
    11. • A sudden tendency to take big risks
    12. • Refusing to eat, drink, or take medications

    If you are feeling suicidal or know someone who is, take the following actions:

    1. • Call a doctor, emergency room, or 9-1-1 right away to get immediate help.
    2. • Make sure you (or the suicidal person) are not left alone.
    3. • Prevent access to large amounts of medication, weapons, or other items that could be used for self-harm.

    With proper treatment, suicidal feelings can be overcome.

    1. • Ensure safety. Sharp items, belts, cords, or items similar to these may be locked away if the client is voicing intentions of suicide.
    2. • Encourage normal activities. The client should be encouraged to do their ADLs or be assisted as needed—for example, with bathing, preparing meals, and taking in adequate fluids. These measures can maintain the body’s homeostasis and prevent other health issues.

    Disruptive, Impulse-Control, and Conduct Disorders

    Disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders are disorders in which clients display difficult, disruptive, aggressive, or antisocial behaviours; these disorders are often associated with physical or verbal injury to self, others, or objects or with violating the rights of others. These behaviours can appear in several forms and can be defensive, premeditated, or impulsive. They are grouped into three types: (1) angry/irritable mood, (2) argumentative/defiant behaviour, and (3) vindictiveness. Clients with dementia may display aggressive behaviours (see Chapter 38), but those behaviours are not premeditated and are merely responses to the environment.

    Oppositional Defiant Disorder

    Clients with oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) repeatedly engage in a pattern of defiant, disobedient, and hostile behaviour toward authority figures, beginning in childhood. This behaviour goes beyond acceptable misbehaviour for the given child’s age and continues for at least 6 months.

    Symptoms of Oppositional Defiant Disorder in Clients

    1. • Resistance to or stubbornness toward taking direction from others
    2. • Unwillingness to compromise
    3. • Frequent anger or resentfulness

    BOX 37.5 Dealing With Your Own Anger

    1. • Understand the hurt and fear underlying the anger. Remember that anger stems from fear and a sense of helplessness, such as a feeling that you are losing control of a situation. The real threat may not be the surface issue (e.g., someone being late to the movie) but the underlying issue (e.g., not being important to someone you love or being mistreated). Identifying emotions of fear and hurt will open the door to recognizing and dealing with underlying issues.
    2. • Develop empathetic understanding. If you choose to decrease your anger at someone, the first step is to make every effort to see the situation from the other person’s point of view. You might begin by asking for an explanation of that person’s perspective and then try to understand that person’s situation, point of view, and the reasons for that person’s beliefs and behaviour. Not having empathetic understanding is usually the major hurdle to getting control of anger. Forgiving is not forgetting; it is remembering and letting go.
    3. • Assume the best intentions (whenever possible). Try to assume the best intentions in others until there are repeated indications for other motives.
    4. • Understand that sometimes “ life is not fair.” There is nothing “fair” about some people being born into happy, prosperous families and living prosperous, long, happy lives, while other people are born into miserable situations and die young after leading a life filled with suffering. This discrepancy is part of reality, and it is pointless to get angry about it. Make the most of what you have.
    5. • Do not hold on to anger for motivation. Do you want to do something just to punish the person you are angry with or to “get even”? Holding on to anger or hurt can only hurt you! Wait until you have calmed down, and then talk to the person, stating reasons for your actions calmly. This course of action is much more effective than giving punishment out of anger!
    6. • Examine underlying expectations of other people. Unfulfilled expectations can lead to anger. What are your expectations of yourself and of others for a given situation? Are you expecting more than is realistic from this person in this particular situation? People are who they are, and one of the root causes of anger is not accepting people (or events) as they are.

    7. • Choose happiness instead of anger. People who habitually choose anger over happiness lead frustrated, angry lives—not happy lives. Remind yourself of this fact to get more control of your anger. Say to yourself, “Self, why choose anger when you can choose thoughts that produce happiness?”

    8. • Frequent cruel or revenge-seeking behaviours

    9. • Frequent arguments with, talking back to, or challenging of authority
    10. • Irritable, resentful, or negative attitude
    11. • Provoking others, which comes across as mean, spiteful, or rude behaviours
    12. • Displaying temper tantrums or outbursts
    13. • Always blaming others or denying responsibility
    14. • Repeated trouble at school
    15. • Few or no friends

    Intermittent Explosive Disorder

    The client with intermittent explosive disorder has recurrent behavioural outbursts, indicating a failure to control aggressive impulses. The outburst may be verbal or physical, and the anger is caused by the inability to cope mentally with a situation. If your clients (or others you know) have persistent problems with anger, they may have important underlying issues that they have not yet resolved—for example, a perceived loss of control in their values being met. They also may be using ineffective emotional coping methods. Many of the calming methods that a person uses to help deal with negative emotions can help when dealing with the person’s anger as well, such as taking a deep breath and walking away to “cool down.” These outbursts usually have social, financial, or legal consequences. Sometimes support workers have to learn to manage their own anger as well (BOX 37.5).

    Symptoms of Intermittent Explosive Disorder

    1. • Intentional behaviours that cause physical or emotional harm to others
    2. • Frequent impulsive actions
    3. • Frequent irritability
    4. • Frequent outbursts of anger
    5. • Attempts to provoke others or retaliate against behaviours of others

    Conduct Disorder

    Clients with conduct disorder repeatedly violate the personal or property rights of others and society. This disorder usually begins in childhood with less severe behaviours such as lying and shoplifting and then progresses to more severe behaviours such as burglary or auto theft, as the client develops increased strength, cognitive abilities, and sexual maturity. The causes of this disorder vary but may include severe family dysfunction with exposure to constant or aggressive bickering between parents, limited financial and emotional resources, instability, disturbances of family values, peer rejection, and poor performance in school.

    Symptoms of Conduct Disorder in Clients

    1. • Bullying, threatening, or intimidating others
    2. • Engaging in antisocial behaviours
    3. • Having difficulty with authority figures
    4. • Frequently starting fights
    5. • Using weapons or tools capable of causing serious physical harm to people or property
    6. • Being physically cruel to animals or people
    7. • Stealing while confronting a victim (e.g., mugging, purse snatching, armed robbery)
    8. • Using physical violence toward others (in the form of rape, assault, murder, etc.)
    9. • Destruction of property (such as starting fires or breaking windows)
    10. • Frequently “conning,” manipulating, or telling lies to obtain things and favours or to avoid debts or obligations
    11. • Staying out at night despite parents’ curfew rules (before the age of 13)
    12. • Repeatedly running away from home, or running away from home for a lengthy period
    13. • Abusing alcohol or drugs

    Supporting Clients With Disruptive, Impulse-Control, and Conduct Disorders

    1. • Remember that as a support worker, you must accept clients in a caring, nonjudgemental way. Clients with disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders may lack insight into their feelings and not be able to deal with them in the moment.
    2. • When approaching the client, maintain a safe distance between the two of you. A client who feels threatened may strike out at you.
    3. • Ensure that you keep an open pathway to an exit in case the client’s actions become aggressive.
    4. • Avoid doing anything that might escalate the situation. For example, do not touch the client.
    5. • If clients are verbally aggressive but not physically aggressive, try to determine the cause of their anger. Determine if they are cold, hungry, in pain, or frightened about something.
    6. • Try to understand the client’s anger in the cultural context. Some men, for example, would rather yell and scream than admit that they are frightened or in pain.
    7. • You do have the right to ask the client to speak to you politely as a professional. Do not yell back, but remain calm and professional yourself.
    8. • Most employers that provide support services to clients with anger management issues will offer (or encourage you to take on your own) courses such as “Managing Disruptive Behaviours” so that you can learn to deal with aggressive clients in a nonconfrontational manner.
    9. • If the client is verbally aggressive, your main concern is your safety and the safety of others. You should leave the situation and immediately call your supervisor. If the client is threatening to harm you physically, you should get to a safe place and call the police immediately. Refer to the section Managing Responsive Behaviours in Chapter 38, on page 914.
    10. • Identifying clients’ important life values and understanding their lack of confidence in their ability to be happy may help you to overcome clients’ anger. With approval from your supervisor, gently inform clients that they need to realize that anger is a choice they have made and that it is not helpful to blame others (or themselves). For some clients, remaining angry can simply be the easiest way out.
    11. • Encourage the client to find new ways to think about a situation. Remind the client that making oneself happy requires skillful effort and mental control.

    Feeding and Eating Disorders

    Feeding and eating disorders are altered body image perceptions that lead to disturbances in eating behaviours and an abnormal concern about body weight and shape. These occur mainly in teenage girls and young women, although there are increasing numbers of men who develop eating disorders. Eating disorders can be life-threatening.

    Eating disorders are influenced by biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors. One reason younger women and men are particularly vulnerable to feeding and eating disorders is that they value an “ideal” figure and go on strict diets to achieve this. Others especially at risk for feeding and eating disorders include athletes, actors, dancers, models, and TV personalities for whom thinness has become a professional requirement. Some affected individuals recover, but some do not. The two most common eating disorders are anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa.

    Feeding and eating disorders are not the same as the anorexia that clients in a long-term care facility may have. Feeding and eating disorders usually occur in young people and may signal a much deeper, more complex psychological issue, whereas an older person may refuse to eat because of a fear of choking, a sick feeling, depression, or a dislike for the texture or taste of the food.

    People who have an ongoing preoccupation with food and weight, even when they are thin, often benefit from exploring their thoughts and relationships with help and guidance from a therapist. Early diagnosis and treatment can help ensure the most successful outcome. Some clients can be treated as outpatients, but others may need hospitalization to correct a dangerously low weight and food bingeing behaviours. The most effective strategies for treatment have been weight restoration to within 10% of normal—weight gain of 2.2 to 6.6 kg (1 to 3 lb.) per week is considered safe and desirable—and individual, family, and group therapies. Fortunately, most of the complications experienced by clients with anorexia nervosa are reversible once their normal weight is restored. However, clients with bulimia nervosa may suffer permanent damage to their bodies, especially to their teeth because of enamel wear or to their throats and esophagus due to the highly acidic stomach contents backwashing onto their teeth during their frequent purging episodes. For these clients, proper oral and dental care must be emphasized.

    Anorexia Nervosa

    Anorexia nervosa is a serious, complex, and often chronic, life-threatening feeding and eating disorder defined by three key features:11

    1. • A refusal to maintain minimal body weight within 15% of an individual’s normal weight
    2. • An intense fear of gaining weight
    3. • A distorted body image of being “fat” or “ugly”

    Like all feeding and eating disorders, anorexia nervosa tends to occur around puberty but can develop during any major life change. Anorexia nervosa predominately affects adolescent girls and young women, although it also occurs in men and older women. Some clients with female genitalia with this illness have amenorrhea (absence of at least three consecutive menstrual periods as normally expected to occur) as a result.

    Personality traits common in persons with anorexia nervosa are low self-esteem, social isolation (which usually occurs after behaviours associated with anorexia nervosa begin), and perfectionism. These clients tend to be good students and excellent athletes. It seems clear (although not to the client) that focusing on weight loss and food allows the client to ignore other issues that are too painful or seem impossible to resolve. The satisfaction of achieving control over weight and food becomes very important, even though the rest of the person’s life may be chaotic and emotionally painful.

    Generally, a doctor who specializes in eating disorders should make the diagnosis and rule out other physical disorders. Other psychiatric disorders, such as depression and obsessive–compulsive disorder, can occur along with anorexia nervosa. In addition, some people will engage in recurrent binge eating and purging episodes. Starvation, weight loss, and related medical complications can result in death. In fact, anorexia nervosa has the highest mortality rate (6%) among all psychiatric conditions.

    People with anorexia nervosa ignore hunger and thus control their desire to eat. This desire is frequently masked by cooking for others or hiding food in personal spaces. Obsessive exercising may accompany the starving behaviour, which may mislead others into thinking that the person is healthy.

    Signs and Symptoms of Anorexia Nervosa

    1. • Obsession with food and weight
    2. • Refusal to maintain minimal normal body weight
    3. • Thoughts of looking fat in spite of being bone-thin
    4. • Brittle nails and hair
    5. • Dry and yellow skin
    6. • Cessation (stoppage) of menstruation
    7. • Depression
    8. • Frequent complaints of feeling cold (because body temperature drops)
    9. • Lanugo (fine hair, usually on a newborn) on the body
    10. • Strange eating habits such as cutting food into tiny pieces
    11. • Refusal to eat in front of others
    12. • Preparing elaborate dishes for others but not eating them
    13. • Abuse of laxatives and enemas to rid the body of food
    14. • Abuse of diuretics (drugs that cause the kidneys to produce large amounts of urine, through which extra fluid in the body is lost, causing weight loss)

    Causes

    Feeding and eating disorders tend to run in families, with female relatives most often affected—for example, a girl has a 10 to 20 times higher risk of developing anorexia nervosa if she has a sibling with the disease. Behavioural and environmental influences, as well as stressful events, are likely to increase the risk for feeding and eating disorders.

    Anorexia Nervosa’s Effects on the Body

    1. • Damage to vital organs such as the heart and brain
    2. • Irregular heart rhythms or heart failure
    3. • Calcium loss from bones, which can become brittle and prone to breakage

    The worst outcome for people with anorexia is starving themselves to death.

    Bulimia Nervosa

    Bulimia nervosa is a psychological feeding and eating disorder that is characterized by episodes of binge eating followed by inappropriate methods of weight control (purging). Binge eating is a compulsion in which an individual eats a much larger amount of food than most people would in a similar situation—sometimes up to 20 000 calories at a time—during periods of uncontrolled, impulsive, or continuous eating. Binge eating is not a response to intense hunger but is usually a response to depression, stress, or low self-esteem. During a binge-eating episode, the individual experiences a loss of control, which is followed by a short-lived calmness and later self-loathing. The cycle of overeating and purging usually becomes an obsession and is repeated often.

    The foods binged on are usually considered “comfort foods”—sweet foods that are high in calories or smooth and soft foods such as ice cream, cake, and pastry. An individual may binge anywhere from twice a day to several times daily.

    People with bulimia nervosa can look perfectly normal. Most of them are of normal weight, but some may be overweight. Women with bulimia tend to be high achievers. It is often difficult to recognize bulimia because bingeing and purging are done in secret and individuals with the condition often deny having it.

    Currently, the causes of bulimia are not definitively known but are generally thought to be similar to those that trigger anorexia nervosa.

    Signs and Symptoms of Bulimia Nervosa

    1. • Eating uncontrollably
    2. • Purging
    3. • Strict dieting
    4. • Fasting
    5. • Vigorous exercising
    6. • Vomiting or abusing laxatives or diuretics in an attempt to lose weight
    7. • Vomiting blood
    8. • Using the bathroom frequently after meals
    9. • Preoccupation with body weight
    10. • Depression or mood swings; feeling “out of control”
    11. • Swollen glands in neck and face
    12. • Heartburn
    13. • Bloating
    14. • Indigestion
    15. • Constipation
    16. • Irregular menstrual periods
    17. • Poor dental health
    18. • Sore throat
    19. • Weakness and exhaustion
    20. • Bloodshot eyes
    21. • Compulsive exercising
    22. • Excessive concern about shape and weight

    Bulimia Nervosa’s Effects on the Body

    1. • Erosion of tooth enamel because of repeated exposure to acidic gastric contents through vomiting
    2. • Dental cavities; sensitivity to hot or cold food
    3. • Swelling and soreness in the salivary glands (from repeated vomiting)
    4. • Stomach ulcers
    5. • Rupture of the stomach and esophagus
    6. • Disruption in the normal bowel release function
    7. • Electrolyte imbalance
    8. • Dehydration
    9. • Irregular heartbeat
    10. • A greater risk for suicidal behaviour
    11. • Decreased libido (sex drive)

    Pica

    Pica is a feeding and eating disorder defined as persistently eating substances without nutritional value for a period of at least a month. Clients who have been diagnosed with pica have been reported to put in their mouth and/or eat a wide variety of nonfood substances, such as clay, dirt, sand, stones, pebbles, hair, feces, lead, laundry starch, vinyl gloves, plastic, pencil erasers, ice, fingernails, paper, paint chips, coal, chalk, wood, plaster, light bulbs, needles, string, cigarette butts, wire, and burnt matches.12

    Although pica is more common in children, it is the most common feeding and eating disorder in clients with developmental disabilities. Depending on what is eaten, pica may not be harmful, may cause some health concerns (such as chipped teeth), or may cause life-threatening conditions such as intestinal obstructions.

    Causes of Pica

    Nutritional deficiencies such as iron-deficiency anemia and zinc deficiency may cause pica. Pica may also be caused by poor diet, malnutrition, starvation, developmentally poor judgement, or obsessive–compulsive disorder.

    Supporting Clients With Feeding and Eating Disorders

    People can and do recover from eating disorders. These points may assist you when supporting someone with a feeding and eating disorder:

    1. • Be patient. Do not be surprised by your client’s reaction of anger to or denial of your presence. There is a lot of shame and pain associated with having a feeding and eating disorder.
    2. • Be compassionate. Feeding and eating disorders are complex conditions, and food and body weight issues are only symptoms of strong cultural body-image messages or may represent deeper issues.
    3. • Be encouraging. Encourage these client to see themselves as more than their feeding and eating disorders. Do so by talking about other aspects of their lives and interests that are unrelated to food.
    4. • Be nonjudgemental. Do not blame or shame the client. Avoid commenting on the client’s looks—even if you are trying to compliment the client, comments about weight and appearance only reinforce the client’s obsession with body image and weight.
    5. • Be positive. Avoid negative statements about behaviours. Find neutral, comfortable places and times to discuss issues.
    6. • Prevent the client from eating nonfood substances. Clients with pica should be prevented from continuing to eat nonfoods so that medical issues, such as nutritional deficiency or lead toxicity, can be diagnosed. Medical treatment for the client may vary according to the severity of the cravings and the cause of the pica and may include psychosocial therapy or medications.
    7. • Report to your supervisor if you hear vomiting or see evidence of food refusal. Ask your supervisor for guidance on ways to deal with these symptoms appropriately.

    Obsessive–Compulsive and Related Disorders

    Obsessive–compulsive and related disorders are mental health disorders characterized by recurrent obsessions and compulsions that interfere with a person’s daily life and relationships.8 An obsession is a persistent thought or desire, and a compulsion is the uncontrollable urge to perform an act. The obsession may be violent in nature and is usually disturbing to the person; the person may try to ignore the obsessive thought or deal with it by repeating an act over and over again. Examples of common obsessions include the following:

    1. • Fearing that one’s thoughts will cause someone harm
    2. • Ruminating about (being preoccupied with) hurting someone

    As the support worker caring for a client with obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), if you observe repeated behaviours, you should report them to your supervisor. You should be aware that the client performs these behaviours to help relieve stress, and the person may not be able to stop without feeling extremely stressed and anxious.

    Examples of common compulsions include the following:

    1. • Repeatedly washing hands, bathing, or cleaning household items
    2. • Checking things several times a day—for example, checking if the stove is off, the windows are locked, and so on
    3. • Repeating a name, phrase, or tune
    4. • Preoccupations with throwing things away that have little value, or the opposite, excessive hoarding

    Hoarding Disorder

    Hoarding disorder is defined as a persistent anxiety and distress in parting with possessions or animals, regardless of their actual value. This behaviour can lead to health concerns for the client due to the accumulation of dust, mould, or excrement; cramped living conditions; and strained relationships with significant others. About 75% of clients with hoarding disorder also live with an anxiety disorder.

    Other Obsessive–Compulsive and Related Disorders

    1. • Body dysmorphic disorder —preoccupation with perceived flaws in one’s own body that are not observable to others
    2. • Trichotillomania (hair pulling) disorder —the need to pull out one’s hair, resulting in hair loss. This hair pulling can cause significant personal and social distress.8
    3. • Excoriation (skin picking) disorder —the need to recurrently pick one’s own skin, resulting in skin lesions. This skin picking causes significant personal and social distress.
    4. • Substance/medication-induced obsessive–compulsive and related disorder —body-related repetitive behaviours that are triggered by exposure to or intoxication from a substance or medication

    Supporting a Client With Obsessive–Compulsive and Related Disorders

    1. • Show that you enjoy being with the client. Listen to the client. Show an interest in the client’s life.
    2. • Do not minimize the client’s thoughts and behaviours. Clients with OCD usually understand that their experiences do not make sense. Trying to argue over one’s obsessions or compulsions does not help anyone.
    3. • Be positive. One positive experience may encourage further positive experiences.
    4. • Encourage rest. It can refresh the client.
    5. • Avoid “helping” behaviours around OCD. Helping behaviours, such as ensuring that a client avoids things that cause anxiety, can make practising healthy coping skills harder for a client in the long run. Instead, you may be more helpful to the client if you focus on the feelings behind the behaviours.
    6. • Provide a safe, secure, stable environment. Signs of OCD can be more difficult to manage during times of stress—even happy occasions can be stressful. Recognize when a client may need extra support and try to plan ahead.
    7. • Follow the care plan. The care plan may state that the client cannot have access to medication bottles but that the person may take medications from a dosette or blister-packaged container only (see Chapter 45).
    8. • Encourage normal activities. The client should be encouraged to do ADLs and be assisted as needed with bathing, preparing meals, and taking in adequate fluids. These measures can maintain the body’s homeostasis and prevent other health issues.
    9. • Recognize accomplishments. Remember that for the client with OCD, every small step toward managing OCD behaviour can take a lot of courage and hard work.

    Personality Disorders

    Personality disorders are a group of disorders involving rigid and socially unacceptable behaviours that are not expected from a person of that culture. A personality disorder is identified by a pervasive pattern of experience and behaviour that is abnormal with respect to any two of the following: thinking, mood, personal relations, and the control of impulses. Individuals with personality disorders experience difficulties relating to others. They may be demanding, hostile, manipulative, inflexible, maladaptive, and antisocial. Because of their behaviours, people with personality disorders are not able to function well in society.

    Most personality disorders that begin as problems in personal development and character peak during adolescence. Personality disorders are not illnesses in a strict sense, as they do not disrupt emotional, intellectual, or perceptual functioning. However, individuals with personality disorders often live a life that is not positive, proactive, or fulfilling. As such, personality disorders are also associated with failure to reach one’s potential.

    In order to heal, individuals must first have the desire to change in order to break through the enduring pattern of a personality disorder. They must also gain insight into their inner experiences (e.g., severe or repeated trauma during childhood, such as abuse) and their behaviours. Healing is possible when individuals choose to take control of and are committed to changing their lives. Support systems (e.g., therapy, self-help groups, friends, family, medication) are usually necessary for full treatment of people with a personality disorder.

    According to the DSM-5, some of the main types of personality disorders are the following:

    1. • Antisocial personality disorder —a lack of regard for the moral or legal standards in the local culture or a marked inability to get along with others or abide by societal rules. People with this disorder are sometimes called psychopaths or sociopaths.
    2. • Avoidant personality disorder— marked social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy, and extreme sensitivity to criticism
    3. • Borderline personality disorder —lack of identity; rapid changes in mood; intense, unstable interpersonal relationships; impulsiveness; and lack of stability in the way one reacts to things and in self-image
    4. • Dependent personality disorder —an extreme need for other people, to the point of being unable to make any decisions or take an independent stand. People with this disorder also have a fear of separation, behave in a submissive manner, and lack decisiveness and self-confidence.
    5. • Histrionic personality disorder —exaggerated, almost theatrical, and often inappropriate displays of emotional reactions in everyday behaviour. People with this disorder shift their emotional expressions suddenly and rapidly.
    6. • Narcissistic personality disorder —general lack of empathy for others, a need to be admired by others, an inability to see the viewpoints of others, and hypersensitivity to the opinions of others
    7. • Obsessive–compulsive personality disorder —perfectionism and inflexibility. People with this disorder are preoccupied with uncontrollable patterns of thought and action.
    8. • Paranoid personality disorder —a marked distrust of others, including the belief, without reason, that others are exploiting, harming, or trying to deceive. People with this disorder have an overall lack of trust and believe that others are betraying them and that there are hidden meanings in messages.
    9. • Schizoid personality disorder —a very limited range of emotion, both in expression of and in experiencing emotions. People with this disorder are indifferent to social relationships.

    Common Signs and Symptoms of Personality Disorders

    1. • Self-centredness that manifests itself through a “me first,” self-preoccupied attitude
    2. • Lack of individual accountability that results in a victim mentality and blame of others, society, and the universe for one’s problems
    3. • Lack of perspective taking and empathy
    4. • Manipulative and exploitative behaviour
    5. • Unhappiness, depression, and other mood and anxiety disorders
    6. • Vulnerability to other mental health disorders, such as obsessive–compulsive tendencies and panic attacks
    7. • Distorted or superficial understanding of self and of others’ perceptions; an inability to see one’s own objectionable, unacceptable, disagreeable, or self-destructive behaviours or the issues that may have contributed to the personality disorder
    8. • Being socially maladaptive, changing the rules of the game, introducing new variables, or otherwise influencing the external world to conform to one’s own needs

    Supporting Clients With Personality Disorders

    1. • Remember that as a support worker, you must accept clients in a caring, nonjudgemental way. Clients with personality disorders may lack insight into their feelings and not be able to deal with them in the moment.
    2. • Speak to your client in a factual, professional manner.
    3. • Provide care and support as specified in the client’s care plan, in a respectful, efficient manner.
    4. • All staff members need to deal with the client in as consistent a manner as possible; otherwise, the client may attempt to play one staff member against another, creating havoc in a unit in a facility.
    5. • Just as you would with any other client in any other setting , refrain from giving your home phone number, email address, or information about your private life to the client to avoid any misunderstanding that doing so constitutes an invitation to private correspondence.
    6. • As with any client in any setting, do not accept gifts, money, or other items from your client. Doing so might be misinterpreted by your client as a sign of affection or special treatment.
    7. • Consult with the client’s family members or close friends for ways to successfully deal with that person.

    Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders

    Schizophrenia spectrum disorders, which affect 1% of Canadians, are extremely complex mental health disorders characterized by symptoms such as delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech (such as frequently becoming incoherent), grossly disorganized behaviours, and reduced or even dazed emotional expressions.8 These disorders affect a person’s ability to function in all aspects of life, including work, school, social life, family relationships, and self-care. Although there are many theories for the cause or causes of schizophrenia, a dominant theory proposes the existence of a biochemical imbalance in those affected.13

    Onset is usually between the late teens and the mid-30s, although late-onset cases (after age 45) have been reported. Men and women are affected equally, but onset is earlier in men (between ages 18 and 25) than in women (between ages 25 and 35). Although the onset of schizophrenia may be sudden, most cases display a variety of signs and symptoms gradually.14 It is also believed that substance and drug abuse may trigger underlying genetic vulnerabilities in some people.8

    People with schizophrenia spectrum disorders display one or more of the following:

    1. • Psychosis—a mental state in which a person’s perception of reality is impaired
    2. • Delusions—false beliefs that are not based on reality
    3. • Delusions of grandeur —false and exaggerated beliefs about the importance, talent, or wealth of oneself. For example, a woman believes she is a goddess or the prime minister.
    4. • Delusions of persecution —false beliefs about being mistreated, abused, or harassed. For example, a man believes that his neighbour wants to kill him.
    5. • Hallucination—seeing, hearing, tasting, smelling, or feeling things that are not real. For example, the person may see faces and hear voices that are not really there.
    6. • Paranoia—extreme suspicion about a person or a situation. For example, a person may imagine being watched, followed, or controlled by someone else and may have delusions of persecution.

    Without treatment, a person with schizophrenia suffers severe mental impairment. The person has difficulty relating to others, responses are inappropriate, and communication is disturbed. The person may ramble in speech or repeat what others say. Sometimes, the person’s speech cannot be understood.

    Some people with schizophrenia may have one severe psychotic episode that lasts for a month or more. Most individuals suffer signs and symptoms throughout their lives but may have periods of remission, when the signs and symptoms of the disease lessen or disappear.

    Some people with schizophrenia have frightening hallucinations and hear voices ordering them to harm themselves or others, and some may have friendly, peaceful hallucinations. Others may have very disorganized thoughts and speech. Some people with severe schizophrenia withdraw (stay away) from others and the world and may sit alone for hours without moving, speaking, or responding.

    Supporting Clients With Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders

    1. • Focus on one task or activity at a time so as to help the client to focus.
    2. • Be aware of your nonverbal communication. Avoid body language and facial expressions that could be considered threatening.
    3. • Do not argue with the client about a delusion or hallucination not being real; it is real to the client. You can suggest gently to the client that the delusion is not real.
    4. • Do not pretend that the delusion or hallucination is real. Comfort the client, and show empathy. Tell your supervisor if your client is having a delusion or hallucination.
    5. • Use distractions to avoid disturbing the client. For example, play music, or take the client for a walk.

    Self-Harm Disorders

    Self-harm means to inflict pain on oneself in order to deal with feelings of anxiety, depression, numbness, loss of control, or self-anger, or to regain emotional control over oneself. Not all people who self-harm live with a mental illness.15 People who self-harm do not do it to end their life; instead, it may be the best way they know to survive, or they do it because the act of self-harm may trigger the release of neurochemicals in the body, such as adrenaline, dopamine, and endorphins. These neurochemicals may reduce the physical and emotional distress the person feels at the time they self-harm.16 Some acts of self-harm are impulsive ones, but some people premeditate and plan to harm themselves.

    People are more likely to self-harm if they:15

    1. • Are teenagers. Self-harm affects 1 to 4% of the population, but rates of self-harm among teens range from 14 to 39%. This is perhaps because these teens feel extreme emotions, or they may have more conflicts with adults and may have to deal with loneliness and peer pressure.
    2. • Need to numb their personal inner pain. Certain people who self-harm say that they do not feel pain when they hurt themselves, or that they do it in order to feel physical pain.
    3. • Feel badly about themselves and have low self-esteem.
    4. • Feel they cannot express their feelings or have a hard time dealing with negative events or emotions.
    5. • Need to deal with abuse they may have endured (see Chapter 12).
    6. • Live with a mental illness. Self-harm is more common among people with a mental illness such as an eating disorder, depression, anxiety disorder, substance use disorder, and some personality disorders.

    The most common ways to self-harm include cutting or burning the skin, scratching that breaks the skin, hitting to the point of bruising or breaking bones, biting oneself, or falling. Some people also take a minor overdose of a substance (for example, they take more than the recommended dose, but not a lethal dose, of an OTC medication) to harm themselves.15

    It may be hard to tell if someone self-harms, because they may try to cover up their injuries if they are embarrassed, ashamed, or fear punishment. Warning signs of self-harm may include the following:

    1. • Unexplainable wounds like cuts, burns, or bruises
    2. • Presence of several unexplainable scars
    3. • Person’s insistence that they are “accident-prone” or have many accidents
    4. • Person’s tendency to cover their body, even during warm weather15

    Supporting Clients Who Self-Harm

    When supporting someone who self-harms, it is important to remember that this person is in distress and to act compassionately and nonjudgementally at all times.15 Other actions include the following:

    1. • Encourage the person to have any wounds properly treated, to avoid infection.
    2. • Focus on their concerns or issues rather than the act of self-harm itself.
    3. • Encourage positive, healthy coping methods. It takes time to learn the positive coping skills that can replace harmful coping skills.
    4. • Encourage open communication. Be willing to listen, without forcing the person to talk.
    5. • Encourage seeking professional help for behaviours.
    6. • Encourage friends and significant others to obtain support from a mental health professional or support group.

    Sleep–Wake Disorders

    Sleep–wake disorders are conditions in which repetitive disturbed sleep patterns lead to distress and impairment in the affected person’s daytime functioning. Sleep is absolutely essential for an individual’s normal, healthy functioning. Scientists and medical professionals do not fully understand this complex physiological phenomenon. It is not clear why the body requires sleep, although inadequate sleep can have severe detrimental effects on health.

    Studies have shown that sleep is essential for normal immune system function to maintain the ability to fight disease and sickness, for normal nervous system function, and for the ability to function both physically and mentally. In addition, sleep is necessary for learning and for normal, healthy cell growth. Sleep–wake disorders are generally classified into 10 conditions:8

      1. Lack of sleep (e.g., insomnia disorder)
      1. Breathing-related sleep disorders (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea, a medical condition)
      1. Hypersomnolence disorder (e.g., excessive sleep)
      1. Narcolepsy, a neurological disorder that affects the brain’s ability to control sleep and wakefulness cycles
      1. Circadian-rhythm sleep–wake disorders
      1. Nightmare disorder
      1. Nonrapid eye movement (NREM) disorder
      1. Rapid eye movement (REM) disorder
      1. Willis–Ekbom disease (restless legs syndrome)
      1. Substance/medication-induced sleep disorder

    In most cases, sleep disorders can be easily managed once they are properly diagnosed. Insomnia, which occurs more often in women and older persons, is the most common sleep disorder.

    The amount of sleep that a person needs to function normally depends on several factors. Infants sleep most of the day (about 16 hours); teenagers usually need about 9 hours of sleep a day; and adults need an average of 7 to 8 hours of sleep a day. Although older people require about as much sleep as younger adults do, they usually sleep for shorter periods and spend less time in the deep stages of sleep. About 50% of adults over the age of 65 have some type of sleep disorder, although it is not clear whether it is a normal part of aging or a result of medications that older people commonly use.

    Both REM and deep sleep are essential parts of the normal sleep cycle. Sleepwalking and bedwetting occur during deep sleep, and dreams occur during REM sleep, when the muscles of the body stiffen, eyes move, heart rate increases, breathing becomes more rapid and irregular, and blood pressure rises.

    Supporting Clients With Sleep–Wake Disorders

    1. • Instruct the client to avoid foods and medicines that alter the balance of chemicals that affect sleep. Caffeine, for example, can cause insomnia (lack of sleep). Antidepressant medications can cause loss of REM sleep. Smoking and alcohol can cause loss of REM sleep and deep sleep.
    2. • Observe if the client appears to have periods of not breathing at all, or if the client wakes up with a headache every morning. If so, sleep apnea should be suspected. Encourage the client to seek medical attention, as sleep apnea is a medical disorder that must be treated. The client might also benefit from sleeping on the side, with a pillow between the knees.
    3. • Clients who tend to sleep too much should see the doctor for a physical checkup to rule out narcolepsy, which is a medical condition.
    4. • Observe, report, and record how long the client is sleeping during each stage of sleep. Make a note of the client’s use of prescription or nonprescription drugs or alcohol, which might be causing any sleep disorders.

    Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders

    A substance-related and addictive disorder is the deliberate misuse of and inability to stop the abuse of substances either by themselves or in combination with another substance or substances. These substances include prescription medications, OTC medication and substances, illegal drugs, alcohol, or other substances, including marijuana. These disorders tend to result in problems in relationships and work. Abused substances affect the central nervous system—some have a calming or depressing effect, others having a stimulating effect on the mind and on thinking. Many are also mood-altering—that is, after taking the substance, users may feel happy, self-confident, and relaxed—but some drugs cause hallucinations, an exaggerated sense of one’s own abilities, or an emotional or aggressive state. People with this disorder may show evidence of tolerance and withdrawal. Drug tolerance occurs when the person needs larger and larger amounts of the substance to experience the same effect. Drug withdrawal is a physical reaction that occurs when the person stops taking the substance. Signs and symptoms of withdrawal, which can be severe, may include depression, agitation, abdominal cramps, nausea, diarrhea, and painful muscle spasms.

    Some people with addiction turn to criminal acts to support their habit. Without treatment, death is a risk; common causes of death are overdose, suicide, and diseases contracted from using contaminated needles.

    Treatment for substance-related and addictive disorder depends on the substance being abused. People who have abused any type of drugs, alcohol, or a combination of these will need detoxification—a process of allowing an abused substance to exit the body naturally or of removing the abused substance from the body medically. The person being detoxified usually goes into drug withdrawal, and usually requires hospital care. Almost all treatment programs involve psychotherapy with mental health professionals who help clients manage their issues.

    Alcohol Use Disorder

    Alcohol is abused more than any other substance. Signs and symptoms of alcohol abuse include intoxication (drunkenness), memory impairment, difficulty concentrating, tremors, and loss of interest in family and friends. These clients are at risk for liver, pancreas, and heart conditions. Many people who abuse alcohol have poor nutrition, and many are also addicted to nicotine (cigarettes), so they are at risk for lung disease and certain cancers. Women who drink during pregnancy can give birth to a baby with fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (see Chapter 41).

    The DSM-5 divides alcohol use disorder into mild, moderate, and severe subcategories. These subcategories are based on the extent of people’s alcohol use, their cravings for alcohol, and the extent to which alcohol has disrupted their lives.8

    For most people, exceeding even two drinks a day can do significant harm. Women who have more than 10 drinks a week have higher rates of cancer and other health concerns than do women who drink less.17 Men who have more than 15 drinks a week also have higher rates of alcohol-related problems.17 Some people drink every day, and some go for days or weeks without drinking and then drink a great deal within a short period. People with mental health disorders who are under stress or are lonely may turn to alcohol to feel better. Older people who live alone are at risk for alcohol abuse.

    People who misuse alcohol may be emotional, aggressive, and abusive, and their families are at risk for abuse and other difficulties. Some people who are “happy drunks” or the “life of the party” may not be violent toward others, but they certainly put their own health at risk, and if they drink and drive, they risk killing themselves and others on the road.

    The main treatment is to avoid drinking. Groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) help people with drinking cessation. Individual and family counselling is often needed as part of the treatment.18

    Supporting Clients With Substance-Dependence Disorders

    1. • Report suspicions of substance abuse at once. You may smell alcohol on a client’s breath, or you may observe that a client’s medication is running out more quickly than it should. This information is confidential. Do not discuss the matter with anyone but your supervisor.
    2. • Avoid confrontation. If you think a client is abusing a substance, do not argue with them about it. Tell your supervisor of the situation immediately. Do not discuss the matter with anyone else.
    3. • Never buy alcohol, drugs, or other substances for clients. Purchasing alcohol, drugs, or other substances for clients is unethical. Report any such requests from clients to your supervisor.

    Suicidal Behaviour Disorder

    Suicidal behaviour disorder is a disorder in which a person has recurrent thoughts of or a preoccupation with ending their own life. Suicide—to kill (caedere) oneself (sui)—is a common cause of death in individuals of all genders, from adolescence through middle age, although it has also been found to be the cause of death among some school-aged children and older persons.19 People with mental health disorders are at high risk for suicide. People who attempt suicide view life as unbearable and may believe that their families and friends are better off without them (see BOX 37.4 : Warning Signs of Suicidal Intent, on page 873).

    Suicide accounts for 24% of all deaths among 15- to 24-year-olds and 16% among 25- to 44-year-olds. It is one of the leading causes of death from adolescence to middle age.1 The mortality rate due to suicide among men is four times the rate among women, although, statistically, women attempt suicide more often than men do. Men tend to use violent means to die by suicide (e.g., hanging, firearms), so their attempts are often fatal. Women usually choose less violent means (e.g., taking an overdose of pills), so many survive their attempts.

    Attempted suicide is a sign of a serious mental health issue, and the person attempting suicide needs to seek professional care and be put on a suicide prevention program. Any child or adolescent who has suicidal feelings, talks about suicide, or attempts suicide should be taken seriously for suicide risk and should receive immediate help from a mental health specialist (see the Focus on Older Persons: Suicide box).

    Risk Factors That May Lead to Suicidal Intent or Behaviour

    Certain risk factors may trigger suicidal intent or behaviour:

    1. • Mental health disorder, especially depression, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia
    2. • A history of abuse
    3. • A family history of suicide
    4. • The suicide of a friend
    5. • A prior suicide attempt
    6. • A major crisis such as the loss of a relationship, family problems, loss of position in society, and work, money, or legal problems
    7. • Pressure to succeed
    8. • Isolation
    9. • Early losses in life
    10. • Sexual identity issues
    11. • Feelings of deep hopelessness and helplessness
    12. • Recent diagnosis of a life-threatening condition
    13. • Substance abuse

    For older persons, risk factors might also include the following:20

    1. • Personality factors, such as personality disorders, emotional instability, rigid personality, and poor coping skills
    2. • Medical illness, such as chronic pain; sensory impairment
    3. • Negative life events, such as loss or financial difficulties
    4. • Functional impairment, such as a loss of independence or difficulties performing ADLs

    Supporting Clients With Suicidal Intent

    1. • If a client talks about suicide, take the person seriously.
    2. • Tell your supervisor at once.
    3. • Do not leave the client alone.
    4. • Encourage the client to talk. Having a conversation gives your supervisor time to get help and also shows the client that you care. Be a good listener.
    5. • Do not minimize the client’s concerns. Do not make comments such as “You shouldn’t have such thoughts,” “Things will work out,” or “Look on the bright side.”
    6. • In a home care setting, stay with the client until help arrives. Your supervisor may send a nurse, a case manager, or emergency personnel to help the client.

    Focus on Older Persons

    Suicide

    Did you know that older persons over the age of 85 have the highest suicide rate of any group in Canada?21 Older people are at risk for suicide because they experience many losses, including the deaths of family members and friends, compromise of physical health, and impairment of cognitive (mental) abilities. Depression is common in older persons (see the Focus on Older Persons: Depression box, on page 871). According to the Canadian Medical Association, recent research indicates that the suicide rate is climbing among older men; right now across Canada, about 31 of every 100 000 older persons, especially those over 85 years old, die by suicide. Most of them are white males, and most die through violent means.

    Some older people choose suicide to avoid loneliness. For others, the loss of independence, such as losing their driver’s licence, or a serious illness makes suicide seem better than the threat of prolonged suffering or the thought of causing hardships for their families.

    Important Questions to Ask the Older Person You Suspect to Be Suicidal22

    Ask about the person’s feelings:

    1. • Do you feel tired of living?
    2. • Have you been thinking about ending your life?
    3. • Have you been thinking about suicide?

    Ask about a suicide plan:

    1. • Have you thought of specific ways to end your life?
    2. • Have you given your possessions away?
    3. • Have you asked someone to help you end your life or join you in death?
    4. • Have you collected pills in order to take an overdose?
    5. • Have you started to put a suicide in action?

    Ask about the person’s reasons to live:

    1. • What has kept you from harming yourself?
    2. • Who or what makes life so worth living that you would not harm yourself?

    As with any person who speaks of suicide, the older person with suicidal intentions should be carefully monitored and should be given medical treatment as soon as possible.

    Key Points

    • Mental illness can be caused by a combination of genetic, biological, personality, and environmental factors. It is a disturbance in a person’s ability to cope with or adjust to stress, affecting the person’s thinking, mood, and behaviours and impairing the person’s functioning.

    • Mental health is a state of mind in which a person copes with and adjusts to the stressors of everyday living in socially acceptable ways. It is influenced by three factors: (1) inherited characteristics, (2) childhood nurturing, and (3) life circumstances.

    • When caring for a person with a mental health disorder, as a support worker you need to follow the 10 principles outlined in BOX 37.2, on page 862.

    • Caring for a person with a mental health disorder usually involves a team approach.

    • Culture has a profound influence on understanding mental health disorders and their treatments.

    • Acquired brain injury (ABI) is a sudden injury to the brain (e.g., concussion, lack of oxygen, infection) that causes temporary or permanent damage.

    • Anxiety disorders (e.g., panic disorder, phobic disorder) are a group of mental health disorders in which fears and worries disproportionate to a situation affect a person’s normal function.

    • Trauma and stressor-related disorders are anxiety disorders that can develop after an individual has experienced or witnessed a major trauma (e.g., war, fire, accident, violent crime). Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is one type of trauma disorder. It can occur in people of all ages, races, cultures, and genders.

    • Bipolar and related disorders are brain disorders that cause unusual shifts in a person’s mood, energy, and ability to function.

    • Depressive disorders are a group of disorders in which the client has sad, empty, or irritable moods accompanied by thought changes. These disorders impair the client’s ability to function.

    • Depression in older persons is often overlooked or misdiagnosed, sometimes as dementia. Many older people do not admit to feeling depressed but instead complain of having aches and pains; others may be agitated and irritable.

    • Disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders are defined by clients’ display of difficult, disruptive, aggressive, or antisocial behaviours, resulting in physical or verbal injury to self, others, or objects or in a violation of the rights of others.

    • Feeding and eating disorders (e.g., anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, pica) are caused by altered body-image perceptions that lead to disturbances in eating behaviours and an abnormal concern about body weight and shape.

    • Obsessive–compulsive and related disorders are mental health disorders characterized by recurrent obsessions and compulsions that interfere with a person’s daily life and relationships.

    • A personality disorder is identified by a pervasive pattern of experience and behaviour that is abnormal with respect to any two of the following: thinking, mood, personal relations, and the control of impulses. Because of their behaviours, people with personality disorders are often not able to function well in society or relate well to others.

    • Schizophrenia spectrum disorder is an extremely complex mental health disorder characterized by delusions, hallucinations, disturbances in thinking, and withdrawal from social activity.

    • Self-harm means to inflict pain on oneself in order to deal with feelings of anxiety, depression, numbness, loss of control, or self-anger, or to regain emotional control over oneself. Not all people who self-harm have a mental illness. People who self-harm do not do it to end their life; instead, it may be the best way they know to survive.

    • Sleep–wake disorders are conditions in which repetitive disturbed sleep patterns lead to distress and impairment in daytime functioning.

    • A substance-related and addictive disorder is the deliberate misuse of and inability to stop the abuse of prescription medications, illegal drugs, alcohol, or other substances, as well as the resulting problems in relationships and work.

    • Health care workers need to be aware of the warning signs of suicidal intent (both in young people and in older persons) and know what to do if they suspect suicidal intent.

    • Suicidal behaviour disorder is diagnosed when a person has recurrent thoughts or a preoccupation with ending one’s own life.

This chapter addresses the changes in behaviour that take place over time as the brain is affected by aging, illness, or, again, lifestyle.     BOX 38.1 A Comparison of Normal and Abnormal Nervous System Changes

    Normal Changes Related to Aging Changes Related to Lifestyle, Disease, or Illness
    Some brain cells are lost. Nerve conduction slows. Vascular dementia is often a further result of stroke damage (Chapter 33). Having a stroke more than doubles someone’s risk of developing dementia.1
    Response and reaction times are slower. Reflexes are slower. Blood flow to the brain is reduced. Mild word-finding problems occur. Blood vessel problems prevent brain cells from getting the oxygen they need, causing them to die. This can impair at least one cognitive domain, such as attention, memory, language, perception, or executive function. This is known as vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) and is usually the result of stroke.1
    Vision and hearing decrease gradually with age. Illnesses such as diabetes, stroke, and head injury and some medications can cause increased vision or hearing impairment. Exposure to loud noises, extremely bright lights, or taking certain medications can cause blurring of vision or hearing impairment.
    Taste and smell decrease. Medications or chronic medical conditions, such as Parkinson’s disease or Alzheimer’s disease, can affect taste.2 Chronic allergies can affect taste or smell.
    Touch and sensitivity to pain decrease. Illnesses such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and head injury and some medications can cause an increased touch and pain sensitivity.
    Changes in sleep patterns occur. Stress, hormones, shift work, foods containing caffeine, or certain medications can cause changes in sleep patterns.

    The section Managing Responsive Behaviours near the end of this chapter describes some of the behaviours exhibited by people with dementia and suggests ways that caregivers can safely and respectfully support clients who demonstrate these disruptive and responsive behaviours.

    You should be aware that disorientation, delirium, and dementia are NOT a normal part of aging . The reasons a person may develop disorientation, delirium, or dementia are discussed in this chapter.

    Diseases that affect the brain can affect cognitive function (the term cognitive relates to brain or mind). Cognitive functioning involves the following functions of the brain:

    1. • Memory
    2. • Thinking
    3. • Reasoning
    4. • Ability to understand
    5. • Judgement
    6. • Behaviour

    Loss of cognitive function affects all dimensions of a person’s life. The state of having lost some or all ability to remember, think, reason, understand, or live independently is referred to as cognitive impairment.

    Disorientation

    Disorientation, also called confusion, refers to an impaired ability to recall people, time, or places as a result (or any combination) of physiological changes to the brain, a disease process, or substance-induced factors. In most circumstances, disorientation is a symptom of an underlying medical condition such as depression, electrolyte imbalance, or even a brain tumour. The word disorientation is a generic descriptive term, and refers only to the client’s disturbed orientation to people, time, or places. Disorientation can occur suddenly (such as after ingesting too much alcohol) or over a long period (such as when it is associated with a mental health disorder). Disorientation is usually more common in older people but can also occur in younger people. Disorientation can be reversible or not reversible, depending on its causes.

    Disorientation that is permanent is caused by physical changes in the structure of the brain and is called nonreversible dementia. Disorientation that is reversible can occur during hospitalization or in other unfamiliar surroundings or when clients are in pain, experience electrolyte imbalance, or are suffering from lack of sleep.

    Disorientation that occurs suddenly and usually without warning is called delirium, discussed later in this chapter. Delirium can be caused by an infection in the body (such as a urinary tract infection [UTI]), extreme lack of sleep, an electrolyte imbalance (from a bout of influenza, for example), certain drugs, a combination of prescription and over-the-counter (OTC) medications, or a toxic amount of these drugs. It is important that people who experience sudden disorientation without a known cause be seen by a physician. For example, sudden disorientation resulting from a stroke needs to be treated medically within hours, or it could result in permanent cognitive, mental, or physical disorders or even death.

    People with disorientation often exhibit behaviour changes and may be angry, restless, depressed, or irritable. Some disoriented people may behave aggressively. Other signs and symptoms of disorientation include the following:

    1. • Anxiety
    2. • Tremors
    3. • Hallucinations (page 917)
    4. • Delusions (page 918)
    5. • Decline in level of consciousness
    6. • Disorganized thinking and speech
    7. • Impaired attention span

    Causes of Disorientation

    1. • UTI (one of the main causes)
    2. • Alcohol intoxication
    3. • Low blood sugar
    4. • Head trauma or head injury
    5. • Concussion
    6. • Fluid and electrolyte imbalance
    7. • Nutritional deficiencies, particularly niacin, thiamine, vitamin C, or vitamin B12
    8. • Fever
    9. • Sudden drop in body temperature (hypothermia)
    10. • Low levels of oxygen (e.g., from chronic lung disorders)
    11. • Medications
    12. • Infections
    13. • Brain tumour
    14. • Certain physiological illnesses, especially in older persons (see Chapter 33)
    15. • Certain mental health illnesses such as clinical depression or schizophrenia (see Chapter 37)
    16. • Sleep deprivation (loss)

    Diagnosing Disorientation, Delirium, and Dementia

    People with ongoing disorientation that has come on gradually (over weeks, months, or years) need to see a doctor to have the concern evaluated. The doctor will perform a physical examination and ask questions to determine if the client has any of the following signs or symptoms of disorientation:

    1. • Getting days and nights mixed up
    2. • Being awake during usual sleep time
    3. • Not recognizing people
    4. • Not knowing where one is
    5. • Not knowing the date and time
    6. • Not answering simple questions appropriately
    7. • Having persistent feelings of disorientation or repeated episodes of disorientation
    8. • Having disorientation that is rapidly getting worse
    9. • Having disorientation that comes and goes

    In addition, the doctor will ask about other conditions that may trigger disorientation, such as the following:

    1. • Recent illness, such as the flu
    2. • Difficulty with urinating
    3. • Recent head injury
    4. • Diabetes
    5. • Lung disorder
    6. • Medication errors, such as taking medications in the wrong combination or not as directed
    7. • Exposure to other drugs or alcohol

    The physical examination will include a thorough evaluation of the nervous system, including the brain. The doctor may also perform neurological cognitive tests, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head, blood and urine tests, and an electroencephalogram (EEG), depending on the signs and symptoms.

    After the necessary tests, the doctor may be able to identify the cause of the disorientation and will determine if it is a symptom of a persistent illness such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), that of a permanent disorder (see Chapter 33, page 797, and the Supporting Mrs. Thomas, Part 1: Certain Illnesses Can Cause Dementia box), or that of an acute condition such as delirium.

    Ways to Minimize or Prevent Disorientation

    Although some types of disorientation cannot be prevented, other types can be prevented through good health practices and lifestyle choices such as the following:

    1. • Getting regular hours of sleep
    2. • Eating a balanced diet that has plenty of vitamins and minerals
    3. • Not drinking alcohol in excess
    4. • Keeping careful control of blood sugar if diabetes is present
    5. • Not smoking

    Delirium

    Disorientation that occurs suddenly is called delirium—a condition of severe disorientation and rapid changes in brain function. It is usually caused by a treatable physical or mental health disorder; it is often temporary and disappears once the illness has been treated. It can occur from a reaction to medications, infection or other illness, poor nutrition, food poisoning, dehydration, or emotional trauma. Major life changes—such as the death of a loved one or a move into a facility—can also cause delirium.

    Delirium should always be treated as an emergency. It often is the first or only sign of a physical disorder in older persons and in people with dementia. If you observe any sudden occurrence of signs and symptoms of delirium, inform your supervisor at once. The cause of the delirium needs to be determined, and the delirium needs to be treated immediately. In many cases (such as with stroke), the symptoms can become permanent or could progress and cause death.

    Signs and Symptoms of Delirium

    Delirium involves a rapid alternation between mental states. For example, someone with delirium may become drowsy, then agitated, and then drowsy again. Other signs and symptoms include the following:

    1. • Attention disturbance, such as an inability to maintain goal-directed, purposeful thinking or behaviour, or difficulty concentrating
    2. • Incoherent speech and inability to stop speech patterns or behaviours
    3. • Disorientation to time or place
    4. • Changes in sensation and perception (which increases the disorientation)
    5. • Sign of illusions or hallucinations
    6. • Altered level of consciousness or awareness
    7. • Altered sleep patterns; drowsiness
    8. • Levels of alertness may vary—the client is usually more alert in the morning and less alert at night (drowsiness)
    9. • Decrease in short-term memory and recall
    10. • Changes in motor activities, movement
    11. • Emotional or personality changes, such as anxiety, anger, apathy (appearance of indifference), depression, euphoria (exaggerated expression of well-being), or irritability

    Diagnosis and Treatment of Delirium

    The client with delirium should be seen as soon as possible by a physician, who will most likely order blood work (including blood and alcohol levels), EEG, computed tomography (CT) or MRI scanning of the client’s head, chest X-ray, or a lumbar puncture (spinal tap) to determine the cause of the delirium. The client is usually admitted to a hospital and treated as an inpatient. Acute disorders that cause delirium may coexist with chronic disorders that cause dementia.

    The goal of treatment is to control or reverse the cause of the symptoms, and treatment depends on the specific condition causing delirium. Sometimes, treatment requires stopping medications (or alcohol consumption). Disorders that contributed to disorientation—for example, heart failure, decreased oxygen, or psychiatric conditions (such as depression)—will be treated. Successful early treatment for coexisting medical and psychiatric disorders often greatly improves mental functioning.

    Some clients may require medications to control their aggressive, agitated behaviours or behaviours that are dangerous to themselves or to others. Medications are usually given in very low doses, with adjustments made as needed. For some clients, admission to a psychiatric facility may be necessary, especially if the dangerous behaviours continue. See the Supporting Mrs. Thomas, Part 2: Recognizing Delirium and Acting Quickly box.

In fact, most older people do not have dementia. While dementia can affect people in their 40s and 50s, it is more common after 65 years of age. Dementia affects a person’s ability to perform complex as well as simple tasks. Usually, dementia starts slowly and progresses gradually to a worse state.     Dementias that may be treated and sometimes reversed (usually referred to as disorientation) are caused by the following:

    2. • Brain injury. Some brain injuries result in internal bleeding in the skull, which can cause a rapid onset of dementia symptoms. Without prompt detention and surgery to correct the bleeding, the client would most likely die or experience permanent changes in cognitive function.
    3. • Brain tumour. Delirium or dementia may result from a brain tumour, depending on the location, size, and type of tumour. Surgery or chemotherapy may be used to treat the tumour.

    Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Dementias

    The most common form of dementia, Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD), accounts for over 64% of all dementias in Canadians.4 It is important to remember that while ADRD is the most common form of dementia, it cannot be assumed that people who are diagnosed with dementia will have ADRD. Other types of dementia may have different symptoms, medical treatments, and illness progression.

    ADRD are primary disorders of the brain; they are not caused by another illness or disease. Depending on the extent, ADRD can be categorized as either a minor or major neurocognitive disorder.5 Dr. Alois Alzheimer first identified Alzheimer’s disease in 1906. The two distinguishing characteristics he found were (1) plaques, deposits in the brain that become toxic to brain cells at excessive levels, and (2) tangles, which interfere with vital processes and eventually choke off living cells.6 The brain physically shrinks as these brain cells die, and ultimately how the brain functions is affected.6 What happens in ADRD is that the nerve cells in the brain become sick and progressively less and less able to pass on messages to other nerve cells, and eventually these cells die. It is the loss of connectivity (the ability of one cell to communicate with another), which occurs long before the cells die, that is responsible for the symptoms of Alzheimer’s disease.7

    ADRD usually occurs after age 65 years and is often diagnosed after age 80. However, it can also affect people in their 40s and 50s. Currently, ADRD has no cure, but there are some medications that can slow its progress. The cause of ADRD is unknown, but a family history of ADRD is a known risk factor.6 The progression (i.e., the rate of decline of cognitive functioning) of ADRD varies from client to client and can span from 3 to 20 years. On average, people live with the disease between 8 and 12 years. The progression can be described as a series of stages; these stages (see page 903) provide a guide to the pattern of the disease and help caregivers make care decisions.

    Warning Signs of Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Dementias

    The Alzheimer Society of Canada has developed the following list of signs to look for when Alzheimer’s disease is suspected. It is important for the person exhibiting these signs to be examined by a physician:8

      1. Memory loss affecting day-to-day abilities—forgetting things often or struggling to retain new information
      1. Difficulty performing familiar tasks—forgetting how to do something one has been doing one’s whole life, such as preparing a meal or getting dressed
      1. Difficulties with language—forgetting words or substituting words that do not fit the context
      1. Disorientation in time and space—not knowing what day of the week it is or getting lost in a familiar place
      1. Impaired judgement—not recognizing a medical condition that needs attention or wearing light clothing on a cold day
      1. Problems with abstract thinking—not understanding what numbers signify on a calculator, for example, or how they are used
      1. Misplacing things—putting things in strange places, like an iron in the freezer or a wristwatch in the sugar bowl
      1. Changes in mood and behaviour—for example, going from being easy-going to quick-tempered
      1. Changes in personality—behaving out of character, such as feeling paranoid or threatened
      1. Loss of initiative—losing interest in friends, family, and favourite activities

    According to the Alzheimer Society of Canada, knowing more about the person with dementia can help community support workers and staff in long-term care facilities build relationships and provide personalized care. Like all people, people who are diagnosed with dementia are whole people with their own values, preferences, and personal life stories. The Alzheimer Society publication, “I’m Living with Dementia,” is a resource that was designed for people with dementia to tell health care providers about themselves, including their needs, likes, dislikes, and interests.9

    Vascular Dementia

    Vascular dementia, sometimes known as multi-infarct dementia, is one of the most common forms of dementia, second only to Alzheimer’s disease in older persons. Vascular dementia is caused by chronic, reduced blood flow to the brain—usually as the result of a stroke or series of strokes. In many cases, the strokes are so small that symptoms may not be noticeable. These strokes are known as “silent strokes” or transient ischemic attacks (TIAs). Over time, the damage adds up, leading to memory loss, disorientation, and other signs of dementia. Research has shown that there is a real connection between heart and brain health and heart conditions such as dysrhythmia (problems with the heart’s rhythm) that has been shown to cause strokes.

    Vascular dementia results in a subtle, progressive decline in memory and cognitive functioning. It is caused when the blood supply carrying oxygen and nutrients to the brain is interrupted by a blocked or diseased vascular system. If blood supply is blocked for longer than a few seconds, brain cells can die, causing damage to the cortex of the brain—the area associated with learning, memory, and language.

    Depending on each client’s health condition and the severity of the stroke or strokes, vascular dementia may come on gradually or suddenly. Currently, there is no known cure, but the good news is that making certain lifestyle changes and using practical strategies may help prevent strokes, compensate for cognitive losses, and slow the disease’s development.

    Vascular dementia affects different people in different ways, and the speed of progression varies from person to person. Some symptoms may be similar to those of other types of dementia and usually reflect increasing difficulty to perform everyday activities such as eating, dressing, or shopping.

    Behavioural and physical symptoms can come on dramatically or very gradually, although it appears that a prolonged period of TIAs—the mini-strokes mentioned above—leads to a gradual decline in memory, whereas a bigger stroke can produce profound symptoms immediately. Regardless of the rate of appearance, vascular dementia typically progresses in a stepwise fashion, where lapses in memory and reasoning abilities are followed by periods of stability, only to give way to further decline.

    Common mental and emotional signs and symptoms of vascular dementia include the following:

    1. • Slowed thinking
    2. • Memory impairment; general forgetfulness
    3. • Unusual mood changes (e.g., depression, irritability)
    4. • Hallucinations and delusions
    5. • Disorientation, which may get worse at night
    6. • Personality changes and loss of social skills10

    Dementia With Lewy Bodies

    Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is one of the most common types of progressive dementia. The central feature of DLB is progressive cognitive decline, combined with three additional defining features: (1) severe fluctuations in alertness and attention—for example, sleep disturbances, frequent drowsiness, lethargy, lengthy periods spent staring into space, or disorganized speech; (2) recurrent visual hallucinations; and (3) Parkinson’s disease–like motor symptoms such as rigidity and the loss of spontaneous movement. People with DLB may also suffer from depression. The symptoms of DLB are caused by a buildup of Lewy bodies—accumulated bits of protein—inside the nuclei of neurons in areas of the brain that regulate particular aspects of memory and motor control.

    The similarity of symptoms between DLB and Parkinson’s disease and between DLB and Alzheimer’s disease can often make a definitive diagnosis difficult for the physician to make. As well, Lewy bodies are often found in the brains of people with Parkinson’s disease and in those with ADRD, a finding that suggests either that DLB is related to these other causes of dementia or that an individual can have both DLB and one of these other diseases at the same time. DLB usually occurs sporadically in people with no known family history of the disease. However, rare familial cases have occasionally been reported.

    Frontotemporal Degeneration Dementia

    Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is another type of dementia. It accounts for about 5% of all dementias and generally occurs between 40 and 60 years of age, with women slightly more affected than men.11 FTD is characterized by dramatic changes in personality, behaviour, and thought processes. Changes in personal and social conduct occur in the early stages of the disease and include loss of inhibition, apathy, social withdrawal, mouthing of objects, and ritualistic compulsive behaviours. These symptoms may lead to misdiagnosis as a psychological or emotion-based condition, or, in older persons, these symptoms can be mistaken for withdrawal or eccentricity.

    Many of the early symptoms of FTD are frontal lobe symptoms, which tend to mark out the differences between FTD and the other types of dementia such as ADRD. In ADRD, the initial symptoms tend to be memory impairment, but in FTD, since the frontal lobes of the brain are affected, the first symptoms occur in emotional and social functioning. The mood changes, often leaning toward euphoria, lack of inhibitions, and deterioration in social skills, are clearly noticeable.

    A client with FTD may become excessively extroverted, withdrawn, rude, impatient, or aggressive and may make inappropriate remarks in public. The person may exhibit altered sexual behaviour, with a markedly increased interest in sex, which can be linked to loss of inhibition; it can be very disturbing and can get the client into trouble with the police.11

    Pick’s disease is one type of FTD. Pick’s disease is marked by the presence of abnormalities in brain cells, called Pick’s bodies, which are found in the affected areas as well as elsewhere in the brain. Behavioural changes can be very subtle at first; dementia is recognized when behaviour becomes more bizarre. Because of the type of brain damage, the disease also causes gluttony (gross overeating) and increased alcohol intake. Clients with Pick’s disease may have a compulsion to put objects in their mouth.11

    Difficulties with language can occur in the early stages of FTD but are not as striking as those in the early stages of ADRD. Difficulty finding words and naming objects occurs initially, and as the disease progresses, memory and apraxia become more marked.11 Someone suffering from FTD will have difficulty maintaining a line of thought, be easily distracted, and have difficulty maintaining conversation for any length of time.11

    Mixed dementia, which has characteristics of both Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia, is now believed to be more common than previously thought.12 This new information is important because scientists now realize that the combination of two diseases may have a greater impact on the brain than only one disease. The symptoms may be similar to those of Alzheimer’s disease or vascular dementia or to both in combination.

    Secondary Dementias

    In many cases, a client’s dementia results from the physical effects of a disease process, from ingestion of damaging substances, or from injury. This type of dementia is called a secondary dementia. This section examines some types of secondary dementias.

    Parkinson’s Disease Dementia

    Parkinson’s disease is a progressive illness that affects the brain’s ability to control movement, resulting in tremors, stiffness, slowness, difficulty walking, and loss of balance. In the later stages of the disease, some people may also develop dementia. Common symptoms include memory loss; difficulty concentrating, thinking, or completing routine tasks; and difficulties navigating the environment. People with Parkinson’s disease may also develop depression (see Chapter 37), hallucinations, and delusions. See Chapter 33 for a more detailed description of Parkinson’s disease.

    Creutzfeldt–Jakob Disease

    First identified in the 1920s, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) is a very rare disease that causes dementia.13 CJD appears to have a genetic link or susceptibility in 5 to 15% of cases and can be passed on through infected brain tissue. It is believed to be caused by what are called proteinaceous prions, which can live in the body for years before any signs of the disease become obvious. CJD occurs in people between the ages of 50 and 70 years and is a rapid, progressive neurological disease that can affect both humans and animals.

    Early signs and symptoms of CJD include fatigue, difficulty sleeping and insomnia, excessive sleepiness, changes in personality, anorexia, progressive deterioration of memory, behaviour disturbances, and spasms of the body. Depression and psychosis are seen in 10% of cases, along with delusions and hallucinations.

    CJD is usually rapidly fatal. In later stages, the client will have poor balance and pain, which will cause increasing problems with stability and walking. Dementia becomes obvious, and it eventually leads to a mute, rigid state. Once the signs of CJD become apparent, the progress of the disease is very rapid. There is no known cure for CJD; symptoms are treated to help alleviate distress and pain and to make the client as comfortable as possible. About 90% of patients with CJD die within 1 year of the first symptoms.

    Normal-Pressure Hydrocephalus

    People who have had a history of brain hemorrhage or meningitis are at increased risk for this type of dementia. It is caused by poor reabsorption of cerebrospinal fluid and the resulting buildup of fluid in the brain. The symptoms include difficulty walking, memory loss, and inability to control urination. It can sometimes be corrected with surgical installation of a shunt in the brain to drain off the excess fluid.

    Substance-Induced Persisting Dementias

    A client whose dementia is a result of the persisting effects of an abused substance, medication, toxic substance exposure, or alcohol may be diagnosed with substance-induced persisting dementia. In this case, the client’s cognitive decline may be caused by any of the following:

    1. • Toxic effects of that substance on the brain cells
    2. • Substance-related effects on internal organs, such as liver, heart, and kidneys
    3. • Acquired brain injuries (ABI) related to falls sustained while impaired or disoriented

    It is important to note that any client with a substance-related persisting dementia should be treated respectfully at all times and no differently from any other client. Support workers need to remember the DIPPS principles and to always act professionally when providing care for these clients.

    Wernicke–Korsakoff Syndrome

    Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome (WKS) is a brain disorder caused by a lack of thiamine (vitamin B1). It affects the nervous system, including the brain, and is usually associated with alcohol use disorder (heavy alcohol consumption) over a long period. WKS causes impairment of memory and intellectual or cognitive skills such as problem solving or learning, as well as symptoms of nerve damage, including damage to areas of the brain. The most distinguishing symptom is confabulation (fabrication)—the making up of detailed, believable stories about experiences or situations to cover the gaps in the memory.14

    WKS may also occur in other conditions that involve severe malnutrition. Thiamine deficiency is often seen in people who consume excessive amounts of alcohol:

    1. • Many heavy drinkers have poor eating habits. Their nutrition is inadequate and does not contain essential vitamins.
    2. • Alcohol can inflame the stomach lining and impede the body’s ability to absorb the key vitamins it receives.

    Treatment involves thiamine replacement therapy by intravenous (IV) or intramuscular (IM) injection, together with proper nutrition and hydration. However, the amnesia and brain damage caused by the disease do not always respond to thiamine replacement therapy. In some cases, drug therapy is recommended. If treatment is successful, improvement will become apparent within 2 years, although recovery is slow and often incomplete.15

    Many other health conditions (see Chapter 33) can also cause secondary dementia, including the following:

    1. • Acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS)
    2. • Huntington’s disease
    3. • Multiple sclerosis (MS)
    4. • Syphilis

    Depression and Dementia16

    It is important to emphasize that the causes of major depression that many people live with (see Chapter 37) and dementia may be totally unrelated; and being depressed does not mean that the client will eventually develop dementia. Delirium and depression (see Chapter 37) in some clients can easily be mistaken for dementia, as they all share many of the same signs and symptoms. For this reason, depression is sometimes called pseudo-dementia, meaning false (pseudo) dementia. On the other hand, some people with early ADRD may seem depressed but not be. Their symptoms are so similar to depression that their behaviours might be mistaken as simple depression. Symptoms that are common to both depression and dementia include the following:

    1. • Lack of concern for surroundings
    2. • Loss of interest in activities and hobbies
    3. • Social withdrawal and isolation
    4. • Trouble concentrating
    5. • Impaired thinking

    However, experts estimate that up to 40% of people with ADRD suffer from major depressive disorder as well (see Chapter 37). Depression in an older person is always of great concern to health care providers, and an accurate diagnosis by a physician is very important. People who live with dementia are less likely to tell others of their sadness, hopelessness, guilt, or other feelings associated with depression. It may also be difficult to diagnose depression in clients with ADRD because their symptoms may differ from those of people without ADRD. Symptom differences may include the following:

    1. • Symptoms of depression may not last as long for people with ADRD as dementia symptoms increase.
    2. • Symptoms of depression may come and go in people with ADRD.
    3. • Symptoms of depression may be less severe in people with ADRD.
    4. • People with ADRD may be less likely to talk about or attempt suicide.

    Getting appropriate diagnosis and treatment for depression can significantly improve the quality of life of people with ADRD. Treatment includes a combination of medication, counselling, and encouraged socialization and activities.

    4. • Make a list of activities, people, or places that the client enjoys and schedule these events more frequently.

    Stages of Dementia

    One staging system explains three stages of dementia: (1) early, (2) middle, and (3) late. Most dementias are explained by means of these three stages, which generally apply to ADRD and most other forms of dementia. Another staging system, used by medical professionals, is called the Global Deterioration Scale (also called the Reisberg Scale), which divides the disease process into seven stages. More information about this scale is available on the Internet or from your local Alzheimer Society.

    Whichever staging system is used, it is important to remember that dementia affects each client differently . The client’s symptoms and behaviours will vary depending on which area of the brain is affected. In the early stages, different dementias can present quite differently but tend to become similar as they progress. Signs and symptoms become more severe with each stage, although the rate of decline will vary from client to client. Eventually, the client will be confined to bed and become totally dependent on others for care. Death occurs when the brain shuts down all body systems.17

    The order in which the symptoms appear, as well as the length of each stage, will vary from client to client. There is no clear line between the end of one stage and the beginning of another. In many clients, stages will overlap; some may experience many of the symptoms in each stage, and others may experience only a few. There may be fluctuations in a client from day to day, and the person may appear more disoriented one day and less so the next day.

    Stage 1: Mild (Early Stage)

    A client living with dementia is usually cared for at home until symptoms become severe. Clients living with dementia may live with family members or may live alone in their own homes and receive support from family, friends, volunteers, and home care workers. The primary caregiver may be the client’s spouse, adult child, another family member, or friend. Other caregivers may periodically provide relief to the primary caregiver. Respite care through community day programs and home care may also provide temporary help.

    Clients living with dementia often need help getting organized, solving simple problems, and remembering appointments, occasions, and medications (TABLE 38.1). They need support and supervision—for example, if a client wants to bake cookies, as their support worker you will need to make sure that the setting is safe for the activity and that the oven is turned off when the client is finished. Safety is an ongoing concern for the client living with dementia, since the person may wander away, swallow a poisonous substance, or leave an appliance on. Ensure a safe setting and supervise the client at all times. It is your responsibility to follow the client’s care plan.

    You may need to help a client start an activity or task and then provide only support and encouragement. Some clients living with dementia will, however, have difficulty completing tasks and will need frequent cues, reminders, and repeating of facts and conversations. For example, a client wants to mail a letter but cannot remember the word stamps or where they are kept. You can help this client remember the word and then help find the stamps. Follow the client’s care plan, which may specify that you should focus on the client’s enjoyment of an activity rather than on its achievement.

    A client in this stage will usually be aware of the diagnosis and will be able to participate in decisions affecting future care. Symptoms can include mild forgetfulness and communication difficulties, such as finding the right word and following a conversation. Some clients are able to stay involved in activities. Some others may become passive or withdrawn, or they may become depressed or anxious because they are frustrated by their changing abilities. It is important to monitor the emotional well-being of the client.

    Stage 2: Moderate (Middle Stage)

    Further decline occurs in the client’s mental and physical abilities in the middle stage (TABLE 38.2). Memory will continue to deteriorate as the client forgets personal history and no longer recognizes family and friends. Some clients become restless and pace constantly or may wander off. Registering the client with the MedicAlert Safely Home program will provide peace of mind to the client’s family, as the client can be easily located should the person become lost.

    A client may react in a number of ways to the loss of abilities. For example, the person may become less involved in activities or may repeat the same action or word over and over again. The local Alzheimer Society can provide education about the disease, resources, and support for clients and their families and can assist them to develop strategies to deal with difficult situations.

    As the client’s dementia progresses, the need for assistance increases. Usually, there comes a time when family and friends can no longer handle the situation or meet all the client’s needs. The client may be unable to tolerate wearing dentures or eyeglasses, which can increase the client’s risk for injury. Such issues should be detailed in the client’s care plan and noted by caregivers. Increased disorientation to time and place will also necessitate assistance with ADLs such as dressing, bathing, and using the toilet.

    Community day programs, respite care, home care, and help from family members, friends, and volunteers are no longer enough at this stage. Long-term care is needed when at least one of the following occurs:

    1. • Family members and friends are not able to meet the client’s needs.
    2. • Family members themselves have health challenges.
    3. • The client’s behaviours present a danger to self or others.
    4. • The client no longer recognizes the caregiver.

    Many long-term care facilities have special care units (SCUs) for clients living with dementia. The health care team ensures that their needs are met. Many facilities provide hospice care for persons in the final stages of dementia.

    When a client living with dementia moves to a facility, the family or close friends usually continue to be involved in the client’s care, as they are an important part of the health care team. Some family members and friends may take part in facility activities, and for many clients living with dementia, they can provide comfort and support. Often, the client may be admitted to a locked SCU that will provide the safety and security the client needs. This type of unit will reduce the client’s risk for injury if the client is prone to wandering.

    Safety

    Clients need a safe, quiet setting. They do not recognize safety hazards and are at risk for falls. If a client living with dementia falls, they may not understand why it is unsafe to move after the fall. Because you should never use force or hold a client down, you may need to allow the client to move about. Talk to the client in a quiet and soothing voice until someone can assist you.

    Clients may feel fearful even in a safe, quiet setting. They do not understand what is happening to them, so always explain calmly what you are going to do. Be prepared to repeat yourself since clients living with dementia often are not able to retain information. Clients may be at increased risk for injury if they are combative or aggressive. Family members or caregivers (who are tired and weary) may be more likely to restrain or confine clients in their care so as to be able to manage them.

    Ways to ensure client safety include the following:

    1. • Remove harmful, sharp, and breakable objects—such as knives, scissors, glass, dishes, razors, and tools—from the area.
    2. • Provide eating and drinking utensils that cannot be easily broken by the client, to help prevent cuts and injuries.
    3. • Place safety plugs in electrical outlets.
    4. • Keep cords and electrical equipment out of reach.
    5. • Remove electrical appliances—for example, hair dryers, curling or flat irons, makeup mirrors, and electric shavers—from the bathroom.
    6. • Store personal care items—shampoo, deodorant, lotion, and so on—in a safe place.
    7. • Use baby locks on doors to cabinets that contain medications or cleaning products.
    8. • Store household cleaners and medications in locked storage areas.
    9. • Store dangerous equipment and tools in a safe place.
    10. • Supervise the client when the client smokes.
    11. • Store cigarettes, cigars, pipes, matches, and other smoking materials in a safe place.
    12. • Practise safety measures to prevent falls, burns, choking, poisoning, and fires (see Chapter 20).
    13. • Keep all doors to kitchens, utility rooms, and housekeeping closets locked.

    Hygiene, Grooming, and Dressing

    Clients with moderate or severe dementia do not understand the need for personal hygiene and aseptic practices. They have to rely on the health care team to ensure good skin care (to prevent skin breakdown) and to help prevent the spread of infection. You need to assist them with handwashing at the following times:

    1. • After they have urinated or defecated
    2. • After they have coughed, sneezed, or blown their nose
    3. • Before or after they have handled or eaten food
    4. • Any time their hands become soiled

    During personal care, look for signs or symptoms of conditions that may cause pain (e.g., skin lesions, rough pieces of clothing, or dental issues). It is important to know that these clients may resist your efforts to keep them clean and dry, as they do not understand what is happening or why. They may fear harm or danger when you get too close or touch them, so they may become agitated and combative (hitting or kicking), shout and cry out for help, or pull away during care. Sudden movements can cause skin tears. Refer to the section Managing Responsive Behaviours, on page 914.

    Elimination Needs

    Clients with moderate and severe dementia may urinate in the wrong places—for example, garbage cans, planters, and heating vents. Some may remove incontinence products and throw them on the floor or in the toilet. Some may soil themselves, the furniture, or the walls. Offer to assist with elimination needs frequently at regular times during both day and night. Provide perineal care after urinary and fecal elimination. Ask your supervisor for help if a client resists you. Never restrict fluid intake in an effort to control elimination.

    Nutrition

    Clients living with dementia may become distracted during meals, or it may be hard for them to sit long enough to eat a meal. Some with later stage dementia forget how to use eating utensils, so finger foods can be offered to them instead. Some clients have difficulty swallowing (see Chapter 27), and some have to be fed. Some clients will resist your efforts to assist them with eating, and disoriented clients may throw or spit food. A quiet and calm environment in the dining area and special mealtimes are often helpful. Clients may be able to eat small amounts more frequently, rather than only three times a day.

    Fluids

    Clients with moderate and severe dementia are at risk for dehydration, as they do not recognize thirst. Encourage them to drink fluids regularly. Keep water and other fluids nearby and offer fluids whenever you are around them. Remember that adequate fluids can reduce the risk for serious health conditions such as UTI or constipation.

    Exercise

    Inactivity and immobility are risk factors for pneumonia and pressure injuries. Exercise is therefore important for clients living with dementia, but they may resist it because they do not understand what is happening and may fear harm. They may become agitated and combative or cry out for help. Do not force a client to exercise or take part in activities. Stay calm. Ask your supervisor for help if needed. Follow the client’s care plan.

    Health Issues

    Many clients living with dementia have other health issues or injuries. However, they may not notice or recognize pain, fever, constipation, incontinence, or other signs and symptoms. Changes in usual behaviour may signal pain, discomfort, or delirium, which should be medically investigated immediately (see page 895). A client who normally moans and groans all the time may become quiet and withdrawn; a client who is usually friendly and outgoing may become agitated and aggressive; a client who is nonverbal and quiet may become restless and cry easily. Loss of appetite may also signal pain. Report any changes in a client’s usual behaviour to your supervisor.

    The client must be kept free from abuse and neglect. Caring for clients living with dementia is often very frustrating because some behaviours can be hard to deal with. Family caregivers and health care providers can become frustrated and short-tempered. It is important to protect clients living with dementia from abuse. Report any signs of abuse to your supervisor at once. If you are becoming frustrated with a client, talk to your supervisor. An assignment change may be needed.

    Comfort

    Comfort is important in the care of a client living with dementia, so make sure that the client is physically comfortable. A quiet environment, talking in a calm voice, massage, soothing touch, music, and aromatherapy are all comforting and relaxing. Make sure that the client’s clothing is also comfortable.

    Sleep

    Dementia interferes with a person’s ability to make decisions and to remember, so as a support worker, it is your responsibility to meet a disoriented client’s physical and safety needs. Fatigue and visual changes, which are common with some dementias (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease), can be stressful for the client. Ways to enhance the client’s ability to fall asleep include the following:

    1. • Follow bedtime rituals.
    2. • Use night lights, which will help prevent accidents and disorientation.
    3. • Limit caffeine intake during the day.
    4. • Discourage naps during the day.
    5. • Encourage exercise during the day.
    6. • Reduce the level of noise in their surroundings.

    Socialization Therapy and Activities

    Clients living with dementia need to feel worthy and useful and to remain active to promote their emotional and physical health. Clients should be encouraged to do what they can and what they enjoy. For example, a client who used to be a good dancer can take part in dancing activities. A client who likes to draw can be encouraged to join a painting group. The social contact may be as important as the activity itself.

    Adult day programs and long-term care facilities provide therapy and activities—such as music programs, art programs, fitness programs, and support groups—to meet the client’s needs and cognitive abilities. Therapists may work with one client, a small group, or a large group.

    In adult community settings, support workers often work with clients in the early stage of dementia. Many of these clients have care requirements but may be able to make some decisions for themselves. Your support enables these clients to remain in their homes.

    Providing Compassionate Care

    The Client Living With Dementia

    1. • Dignity. Clients living with dementia do not choose to be forgetful, incontinent, agitated, angry, or rude. Nor do they choose to display other behaviours, signs, and symptoms of the disease. They cannot control what is happening to them; it is the disease that causes the behaviours. The dementia is responsible, not the client.
    2. • Treat all clients living with dementia with dignity and respect. They have rights under human rights codes and long-term care legislation. In the advanced stages of dementia, they may not know or be able to exercise their rights, but they are still entitled to them. Be patient and calm when caring for clients living with dementia. Always explain what you are going to do. Do not assume that the client cannot understand you.
    3. • Take care never to make the client feel foolish about forgetting or misplacing something. Even in the later stages of dementia, clients can still feel embarrassment and shame. Protect the client’s dignity when you are providing care. Only those involved in the client’s care should be present for care procedures.
    4. • Independence. The client has the right to autonomy. Clients living with dementia should be encouraged to be independent for as long as safely possible. Encourage clients to do what they can for themselves.
    5. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan. With Alzheimer’s disease and most other dementias, clients continue to experience all forms of emotions well into the final stages. When a client can no longer use language, being able to recognize the client’s emotional state can help you determine their wishes, preferences, fears, likes, and dislikes.
    6. • Preferences. Personal choice is important. Clients with early stage dementia can voice their preferences. Encourage clients with moderate stage dementia to make simple choices—for example, choosing one of two sweaters or watching or not watching TV. The family or substitute decision maker usually makes choices regarding bath times, menus, clothing, activities, and other care if the client is not able to.
    7. • A client may choose to use a special pillow or blanket. The client may insist on wearing a particular sweater or cardigan because the item may have special meaning, or it may provide comfort and security. Eventually, the client may not know why the item is important or may not even recognize it. However, do not remove the item, as it is still important to the person.
    8. • Privacy. The client has the right to privacy and confidentiality. Provide space and privacy for the client to visit with others. Protect confidentiality. Do not share information about the client with others. Do not relate stories about the client’s behaviour to others.
    9. • Safety. A safe, quiet, and calm setting promotes quality of life. Follow the safety measures in Chapter 20. All clients have the right to be free from restraints, which require a physician’s order in any case and are used only if it is the best way to protect the client, not for the convenience of the health care team. Restraints can worsen disoriented and agitated behaviours. Your supervisor will tell you when to use restraints.

    Managing Responsive Behaviours

    Clients living with dementia often display responsive behaviours, many of which may be disruptive to other clients or residents around them and may even challenge the abilities of the caregivers to provide compassionate care.

    Responsive behaviours usually originate as a response to an illness, infection, or physical discomfort. They are, therefore, often referred to as responsive behaviours.19 It is important for you to remember that all behaviour has meaning. Clients who demonstrate behaviours that challenge caregivers are merely responding to something in their internal or external environment. It is the responsibility of all caregivers to try to understand these behaviours and to try to remedy the situation, following the DIPPS principles (dignity, independence, individualized care, privacy, preferences, and safety).

    It is important to know that clients’ responsive behaviours result from their dementia. Clients living with dementia cannot control their actions, so you should never take their behaviours personally, become upset or angry with these clients, or blame yourself for their behaviours.

    Clients living with dementia may be unable to understand that they are cold or hungry because their brain may (1) no longer be able to sort out the vast number of messages received, (2) no longer send out the correct message to other parts of the brain, or (3) not be able to understand the meaning behind certain actions. As a result, the client living with dementia may act out, become very resistant to staff, or even become very vocal or physically combative. Some clients living with dementia may become aggressive toward other clients or toward the caregiving staff and may even spit, bite, or punch others. If a client is demonstrating aggressive behaviours, you need to ensure your own safety and that of others. You should also report unusual or increased instances of responsive behaviours. Your observations are very important in determining the causes of the person’s behaviour (see the Think About Safety: The ABCDs of Managing Responsive Behaviours box on page 920).

    As a support worker, you must learn how to safely and respectfully care for clients who can no longer communicate in the manner that they once did. You need to try to determine what the client is trying to tell you. At all times, you should focus on the client’s right to dignity, independence, individualized care, privacy, preferences, and safety.

    Validation Therapy

    Validation therapy is a type of therapy for people with dementia that focuses on empathy and advocates accepting the affected person’s perception of reality. Developed by Naomi Feil and practised throughout the world, validation therapy aims to make the client feel supported and respected. Rather than trying to bring the client living with dementia back to our reality, this therapy suggests it is less stressful for the client if caregivers simply refrain from challenging, arguing, or disagreeing with the client.20

    For instance, if a person with dementia believes that she is waiting for her children, all now middle-aged, to return from school, family members and caregivers who use validation therapy would not argue the point or expect the person with dementia to have insight into her behaviour. They would not correct her beliefs but would simply allow the client to discuss her perception of reality.20 The caregiver can focus on descriptive clues the client provides, prompting her to describe what she sees, feels, hears, smells, or touches at that moment.20 See BOX 38.3 and the Supporting Mrs. Yi: Using Validation Therapy box.

    Preventing Wandering

    Clients living with dementia are not oriented to person, time, and place, so they may wander away and not be able to find their way back. They may wander off very quickly by foot, car, bicycle, or other means. They may be with you one moment and gone the next.

    Judgement in these clients is poor. They cannot tell what is safe and what is dangerous, so they are at high risk for life-threatening accidents. They can walk into traffic or into a nearby lake, river, or forest. If not properly dressed, they are at risk for heat or cold exposure.

    Secured Units

    Some long-term care residents tend to wander throughout the facility or try to leave the building, which may put them at risk for injury. These residents may be moved to a secured unit—an area in the facility where entrances and exits are locked—which provides a safe setting for residents to move about but not wander away.

    Secured units are considered a form of environmental restraint, so the facility must take care to use the least restrictive approach. A dementia diagnosis and a physician’s order are needed to place a resident in a secured unit, and the health care team should regularly review the client’s need to be in a secured unit, always taking care to protect the resident’s rights.

    As the dementia progresses to the advanced stage, a secured unit is no longer needed, as the client cannot sit or walk and is bedridden. Since wandering is not a risk anymore, the client is usually transferred to another unit.

    Standards of care for SCUs have been established by legislation. Staff must have special training in the care of people with dementia. The unit must have programs that promote clients’ dignity, personal freedom, and safety.

    Sundowning

    Sundowning is a condition in which the signs, symptoms, and behaviours of dementia increase during the hours of darkness—in the late afternoon and evening hours. As daylight ends and darkness sets in, disorientation, restlessness, anxiety, agitation, and other symptoms increase. Behaviour worsens after the sun goes down and may deteriorate throughout the night. The client may be afraid of the dark or may see things that are not actually present, because of poor lighting or shadows.

    If the client tells you, for example, that there is a “strange person” in the room, you should never ignore the client’s statement. Turn the light on, and reassure the client. Remove things that may be casting a shadow or disturbing the client, and provide the client with verbal reassurance. See the Supporting Reverend Welikij: How Sundowning Can Upset Families box, on page 919. Ways to reduce the likelihood of client injury or agitation due to sundowning include the following:

    1. • Complete treatments early in the day if doing so will reduce the client’s agitation.
    2. • Provide a calm, quiet setting late in the day. Playing soft music may help.
    3. • Ensure that the client is clean and dry and is in no danger of falling from the bed or chair.
    4. • Do not restrain the client.
    5. • Encourage exercise and activity early in the day.
    6. • Hunger can increase restlessness. The client who is experiencing sundowning may be tired or hungry, may have to go to the bathroom, or may not recognize the place where they are.
    7. • Promote regular elimination. The need to eliminate can increase restlessness.
    8. • Do not try to reason with the client, as communication is impaired, and the person cannot think or speak clearly.
    9. • Shadows can be disturbing for some clients. Keep the environment well lit.

    Hallucinations and Delusions

    A hallucination is seeing, hearing, or feeling something that is not real. Senses are dulled in affected people, who see animals, insects, or people who are not really present. Some may hear voices, feel bugs crawling on them, or feel that they are being touched by someone.

    FIGURE 38.5 Use touch to calm the client living with dementia. Note how this support worker is safely positioned to the side of the client, within the client’s line of sight.

    Delusions are false beliefs. A client living with dementia may believe that a doll is a baby or that a spouse has been unfaithful. Some may believe that they are being held captive, killed, or attacked. A client may believe that the caregiver is someone else. Many other false beliefs that can cause intense fear or other emotions in the client can occur.

    Ways to diminish the impact of hallucinations or delusions on the client living with dementia include the following:

    1. • Ensure that the client wears eyeglasses and hearing aids, as prescribed. The problem can be made worse by impaired vision or hearing. Follow the client’s care plan.
    2. • Do not argue with the client.
    3. • Reassure the client. Say that you will ensure protection from harm.
    4. • Distract the client with some item or activity. Taking the client for a walk may be helpful.
    5. • Use touch, if appropriate, to calm and reassure the client (FIGURE 38.5).
    6. • Eliminate noises that the client could misinterpret and that could be upsetting—for example, noises from the TV, radio, stereo, furnaces, air conditioners, appliances, and other sources.
    7. • Check the lighting. Make sure there are no glares, shadows, or reflections.
    8. • Cover or remove mirrors. The client could mistake the reflection for another person and become frightened.

    Catastrophic Reactions

    Catastrophic reactions are extreme responses to what the client perceives as extreme danger, disaster, or tragedy. The client may scream, cry, or be agitated or combative. These reactions are commonly caused by too much stimulation that can overwhelm the client—for example, being in a noisy dining room, hearing radio or TV noises, and being asked questions all at the same time.

    Sometimes, clients have extreme reactions whenever anything out of the ordinary or unexpected happens. For example, a flickering light may be perceived as a fire, resulting in the client running away screaming. The client may even be frightened if someone approaches too quickly or stands too close.

    Clients living with dementia should never be “sneaked up on.” Approach them from the side, in full view of them. Calmly address the client to gain attention, taking care not to startle the client.

    Agitation and Restlessness

    The client may fidget, pace, hit, or yell and may resist care. Common causes of such behaviours are pain or discomfort, anxiety, lack of sleep, and too much or too little stimulation, as well as hunger, thirst, and the need to eliminate. A calm, quiet setting and the meeting of basic needs can help calm the client.

    It is important not to overstimulate the client. Use a calm, gentle voice when talking with them. Do not overwhelm the client with instructions or choices. Do not rush the client or be impatient with them.

    Aggression and Combativeness

    Aggressive or combative behaviours include hitting, pinching, grabbing, biting, or swearing. They may result from agitation and restlessness, pain, fatigue, too much stimulation, response to caregiver stress, and a feeling of being lost or abandoned. The behaviours often occur during care procedures that upset or frighten the client (e.g., bathing, dressing). See Chapter 8 for ways to deal with an angry client. It is your responsibility to always (1) follow the client’s care plan regarding these procedures, (2) respect the DIPPS principles, and (3) ensure your safety and the safety of others. See the Think About Safety: The ABCDs of Managing Responsive Behaviours box on page 920.

    Screaming

    It is common for clients in the later stages of dementia who are very disoriented and have poor communication skills to scream to communicate. Clients may scream a word or a name or just make screaming sounds.

    Possible reasons for screaming include hearing and vision impairment, pain or discomfort, fear, and fatigue. Clients may have been overstimulated or understimulated. A disoriented client may react to a caregiver or family member by screaming.

    Sometimes, the following measures are helpful:

    1. • Providing a calm, quiet setting
    2. • Playing soft music
    3. • Having the client wear a hearing aid or eyeglasses, as prescribed
    4. • Having a family member or favourite caregiver comfort and calm the client
    5. • Using therapeutic touch (a noninvasive method of healing in which the caregiver places hands on the client in a gentle and nonthreatening manner) to calm the client

    Sexual Behaviours

    Sexual behaviours can be labelled “abnormal” on the basis of how and when they occur. Clients living with dementia are not oriented to person, place, or time. Sometimes, clients living with dementia are not able to control their behaviours. For example, some clients may touch and fondle their genitals for pleasure or masturbate in public. Masturbation in public is viewed as sexually aggressive behaviour, but some clients may not understand that their behaviour is socially offensive. Some clients living with dementia may mistake someone for a sexual partner and may try to kiss, hug, or touch that person.

    Some abnormal behaviours are not sexual in nature. Touching, scratching, and rubbing the genitals can signal infection, pain, or discomfort in the genitourinary system or some other underlying health issue that can cause soreness and itching (see the Supporting Reverend Welikij: How Sundowning Can Upset Families box). Other causes can be poor hygiene or being wet or soiled by urine or stool. Clean the client quickly and thoroughly after elimination to ensure that the client is not wet or soiled.

    Sexually aggressive behaviours have many causes, including the following:

    1. • Disorientation
    2. • Nervous system disorders
    3. • Adverse effects from medications
    4. • Fever
    5. • Dementia
    6. • Acquired brain injury

    Report to your supervisor incidences of a client repeatedly touching the genitals. Try to determine the cause of the client’s behaviour. Provide for privacy and safety. Tell your supervisor about the situation. A health care provider may assess the client for health issues or may encourage the client’s sexual partner to show affection. Remember that normal practices such as hand holding, hugging, kissing, and touching are to be encouraged and may reduce the client’s inappropriate behaviour. If you observe a client masturbating in public, lead the client to their room, and provide for privacy and safety.

    Reverend Welikij, 72, a retired clergyman, lived at home with his wife until several months ago. He was admitted to an extended care facility because of his dementia. Reverend Welikij was usually a quiet, soft-spoken man, but the staff at the facility had begun to notice that he was becoming increasingly agitated in the evenings. One night, he took his pants down and started pulling on his penis while his wife and daughter were visiting him in the lounge area. They were horrified and embarrassed and could not understand why he was behaving in this way.

    The support staff took him to his room to provide him with privacy, assisted him to the bathroom, and then to bed. They then reported this behaviour to the nurse in charge, who conveyed the information to his doctor. It was later found that Reverend Welikij had a UTI, and because he was unable to state that something was wrong, he acted out to show his discomfort.

    His wife and daughter were very relieved when this information was relayed to them. They decided to visit him earlier in the evenings when he was “more like himself.” This helped make Mrs. Welikij’s visits with her husband less distressing.

    Repetitive Behaviours

    Repetitive means repeated over and over again. Clients living with dementia sometimes repeat the same actions or verbal expressions over and over. For example, a client may fold a napkin, may say the same words, or ask the same question again and again. Such behaviours are not hurtful to the client but can be annoying to caregivers and family members.

    Harmless acts should be permitted, or the client could be distracted from the act with music, picture books, exercise, movies, or a walk.

    Hoarding

    Hoarding means collecting things and putting them away in a guarded manner. Clients who hoard items may hide them in drawers or under the bed. Some clients will hoard any and all items that they can find, and some will seek out specific items and hoard them. When a client is hoarding, you should try to find out from the family why these items are significant to the client.

    Caregiver Needs

    Being a primary caregiver to a person with dementia can be extremely stressful physically, emotionally, socially, and financially. Many adult children in the “sandwich generation” have their energies divided between taking care of both their children and their parents, all of whom need attention. They not only care for two families but also often have full-time jobs outside their home, which can add to their stress.

    Caregivers can, therefore, suffer from anger, anxiety, sleeplessness, and depression. Some find it difficult to concentrate on anything and are always irritable. Abuse may occur in very stressful caregiving situations.

    Because they are constantly under stress, caregivers are susceptible to health issues themselves, so they need to take care of their own health by ensuring a healthy diet, exercise, and plenty of rest. Caregivers need to ask family and friends for help when their responsibilities become overwhelming.

    Caregivers need much support and encouragement. Many of them join support groups sponsored by hospitals, long-term care facilities, and the Alzheimer Society of Canada, which has chapters in cities and towns across the country. People in similar situations voice their feelings, anger, frustration, guilt, and other emotions and share coping and caregiving strategies.

    Caregivers, family members, and friends of a person with dementia often feel helpless. No matter what they do, the person only gets worse. Much time, money, energy, and emotion are needed to care for the person. Guilty feelings are common in these caregivers as well. Family and friends know that the person has not chosen to develop dementia or to live with its signs, symptoms, and behaviours. Nevertheless, sometimes behaviours are frustrating, embarrassing, or threatening. Family and friends may be upset and angry that their loved one can no longer show love or affection.

    As a support worker, you play a key role in caregiver relief. You may assist the primary caregiver or other caregivers, or you may care for the client to give the caregiver some time off from their responsibilities. Follow the care plan, and perform your tasks competently. The caregiver should have confidence in your skill and ability in order to relax and let go of control for some time. The care plan may ask you to encourage the caregiver to take care of personal needs and to leave the home to have a break from caregiving while you are there. You may observe signs and symptoms of caregiver stress and signs of depression and abuse. Report these observations immediately to your supervisor (see the Case Study: Supporting Caregivers of Clients Living With Dementia box).

    Case Study

    Supporting Caregivers of Clients Living With Dementia

    Mrs. Munroe, 70, has Alzheimer’s disease. She has stage 2 (moderate) dementia. She lives at home with her husband, who is her primary caregiver. Leila is assigned to provide morning respite care. This is her fourth visit. Mrs. Munroe is incontinent; she also needs assistance with feeding, bathing, and dressing. She is frequently rude and aggressive and becomes angry if someone stands too near her or tries to make her do something. Quick, sudden movements and loud noises also upset her. The care plan calls for Mr. Munroe to take a break—preferably away from the house—while Leila takes care of Mrs. Munroe’s needs. Mr. Munroe is reluctant to leave, since he is anxious about his wife.

    Discussion

    Leila’s visit is scheduled for 3 hours. She knows that Mr. Munroe will not leave until he is satisfied that his wife is in competent hands. Leila starts her visit by sitting down with Mrs. Munroe at the kitchen table and talking quietly and gently. She explains that she is going to make breakfast and gives Mrs. Munroe two food choices. Leila makes breakfast while she continues talking with Mrs. Munroe in a quiet, reassuring voice. Mr. Munroe is anxiously watching everything from the living room. When Mrs. Munroe is settled, Leila suggests to Mr. Munroe that he go out for a coffee, and he reluctantly agrees to go. When he comes back 1 hour later, Mrs. Munroe has been bathed and dressed, and she is sitting quietly watching TV. Mr. Munroe feels better for having had a break, and now he feels confident in Leila’s ability to care for his wife.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • Disorientation, delirium, and dementia are not a normal part of aging.

    • Diseases that affect the brain can affect cognitive functioning, which involves the following functions of the brain: (1) memory, (2) thinking, (3) reasoning, (4) ability to understand, (5) judgement, and (6) behaviour.

    • Disorientation is the inability of a person to think with the usual clarity. There are numerous causes of disorientation. Disorientation may be temporary or permanent. Permanent disorientation is caused by physical changes to the structure of the brain.

    • Delirium is disorientation that occurs suddenly. It is usually caused by treatable physical or mental health disorders. Delirium should always be treated as an emergency requiring medical attention.

    • Dementia is a general term that describes the progressive loss of functions, including cognitive and social functions. Dementia usually gets worse over time.

    • Early warning signs of dementia include the following: (1) memory loss that affects daily functioning; (2) disorientation; (3) repeating words or sentences; having problems finding words or following conversations; (4) using poor judgement; (5) having difficulty completing common tasks; (6) changes in mood, behaviour, or personality; and (7) loss of interest in hobbies or activities.

    • All caregivers should be aware of ways to support clients with dementia. These clients face the following challenges: (1) environmental challenges, (2) communication challenges, (3) wandering, (4) sundowning, (5) hallucinations or delusions, (6) sleep disturbances, and (7) impaired ability to meet basic needs.

    • Dementia usually progresses in three stages: (1) early, (2) moderate or middle, and (3) late. As a support worker, you need to be aware of the typical symptoms of each stage and the care that you must provide accordingly.

    • The ABCDs of managing responsive behaviours are (A) activating event, (B) behaviour, (C) consequences, and (D) deciding how to support clients, decreasing their responsive behaviours, and debriefing others.

    • When a client displays responsive behaviours, remember the DIPPS principles and ensure your safety and the safety of others.

    Oxygen (O2) is a gas that is part of the air we breathe. At room temperature, it has no taste, odour, or colour. Oxygen is the most important basic need for life, and without it, death will occur within minutes. Oxygen enters the body as part of the air that we breathe and enters our body through an airway. This is the pathway that air takes as it enters the body and goes into the lungs. An airway can be our nose, mouth, or through a breathing tube or tracheostomy.

    FIGURE 39.1 The respiratory system.

    Our respiratory system (FIGURE 39.1) is something that we take for granted until we experience difficulty breathing. People who have certain illnesses (see Chapters 19 and 33) or are in stressful situations might experience difficulty breathing, which can affect the amount of oxygen in their blood cells. See Chapter 32 for a description of the respiratory system and Chapter 33 for a description of common conditions related to the respiratory system.

    As a support worker, you will often work with clients who have difficulty breathing or absorbing enough oxygen into their bodies. To survive, these clients require supplemental oxygen, which is supplied to them through a nasal tube or face mask. It is your responsibility to know how to provide safe and effective care to these clients.

    Oxygen therapy is usually defined as the administration of oxygen to a client at concentrations greater than those found in room air. Oxygen is considered to be a medication in that there are very strict rules to follow when providing oxygen to a client, and only qualified health care providers can administer it. Only a physician or nurse practitioner can prescribe oxygen therapy to a client. The amount of oxygen the client is to receive, the type of device to use, and the times for therapy must also be prescribed. It is usually the role of the respiratory therapist (or nurse at some agencies) to set up the oxygen device and take the leadership role in providing respiratory therapies. A respiratory therapist is a regulated professional who evaluates, treats, and maintains heart and lung function and helps the client to keep breathing.

    Some clients may need oxygen constantly, whereas others may need it for relief of symptoms such as chest pain (in those with heart disease) or shortness of breath (in clients with respiratory diseases who may have enough oxygen while at rest but become short of breath with mild activity). It is important that the client receive only the amount of oxygen that is ordered; receiving too much oxygen can slow the client’s respiratory rate to a dangerous level.

    Factors Affecting Oxygen Needs

    For cells to get enough oxygen, the respiratory and cardiovascular systems must function properly. Any disease, injury, or surgery involving these systems can affect the body’s ability to absorb oxygen from the air and deliver it to the cells. Body systems depend on each other, so altered function of any system (e.g., nervous, musculoskeletal, urinary system) can affect oxygen absorption and delivery. Major factors affecting oxygen absorption and delivery are discussed in BOX 39.1.

    BOX 39.1 Factors Affecting Oxygen Needs

    • • Respiratory system function. The airway must be open (patent), and there must be an adequate number of alveoli (single-celled air sacs in the lungs) to effectively absorb oxygen and excrete carbon dioxide (CO2), which is a waste product.
    • • Cardiovascular system function. Oxygen is required by all cells to produce energy to carry out their proper functions, and blood must flow normally to and from the heart to all bodily cells. When blood vessels are narrowed by disease or other causes, blood flow will be constricted. Capillaries and cells must exchange O2 and CO2 efficiently.
    • • Red blood cell count. Blood must have enough red blood cells (RBCs), which are produced in bone marrow and contain hemoglobin. Hemoglobin contains iron, and it picks up oxygen in the lungs and carries it to the cells. Health challenges that reduce RBCs in the blood include chemotherapy, leukemia, iron-deficiency anemia, and blood loss.
    • • Nervous system function. Nervous system diseases and injuries can affect respiratory muscles, making breathing difficult or impossible. Brain injury as well as use of narcotics and depressant drugs can affect the respiratory rate, rhythm, and depth.
    • • Aging. Respiratory muscles weaken and lung tissue becomes less elastic with advancing age, making coughing more difficult. If the person cannot cough to remove secretions from the upper airway, pneumonia (infection of the lungs) can develop. For this reason, older people are at risk for respiratory complications after surgery.
    • • Exercise. The body requires more O2 when it is exercised, to supply energy needed by cells. The O2 demand throughout the body is usually met by increasing the respiratory rate and depth (in the lungs).
    • Clients with heart and respiratory disease may have enough O2 while they are at rest, but with even a slight increase in activity, their bodies may not be able to bring in enough O2 and deliver it to the cells for energy. This lack of O2 stresses the heart and lungs, which (like all tissues) require oxygen to perform their necessary tasks.
    • • Fever. When fever is present, O2 needs increase because the body is working hard to fight the infection that is causing the fever; the metabolic rate also increases. To keep up with the body’s O2 needs, the respiratory rate and depth must increase.
    • • Pain. Pain increases the need for O2, so the body responds by increasing the respiration rate and depth. Clients with chest and abdominal injuries or surgeries are unwilling or unable to take deep breaths because of the pain caused by breathing in and out, which further increases their need for O2. The extra O2 they need is, therefore, supplied to them by way of a mask or tube until they can take deep breaths on their own.
    • • Drug (narcotic or barbiturate) overdose. Normally, when used as prescribed, the proper amount of a drug will control moderate to severe pain (narcotics) or provide sedation (barbiturates). When a narcotic, such as morphine or meperidine (Demerol), or a barbiturate is taken for “recreational” purposes, mixed with alcohol or other drugs that have not been prescribed, or taken beyond the safe dosage, the breathing centre in the hypothalamus of the brain is affected. The brain cannot tell the person to take deep breaths, so respirations are suppressed and become dangerously slowed or even stopped, possibly resulting in a loss of consciousness or death. Substance abusers are at risk for respiratory depression (breaths not deep enough to bring enough air into the lungs) and respiratory arrest (stoppage of breathing) from overdoses of these drugs.
    • • Smoking. Individuals who smoke inhale air that is mixed with harmful substances that can damage the lung tissue and have been proven to cause lung cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Smoking is also a risk factor for coronary artery disease.
    • • Allergies. An allergy is a sensitivity to a substance—for example, pollen, dust, foods, drugs, or smoke—that causes the body to react with signs and symptoms such as a runny nose, wheezing, or congestion. As part of the allergic reaction, the upper airway mucous membranes may swell. With severe swelling, the airway can close, resulting in shock and death. More commonly, however, people with allergies are at risk for chronic bronchitis and asthma.
    • • Pollutant exposure. A pollutant is a harmful chemical or substance in air or water. Air pollutants—for example, dust, fumes, toxins, asbestos, coal dust, and sawdust—damage the lungs. Exposure to pollutants can occur at home, in the workplace, or in the community.
    • • Nutrition. Good nutrition that includes iron and vitamins (vitamin B12, vitamin C, and folic acid) is necessary to produce RBCs, without which the body cannot transport O2 to cells.
    • • Alcohol. Alcohol depresses the brain. Excessive amounts reduce the cough reflex and increase the risk for aspiration, which can cause obstructed airway and pneumonia.

    Altered Respiratory Function

    The function of the respiratory system involves three processes that must work together. Respiratory function is altered if even one of these processes is affected:

      1. Air moves into and out of the lungs.
      1. O2 and CO2 are exchanged at the alveoli.
      1. The blood transports O2 to the cells and removes CO2 from them.

    The client who is short of breath (SOB) or who is short of breath on exertion (SOBOE) has altered respiratory function—that is, something is preventing the person from breathing easily. Altered respiratory function may be an acute or chronic condition. When unable to breathe easily, a client may panic or feel anxious. The client becomes uncomfortable, is usually visibly struggling to breathe, and often wants to sit up in the bed or in a chair since it may be more difficult to breathe when lying flat (orthopnea) (BOX 39.2).

    Struggling to breathe is both exhausting and dangerous for the client, as usually more energy is used (and therefore more oxygen) in doing so. Report your observations to your supervisor promptly and accurately so that quick action may be taken to correct the problem and prevent it from getting worse. You should be aware, however, that if the client uses an oxygen tank, you should never turn up the oxygen level if the client’s care plan does not direct you to do so. In certain circumstances, turning up the oxygen could cause extreme harm to the client and can make the client even more short of breath!

    Abnormal Respiratory Patterns

    The normal respiration rate for adults is 12 to 20 per minute. Infants and children have faster rates (see TABLE 24.3, on page 544). Normal respirations are quiet, effortless, and regular, and both sides of the chest should rise and fall in a uniform manner. The medical term for normal respirations is eupnea.

    The following respiratory patterns are abnormal (FIGURE 39.2):

    FIGURE 39.2 Some abnormal respiratory patterns.

    1. • Tachypnea—rapid (tachy) breathing (pnea). Respirations are 20 or more per minute. Fever, exercise, pain, airway obstruction, and hypoxemia are common causes.
    2. • Bradypnea—slow (brady) breathing (pnea). Respirations are fewer than 12 per minute. Drug overdoses and nervous system disorders are common causes.
    3. • Apnea—the lack or absence (a) of breathing (pnea). It occurs in cardiac arrest and respiratory arrest. Sleep apnea in adults and periodic apnea in newborns are other types of apnea.
    4. • Kussmaul respirations—deep and rapid respirations characteristic of diabetic acidosis or other conditions causing acidosis.
    5. • Hypoventilation—slow (hypo), shallow, and sometimes irregular respirations. Common causes include lung disorders (such as pneumonia) that affect the alveoli. Other causes include obesity, airway obstruction, adverse effects of drugs, and nervous system and musculoskeletal disorders affecting the respiratory muscles.
    6. • Hyperventilation—rapid (hyper) and deeper-than-normal respirations. Its many causes include asthma, emphysema, infection, fever, nervous system disorders, hypoxia, anxiety, pain, and some drugs.
    7. • Dyspnea —difficult, laboured, or painful (dys) breathing (pnea). Heart disease, exercise, and anxiety are common causes.
    8. • Cheyne–Stokes—respirations that gradually increase in rate and depth and then become shallow and slow. Breathing may stop (apnea) for 10 to 20 seconds. Drug overdose, heart failure, renal failure, and brain disorders are common causes. Cheyne–Stokes respirations are common when death is near.

Hypoxia is a life-threatening condition because without enough oxygen to function, cells will be damaged or die.

    Promoting Oxygenation

    For the body to get enough oxygen, air must move deeply into the lungs and reach the alveoli, where O2 and CO2 are exchanged. Disease and injury can prevent air from reaching the alveoli. Pain, immobility, and narcotics interfere with deep breathing and with coughing up secretions. These factors allow the secretions to collect in the airway and lungs, interfering with air movement and lung function. Secretions also provide an environment for microbes to grow and multiply, with the possibility of leading to a life-threatening infection.

    FIGURE 39.3 The client is in the orthopneic position. Note that a pillow has been placed on the overbed table for the client’s comfort.

    To completely meet the client’s oxygen needs, the care plan will list measures to promote oxygenation. Some of the measures may include the administration of O2 or other activities that simply involve movement—for example, a short walk—to expand the client’s lungs. Some other measures that are commonly listed in care plans are discussed below.

    Positioning and Rest Periods

    For clients confined to bed, breathing is usually easier in the semi-Fowler’s or Fowler’s position. Clients who experience orthopnea (shortness of breath when lying in a flat or supine position) often prefer to sit up and lean over a table (called the orthopneic position). You can increase the client’s comfort by placing a pillow on the overbed table (FIGURE 39.3). Make sure that clients have sufficient rest periods between activities so that they can breathe more easily without struggling for oxygen (BOX 39.4).

    When a client is confined to bed for long periods, it is important that the person changes positions frequently, at least every 2 hours. If the client is not moved, the lung cannot expand on one side, and secretions could pool (collect) at the bottom of the lung. Unless the physician has placed restrictions on positioning, the client should never lie on one side for a long time. As the client’s support worker, you should always follow your client’s care plan.

    BOX 39.4 Caring for a Client Who Has Altered Respiratory Function

    When caring for the client who has altered respiratory function (see Chapter 33), whether at home or in a facility, it is important to provide care, such as the following, that will relax the client and reduce the client’s need for more oxygen:

    Positioning

    1. • Assist the client into the sitting position—the semi-Fowler’s or high Fowler’s position (see Chapter 14)—whenever possible, as this makes breathing easier. Clients who are sitting up can expand their lungs much more easily than when they are lying down. Place several pillows behind the client’s back or use a large foam wedge if the client has one.
    2. • Use a reclining chair for the client’s naps or sleeping. A reclining chair can enable the client to sleep in a partially upright position, which gives the lungs more room to breathe.
    3. • If the client is in a hospital bed, mechanically elevate the head of the bed to reduce the client’s need to move in bed without help.

    Time Management

    1. • Provide care at a time when the client is as well rested and comfortable as possible.
    2. • Allow for frequent rest periods.
    3. • Be organized. Arrange to do all of your care in the shortest, least strenuous way possible.

    Client Care

    1. • Assist the client with moving and turning to reduce oxygen need as much as possible.
    2. • Never wear powders or apply powders on the client, as they can irritate the airway and cause the client to have difficulty breathing.
    3. • Make sure the client is as comfortable as possible. Discomfort can cause a client to become anxious, and anxiety will increase the need for oxygen. Smooth out the sheets on the bed, and turn the pillow over to the smoother, cooler side. Make sure that the client does not have any restrictive clothing on.
    4. • Encourage relaxation. Feelings of tension can contribute to breathlessness, and muscle tension can make the act of breathing harder. You can help reduce the client’s tension level by promoting pleasant activities as well as relaxation exercises.
    5. • Make sure that the bedside and surrounding area are clean and dust-free. Wipe down all surfaces with a damp cloth (do not use disinfectants around the client as they can trigger an allergic reaction). Remove any clutter from the room, as clutter can trap dust.
    6. • If you must make the client’s bed, make sure that the sheets are never shaken in the air (see Chapter 21).
    7. • Open a window or use a fan to get air moving in the room; air movement will help the client to feel less short of breath.
    8. • Make sure the client is well hydrated. Most clients who are short of breath tend to breathe through the mouth, which can dry the lips, oral mucous membranes, and the throat. Apply water-soluble jelly to the lips (never use oil or grease, as these substances are combustible and may create a risk for fire). Have the client drink fresh water frequently, at least once every hour. Avoid the use of straws, as drinking from a straw increases the breathing effort and requires more energy.
    9. • In the winter, use a humidifier or place pans of water near radiators to moisten the room air that is breathed in. A humidifier can help loosen up thick phlegm (mucus) in the lungs so that it is coughed up easily, and it will also keep the throat and nasal passages from becoming too dry. Wash the humidifier or pans daily to prevent bacteria from colonizing in this warm, moist setting.
    10. • If it is allowed on the client’s care plan, offer throat lozenges or hard candy to help moisten a dry throat. Sucking on lozenges or candy increases the production of saliva, which moistens the throat as the lozenge or candy is swallowed.

    Other Professional Responsibilities

    1. • Avoid wearing perfumes that can trigger an allergic reaction, which would make breathing more difficult for the client.
    2. • Be friendly and professional at all times, but try not to engage the client too much in conversation. Ask the client questions that require short or one-word answers only.

    Oxygen Therapy

    1. • Oxygen supports combustion, so you must keep combustible items (e.g., clothing, towels, and sheets that are static-charged) away from the oxygen source to reduce the risk for a fire.

    Coughing and Deep Breathing

    Coughing removes mucus from the lungs, and deep breathing moves air into most parts of the lungs. Therefore, coughing and deep-breathing exercises help clients with respiratory disorders. These exercises are done after surgery and during bed rest; however, clients may be reluctant to do them as they may cause pain after injury or surgery, or the client may be afraid of opening an incision during coughing.

    Coughing and deep breathing help prevent pneumonia and atelectasis. The walls of healthy lungs (lined in a sticky mucous membrane) expand with every deep breath and are thereby prevented from collapsing and sticking to each other. In atelectasis, a portion of the lung collapses after mucus collects in a section of the airway, preventing air from entering that part of the lung. Clients who do not perform deep-breathing and coughing exercises risk developing atelectasis because mucus is then allowed to collect in their lungs, increasing the chance of the sticky, mucus-lined lungs not expanding. The mucus that collects in the lungs and the mucus on the walls are attracted to each other, causing that portion of the lung to collapse. The postsurgical period, bed rest, lung disease, and paralysis are risk factors for atelectasis.
