    Hair removal of any kind, including that of pubic hair, can result in ingrown hairs (also known as razor bumps), which may be painful, and the surrounding skin becomes red. An ingrown hair (FIGURE 18.6) does not grow straight out of the follicle opening. Instead, it curls into the side wall of the hair follicle or into the skin surface, causing the skin to become inflamed. This inflammation can result in the development of a pimple (such as a papule or a pustule) or a cyst (nodule), both of which are usually tender. The hair tip may continue to grow into the adjacent skin outside the follicle and may produce a larger and more inflamed area, which can produce permanent scarring or changes in the colour of the skin. If you observe any ingrown hairs, you should report and record them, according to your agency’s policies.

    As a support worker, circumstances may arise in which you would be asked to remove some or all of a client’s pubic hair, provided it is within your agency’s policies. The pubic area is very delicate and can easily be nicked by razors or cut by sharp scissors. However, if you are required to do so, you should do it in a tactful, professional manner, ensuring that the client’s rights to privacy and dignity are preserved at all times. The client’s care plan would specify the steps involved in removing or trimming pubic hair.

    Care of Nails and Feet

    Foot and nail care is a very important part of daily personal care to prevent infection, injury, and odours. Long or broken nails can scratch skin or snag clothing. Hangnails, ingrown nails (nails that grow in at the side), and nails torn away from the skin cause skin breaks, which can provide portals of entry for microbes and pathogens.

    Feet are easily infected and injured. Dirty or moist feet, socks, and stockings harbour microbes and cause odours, and shoes and socks provide a warm, moist environment for the growth of microbes. Socks should be changed every day. Injuries occur from stubbing of toes, stepping on sharp objects, or being stepped on. Shoes that fit poorly can cause bunions, calluses, corns, blisters, or ingrown nails, all of which can be considerably painful. Infections or foot injuries are particularly serious in older persons and in people with circulatory disorders. Poor circulation in the feet prolongs healing because it prevents white blood cells (WBCs) and oxygenated blood from entering that area. WBCs engulf and eat infected material, and oxygen provides the necessary energy for cells to function well. Diabetes and vascular disease are common causes of poor circulation, and gangrene and amputations are serious complications (see Chapter 33).

    Important Things to Watch for

    It is your responsibility to check the client’s feet every day. Daily checking is especially important if the client has a circulatory disorder or diabetes or takes medications that affect blood clotting. Notify your supervisor if you observe any of the following:

    1. • Very dry skin
    2. • Foot odours
    3. • Cracks or breaks in the skin, especially between the toes
    4. • Ingrown nails
    5. • Loose nails
    6. • Reddened, irritated, or calloused areas on the feet, heels, or ankles
    7. • Drainage or bleeding
    8. • Change in colour or texture of nails, especially black, thick, or brittle nails
    9. • Corns, bunions, or blisters

    Nail Soaks Prior to Nail Care

    Fingernail and foot soaks may be part of the client’s care plan, and you may be responsible for providing them (see the procedure Providing Nail and Foot Care, on p. 353). Generally, nails are easier to trim and clean when they have been softened. However, you should not cut a client’s toenails immediately after soaking, as the tissue around the nail will be puffy from water absorption and more prone to nicks and cuts. Nails should never be soaked for longer than 5 minutes. Follow your agency’s policies and the client’s care plan for the right method to soften the client’s nails. Some agencies advise the use of specific lotions or wrapping the foot with a warm, moist towel for this purpose.

    Tub baths are a good time for soaking feet and fingernails. If soaking is done at other times, the client can sit on the side of the tub and soak the feet. Make sure the client can step into and out of the tub. Otherwise, soak the feet in a basin. If it is comfortable, the client can soak their fingers in the sink or in a small basin or bowl. Usually, fingernail or foot soaks should last no longer than 5 minutes. Feet are easily burned, so you should check with your supervisor about the correct water temperature for foot soaks. You must be very careful when preparing soaks for people with diabetes, decreased sensation, or circulatory problems. Some facilities do not recommend soaking a diabetic client’s feet because it might lead to drying and cracking of the skin. Such clients may not feel hot temperatures and are at risk for scalding or burning of their feet.

    Think About Safety

    Safety Considerations Regarding Nail Care (also refer to Chapter 17)

    1. • Some employers do not allow support workers to cut or trim toenails under any circumstances, so you should always follow your employer’s policy. Trimming and clipping toenails can easily cause injuries.
    2. • Not all facilities allow nail care to be performed while the client is in bed. It is important to follow your facility’s policies at all times.
    3. • Nail care is much easier when the client is upright (if possible at all).
    4. • Nails should never be clipped directly after soaking, especially in older persons, as the nails tend to tear (especially fingernails).
    5. • Generally, only registered staff are permitted to provide nail care to clients who are diabetic or have circulation challenges. Always refer to the client’s care plan prior to starting nail care.
    6. • Diabetic clients should not have their feet soaked for any longer than 5 minutes at a time.
    7. • Observe the client while providing nail care to see if the client is comfortable and safe. Report and record any observations immediately if there are any concerns.
    8. • Cut fingernails only if instructed to do so by your supervisor and if it is included in the client’s care plan. Nail clippers should be used to cut fingernails only— know your agency’s policy.
    9. • When using nail clippers, use extreme caution to prevent damage to nearby tissue. Toenail scissors or ceramic nail files are preferred over the use of toenail clippers when trimming toenails. Any equipment used for cutting nails should be discarded or thoroughly sanitized after each use, according to your agency’s guidelines and policies.
    10. • If a client has a disease or illness that increases the risk for infection, you should know this.
    11. • Support workers do not cut or trim toenails of the following clients:
      1. • Clients who have diabetes
      2. • Clients with poor circulation to the legs and feet
      3. • Clients who take medications that affect blood clotting
      4. • Clients with very thick or ingrown nails
      5. • Clients who are uncooperative or combative
    13. • If a client needs their toenails trimmed, tell your supervisor—never assume that you can go ahead and trim them yourself. If you are allowed to trim a client’s toenails, always use either large, straight nail clippers or specialized toenail clippers.
    14. • In many areas, nurses or other health care workers who have specialized training in nail care can be contacted to provide nail care to a client. The client usually must pay a small fee for this service.
    15. • Support workers do not treat corns, bunions, or blisters, as these foot problems may require the care of a podiatrist. A podiatrist is a physician who specializes in the evaluation and treatment of diseases of the foot.

    Changing Clothing, Incontinence Briefs, and Hospital Gowns

    Like everyone else, clients who live in the community or in long-term care (continuing care) facilities dress and undress at least once a day. Most clients wear clothes during the day and change into sleepwear at bedtime. Some are incontinent of urine or feces or both and must have their incontinence brief changed when necessary (see the procedure Changing an Incontinence Brief, on page 360). Clients who have incontinence may require more frequent changes of clothing. Some clients who have illnesses and cannot get out of bed choose to wear sleepwear and robes through the day. Clients who are hospitalized wear hospital gowns, especially if they are receiving intravenous (IV) therapy. Hospital gowns are put on and removed in a certain way when the client is receiving IV therapy (see the procedure Changing the Gown of a Client With Intravenous Tubing, on page 366).

    Helping clients dress and undress is easier when the clients can move their arms and legs. Special techniques are required to safely, comfortably, and quickly dress and undress clients with arm or leg injuries or paralysis; some clients may require the assistance of two or three people to change their gown and be repositioned. Follow the guidelines in BOX 18.2 when dressing and undressing clients.

    Pay particular attention to clients’ comfort when you are dressing them. Moving the joints can sometimes be very uncomfortable for the client, so you should always be organized and efficient and dress them as quickly and painlessly as possible. Never jerk or pull the client, which can be very uncomfortable and distressing.

    When assisting a client with putting on socks and pants, put the client’s socks on before putting the pants on. By dressing the client in this order, loose threads or the hem of the pants will not catch on the client’s toes. Ensure that the client’s socks go on smoothly and are not binding or bunched at the top.

    BOX 18.2 Guidelines for Changing Clients’ Clothing or Hospital Gowns

    1. • Provide for privacy. Practise proper hand hygiene. Do not expose the client.
    2. • Encourage the client to do as much as possible.
    3. • As much as possible, allow the client to choose what to wear. Make sure the right undergarments are chosen.
    4. • Support the arm or leg when removing or putting on a garment.
    5. • Put clothing on the affected side (the side affected by disease or disability) first. Never refer to the affected side as the “bad” side. When dressing a client, remember the acronym DAF—Dress Affected side First.
    6. • Remove clothing from the unaffected side (the side not affected by disease or disability) first. When undressing a client, remember the acronym RUF—Remove from Unaffected side First.
    7. • If the client is on an IV pump, a registered staff member may be required to turn off the pump or start it up, or to re-insert tubing. It is important to understand your facility’s policies.

    Report and record the following observations after helping a client dress or undress:

    1. • How much help was given
    2. • How the client tolerated the procedure
    3. • Any complaints from the client

    Be aware that some clients (and their family members) may request a support worker they feel the client would be most comfortable with for getting dressed. For example, a client with male genitalia might not want a young female support worker because the client has grandchildren that age, or a client with female genitalia might not want to have perineal care given to them by a male support worker.

    Applying Elastic Anti-Embolic Stockings and Bandages

    Some clients need to wear anti-embolic stockings or bandages on their legs, as ordered by their physicians. Anti-embolic stockings, also called compression stockings or thromboembolic devices (TED), are close-fitting, circular, knitted, elastic stockings that are designed to prevent swelling and blood clots in lower extremities by exerting pressure on the veins, increasing venous blood flow to the heart. When prescribed, these stockings must be applied as part of the client’s daily dressing routine. Clients with circulatory disorders and heart disease and those on bed rest are at risk for blood clots. A blood clot (thrombus) can form in deep leg veins, break loose, travel through the bloodstream, and lodge in a distant vessel. If the clot lodges in the lungs, severe respiratory problems or death can result.

    Applied incorrectly, anti-embolic stockings can harm the client. Therefore, you may be asked to apply them only on clients in stable condition. It is your responsibility to know and follow your employer’s policy, the stocking manufacturer’s instructions, and the client’s care plan.

    Applying Anti-Embolic Stockings

    Anti-embolic stockings come in a variety of sizes and lengths (thigh-high or knee-high). The care plan lists the correct size to use. Stockings are applied in the morning before the client gets out of bed, as otherwise the client’s legs can swell from sitting or standing, making the stockings very difficult to put on. They are removed every 8 hours for 30 minutes or according to the care plan. The client lies in bed whenever the stockings are off to prevent the legs from swelling. Most stockings have an opening near the toes. Others have an opening at the top or bottom of the foot. The opening is used to check circulation as well as skin colour and temperature.

    The client usually has two pairs of stockings. One pair is washed while the client wears the other pair. Wash the stockings by hand with a mild soap, and then hang them to dry.

    Make sure that the stockings are smooth, not bunched up or binding, and without twists, creases, or wrinkles after you apply them. Bunching, binding, or twisting stockings can affect circulation, and creases and wrinkles can cause skin breakdown. Report and record the following observations after applying elastic stockings:

    1. • Skin colour and skin temperature
    2. • Leg and foot swelling
    3. • Signs of skin breakdown
    4. • Complaints of pain, tingling, or numbness

    Applying Elastic Bandages

    Elastic bandages are applied to the upper or lower extremities and have the same purposes as anti-embolic stockings; they may also hold dressings in place.

    The bandage is applied from the lower (distal) part of the extremity to the top (proximal) part. Your supervisor and the care plan will tell you which area to bandage and the proper width of the bandage to use for that client. If the roll of bandage is not long enough to cover the part, another roll of bandage may be added.

    Elastic bandages are removed every 8 hours for 30 minutes. If specified in the client’s care plan, bandages may be washed in mild soap and water, rinsed well, patted dry, and then hung up to dry overnight. Cleaning them properly is especially important if the client perspires on the bandage or has soiled the bandage in any way.

    Follow these guidelines when applying elastic bandages:

    1. • Use the correct length and width.
    2. • Position the body part in good alignment.
    3. • Face the client during the procedure.
    4. • Expose fingers or toes, if possible, to allow circulation checks.
    5. • Apply the bandage with firm, even pressure.
    6. • Make sure the bandage is firm and snug. It must not be tight, as a tight bandage can affect circulation.
    7. • Check the colour and temperature of the extremity every hour.
    8. • Reapply a loose, wrinkled, moist, or soiled bandage.

    Report and record the following observations after applying elastic bandages:

    1. • Skin colour and skin temperature
    2. • Swelling in the leg and foot
    3. • Signs of skin breakdown
    4. • Complaints of pain, tingling, or numbness

    Care of Wigs

    Wigs are worn for a variety of reasons. Some Orthodox Jewish married women remove all their hair and wear only a head covering, while some others wear a wig to cover their hair. Some clients may have lost their hair due to natural aging or as a result of chemotherapy treatments for cancer. Some clients may be bald from birth, and some others may have become bald after an illness or an injury to their scalp.

    Whatever the reason, people may wear wigs for warmth, modesty, or body image. Wigs can be very expensive, so it is important to care for them properly so that they are kept clean, attractive, and comfortable. When caring for a client’s wig, you should follow the maintenance steps in the client’s care plan. Following are some tips on maintenance.4

    Storage

    After removing the wig, keep it on a wig stand (or head form) to maintain the shape. Smooth out straight hair or fluff curls with a wire brush or pick. Cover the wig with a hair net that maintains the style without matting. Avoid leaving the wig near heat (radiators, vents), humidity (showers), or dusty areas.

    Washing

    Wigs are usually washed and cared for by the client (or family), a hairdresser, or perhaps even by representatives from the store that sells specialty items such as wigs or mastectomy prosthetics. Because wigs can be very expensive to replace, it is important to check your agency’s policies before washing a client’s wig . Some agencies do not allow support workers to wash a client’s wig or hairpiece. Shampooing frequency depends on wearing frequency. Generally, a wig should be washed after every 6 to 8 uses in warm climates or after every 12 to 15 uses in cooler ones. However, if the client is especially active, uses heavy hairspray, or lives in an especially humid climate or in one with poor air quality, the wig should be washed about once a week to remove excess perspiration and dirt.

    Before washing, smooth out a straight-hair wig or fluff a curly-hair wig gently and completely with a wire brush or pick. Add a capful of wig shampoo or baby shampoo to a basin of cool (never hot) water. Immerse the wig gently and soak for 2 minutes. Rinse well by swishing in clean, cool water. Gently squeeze out excess water without twisting the wig. For extra shine and softness, apply wig conditioner and leave it on for 5 minutes. Rinse again in clean, cool water. Gently squeeze out excess water without twisting the wig.

    Drying

    After washing the wig, tightly finger-squeeze each curl. Gently towel-blot both straight-hair and curly-hair wigs to remove excess water. Let the wig air-dry on a clean, dry towel or on a wire head form that allows air circulation. Do not set the wig stand near heat sources or in direct sunlight. Never use blow-dryers or other heat appliances on synthetic wigs. Also, be sure not to comb, brush, or pick a wet wig unless you are completely restyling it. As the wig dries, shake it out periodically for fast drying. Once the wig is fully dry, style it in the normal manner.

    Wearing

    Once the client has become accustomed to the wig, the wig can be worn for daily activities. Advise the client to avoid blasts of heat on synthetic wigs—for example, when quickly opening an oven door.

    Infection can be a serious safety and health hazard. Some infections cause only minor symptoms, whereas others are much more serious. They may delay a person’s recovery, create long-term health problems, or even cause death in some situations. Older persons, people with health challenges, and people with disabilities are at increased risk of developing infections. Infections can easily spread from worker to client, from client to client, or from client to worker.

    All members of the health care team must take every precaution to protect clients and themselves from infection. As a support worker, in order to prevent infection, you must understand how infections spread and also must follow your employer’s infection-control policies. Infection control refers to policies and procedures used to prevent the spread of infection within health care settings. Proper hand hygiene—the easiest way to reduce the risk for infection spread to yourself and others—should always accompany any other infection-control efforts. 1

    Microorganisms

    A microorganism (microbe), sometimes called a “germ” or “bug,” is a form of life (organism) that is so small (micro) it can be seen only with a microscope. Microbes live and grow everywhere—in and on water, air, food, soil, plants, animals, and inanimate (nonliving) objects such as clothing, furniture, medical equipment, and personal care items. Microbes also live and grow on and in people—on the skin and in the mouth, nose, respiratory tract, stomach, and intestines.

    Most microbes usually do not cause infection or disease; these are called nonpathogens. Microbes that are harmful and can cause infection or disease are called pathogens.

    Types of Microorganisms

    Within health care settings, the four types of microbes that cause the greatest risk for infection are bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites.

      1. Bacteria—single-celled microbes that naturally occur on living, dead, or inanimate objects. They can multiply rapidly or, if necessary for survival, can remain dormant (nonactive), surrounded by a protective shell called an endospore. Several types of bacteria can form endospores (primarily Bacillus and Clostridium [botulism] species), which protect them from harsh environmental conditions, making them difficult to kill. In the right conditions, bacteria can become active again and resume multiplying rapidly. They can be nonpathogenic, or they can cause serious infections in any body system. Infections caused by bacteria are usually treated with antibiotics. Examples of bacteria include Salmonella enteritidis, Helicobacter pylori, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Staphylococcus aureus.
      1. Viruses—infectious particles that are much smaller than bacteria and infect living cells to grow and multiply. They take over the cells’ machinery to produce new virus particles. Viruses can kill their host cells while producing new virus particles (lytic cycle), or they can incorporate into a host cell’s DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and remain dormant for a certain period (lysogenic cycle). Acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), hepatitis, influenza, herpes, and the common cold are examples of infections caused by viruses. Antibiotics are not effective against viruses, but a few antiviral medications that can kill specific viruses are available. Some viral diseases (such as measles, mumps, rubella, polio, and influenza) can be prevented by vaccination. A vaccine is a suspension containing a live or killed bacterium or virus that is administered for the purpose of inducing active immunity to a specific infectious disease. Examples of viruses include chicken pox (varicella zoster); hepatitis A, B, and C; herpes simplex (“cold sore virus”); influenza; and COVID-19.
      1. Fungi—microbes that live only on organic matter such as plants and animals. Certain types of yeasts and moulds are common fungi that can be pathogenic. In humans, fungi can infect the mouth, vagina, skin, feet, and other body areas. Most fungal infections, such as athlete’s foot, are mild, but fungi can also cause life-threatening infections, especially in susceptible people. Examples of common fungal infections include athlete’s foot (tinea pedis), ringworm (tinea corporis), scalp fungus (tinea capitis), and Candida (found in the mouth [thrush], intestines, or vagina).
      1. Parasites—organisms that derive nourishment and protection from other living organisms, known as hosts. Parasites may be transmitted from animals to humans, from humans to humans, or from humans to animals. They may be transmitted from host to host through consumption of contaminated food and water—for example, by putting something that has touched the feces of an infected animal or human into the mouth. The illnesses they can cause range from mild discomfort to severe illness and possibly death. Parasites range in size and shape from tiny, single-celled, microscopic organisms to worms that can be seen without a microscope.2 Parasites are more common in developing countries. Common parasites in Canada include lice, mites, pinworms, Giardia, and Trichomonas (a sexually transmitted parasitic infection).

    All four types of microbes require certain conditions in which to live and grow. The reservoir (host) is the environment where the microbe lives and grows. Microbes grow in reservoirs that are warm and dark, and they need water and nourishment. Most microbes also need oxygen, although some can thrive without it. Microbes that infect humans have evolved to grow best at body temperature but are destroyed by heat and light.

    Normal Flora

    Normal flora are made up of the microbes that naturally live and grow in certain locations on or in the human body. Certain microbes, for example, are found on the skin and in the respiratory tract, digestive system, eyes, ears, and vagina. These microbes are harmless (nonpathogens) and are beneficial to the body (“good bacteria”). The normal flora contain organisms (bacteria) that play a number of essential roles in the health of humans: They aid digestion, produce some vitamins, and help fight off infections of pathogenic organisms. If a bacterium in the normal flora is transferred to another site in the body, it can cause serious disease. For example, Escherichia coli (E. coli) is a bacterium normally found in the colon that assists with food metabolism, but if E. coli enters the bloodstream or the urinary tract, it can cause a serious infection.

    The numbers of microbes that make up the normal flora of a body surface are constantly affected by certain factors, including infection by pathogenic organisms. Maintenance of healthy normal flora, therefore, is a delicate number balance of all types of beneficial bacteria that share the same body area. When a person is ill or on medications (such as antibiotics), some of the normal flora can be killed, disrupting this balance. The person can then develop a potentially harmful infection. Probiotic foods such as yogourt, cheeses, and pickled foods can be very beneficial in maintaining the normal flora, especially while a person is on antibiotic medication.

    Antibacterial soaps—intended for handwashing or for washing contaminated substances from skin—have been blamed for certain illnesses because their overuse (using them for bathing, for example) can destroy the delicate balance of a person’s normal flora. It is important to recognize that the normal flora play a critical role in helping our immune system protect us from infection, and overuse of antimicrobial soaps and alcohol-based sanitizers can damage our beneficial bacterial organisms.

    Multidrug-Resistant Organisms

    Bacteria reproduce rapidly, and their large numbers can quickly make a person ill if there are not enough “good bacteria,” or normal flora, to stop them from multiplying. Misuse and overprescribing of antibiotics in the past have led certain strains of bacteria to mutate (change) to become more resistant to most antibiotics. Many bacteria now have DNA that allow them to make membrane proteins that eliminate antibiotic medications, which then have no effect on the bacteria. Some bacteria have developed resistance by producing proteins that neutralize or deactivate antibiotic medications. In addition, many bacteria have the ability to undergo “conjugation,” which allows them to transfer antibiotic-resistant genes between different species of bacteria, meaning that some people will get infections that are resistant to most antibiotics.

    Bacteria that are resistant to more than one antibiotic are known as multidrug-resistant organisms (MROs), sometimes called “superbugs.” Infections with MROs are becoming increasingly common and are easily spread among people at risk for infection (such as babies, older persons, and people with serious medical conditions or a weakened immune system). Since MROs are difficult to treat, they can be fatal if the infection is severe. As a result, MROs pose a serious threat to clients in health care facilities.

    The most common MROs in Canada are methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), and Clostridium difficile (C. difficile, which causes diarrhea infection common in hospitalized clients on antibiotic treatment), although new strains of MROs are continually being reported throughout the world by health care officials. One MRO that is increasingly associated with urinary tract infections, wound infections, pneumonia, and bloodstream infections is extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL). It is treated in a similar manner as MRSA or VRE (BOX 19.1).

    As a support worker, you will be trained to take special precautions when you are in contact with clients who are infected, or suspected of being infected, with MROs, and you should be aware of the precautions you must take for any new MRO that you may come into contact with (see Box 19.4 Ways the Support Worker Can Break the Chain of Infection on page 386). If precautions are not taken, many healthy health care workers can become carriers for these infections and spread them to susceptible clients without having symptoms themselves.

    If you are responsible for providing personal care to a number of clients in a facility, personal care for clients who are not infected with an MRO should be provided first and then you should proceed to clients who are infected with an MRO. This practice can reduce the risk of spreading the infection to uninfected clients. It cannot be emphasized enough that hand hygiene (especially handwashing and wearing gloves when moving from working with one infectious client to another) is a necessary component in the fight against the spread of MROs (see the Think About Safety: Caring for Clients Who Are Infected With an MRO in Their Home box).

    BOX 19.1 Multidrug-Resistant Organisms: C. difficile, MRSA, and VRE

    Clostridium difficile (C. difficile)

    Clostridium difficile (C. difficile) is a bacterium that causes mild to severe diarrhea and intestinal conditions like pseudomembranous colitis (inflammation of the colon). C. difficile is the most frequent cause of infectious diarrhea in hospitals and long-term care facilities in Canada, as well as in other industrialized countries. Symptoms include watery diarrhea, fever, loss of appetite, nausea, and abdominal pain and tenderness.

    Most cases of C. difficile occur in patients who are taking certain antibiotics in high doses or over a prolonged period of time. Some antibiotics can destroy a person’s normal bacteria found in the gut, causing C. difficile bacteria to grow. When this occurs, the C. difficile bacteria produce toxins, which can damage the bowel and cause diarrhea. However, some people can have C. difficile bacteria present in their bowel and not show symptoms.

    As with any infectious disease, frequent hand hygiene is the most effective way of preventing the transmission of health care–associated infections. Handwashing with soap and water is important during C. difficile outbreaks and is one of the best defences against further spread of the bacteria. If you do not have access to soap and water, frequent use of alcohol-based hand rubs is encouraged. Most health care facilities provide alcohol-based hand rubs at entrances. Be sure to use them, but be aware that they are less effective than washing with soap and water as they do not destroy C. difficile spores.

    For people with mild symptoms, no treatment may be required. For more severe cases, medication and sometimes surgery may be necessary. There are also new treatments, such as fecal transplantation, currently being studied for treating persistent C. difficile infection.

    Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)

    Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a type of common bacterium that is not killed by most antibiotics. The symptoms of MRSA depend on which body system is infected. MRSA can cause pneumonia, sepsis, toxic shock syndrome, and necrotizing fasciitis (flesh-eating disease). Most people with MRSA do not have signs or symptoms of an infection but carry the bacteria on the skin, in the nose, or in the gastrointestinal tract. These people are known as carriers. A person who is a carrier of MRSA bacteria may not carry these bacteria forever, as they can be treated with certain powerful antibiotics, or the problem may resolve itself. S. aureus is a normal flora bacterium that normally colonizes, or grows in, the nostrils and the respiratory tract.

    Hospital inpatients who have incisions, wounds, or tubes, which make it possible for the germs to enter their bodies, are prone to MRSA infections. These infections are most likely spread in hospitals or long-term care settings through person-to-person contact, through contact with infected wound discharge or urine, or through health care workers who fail to practise hand hygiene or fail to wear proper gloves.

    A test can be done to confirm the presence of the MRSA germ. If the test proves positive for MRSA, multiple powerful IV antibiotics are prescribed.

    Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococcus (VRE)

    Enterococci are bacteria that are normally found in the digestive tract and are not dangerous in healthy people with strong immune systems, as the balance of healthy flora in their digestive tracts helps keep the bacteria from getting out of control. Because of overuse of antibiotics, however, a strain of Enterococcus, called vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), has become resistant to all antibiotics, including vancomycin. It is most commonly spread from the hands of health care workers who acquired VRE after contact with clients colonized or infected by the bacteria or from handling contaminated material or equipment.

    VRE is dangerous because it cannot be controlled with antibiotics, and it causes life-threatening infections in people with compromised immune systems—clients who are very young, very old, and very ill. Also, it can easily pass on the resistance gene to other, more dangerous bacteria such as Staphylococcus and Streptococcus.

    There are other ways that VRE can be spread—for example, from contact with contaminated surfaces such as call bells, bed rails, blood pressure cuffs, and other equipment. Contamination of the environment with VRE is more likely when a client has diarrhea.

    Both MRSA and VRE can cause serious infections. The best way to stop the spread of both the MRSA and VRE bacteria is by careful handwashing with an antibacterial soap; when gloves are necessary, health care workers should always change them between clients. As well, the number of people visiting a very sick person should be limited to one, and visitors should be sure to wash their hands well. Residents of long-term care facilities who have recently been admitted to a large hospital are usually tested for MROs before they are transferred back to their facilities. If they are found to have drug-resistant bacteria, they are then isolated from other residents.

    Not all clients who are infected with an MRO are admitted to a health care facility. Many would prefer to live in their own homes while they receive care.     Signs and Symptoms of Infection

    Older persons are especially at risk for infections because of the normal age-related changes occurring in their bodies. If they had a serious health concern before they were infected, they are also more likely than younger adults to become seriously ill or to die from an infection; for example, pneumonia, influenza, and COVID-19 are often life-threatening illnesses in older people because their immune systems are not as capable of mounting a strong reaction to new infections as the immune systems of younger, healthier people.

    Pandemics

    A pandemic occurs when a communicable illness or infection spreads quickly throughout the population of a country or even throughout the world. The Canadian government, for example, is preparing for an influenza (“the flu”) pandemic, and as of this writing the entire world is dealing with the COVID-19 pandemic (see the Think About Safety: COVID-19 and Its Effect on the World box). Influenza is a highly contagious infection of the respiratory tract by the causative (disease-causing) virus in airborne droplets. Symptoms include sore throat, cough, fever, muscular pain, and weakness. Normally, people are exposed to different strains of the influenza virus frequently during their lives, so even though the virus may change (or mutate), previous exposures to influenza may offer some protection against infection. This protection results from viruses mutating only some of the proteins on their surface each year. We usually have memory cells that will recognize at least some of the proteins, allowing us to recover quickly. However, for unknown reasons, three to four times in a century, a radical change takes place in the influenza A virus, causing a new strain to emerge.4 Then, we have no memory cells for any antigen, which can lead our immune systems to overreact, causing death, as occurred in the SARS (severe acute respiratory syndrome) epidemic in 2002 and 2003 and in the current COVID-19 pandemic. In such cases, even young, healthy people can die from influenza, as they did during the flu pandemic of 1918. In the past, radical mutation in pathogens has resulted in widespread illness and deaths, especially among the young, the ill, and the very old. Across Canada, public health care departments are involved in attempts to prevent such a disaster from happening through widespread health teaching and influenza vaccinations.

    COVID-19 and Its Effect on the World

    The Coronavirus 2019 (abbreviated as “COVID-19 virus”) is a recently formed virus that is highly contagious. Originating in 2019, this virus spread rapidly to countries around the world, including Canada. In less than a year, COVID-19 infected 30 million people and killed over 1 million people globally. The highest morbidity rates have been among people in long-term care facilities, bringing long-term care under scrutiny by all levels of government across Canada. There have also been a number of “post–COVID-19 illnesses” reported throughout the world, and they are currently being researched.

    The Chain of Infection

    A number of factors work together for an infection to develop. The spread of infection involves a process known as the chain of infection (FIGURE 19.2). The links in the chain are as follows:

    1. • Pathogen —a microbe capable of causing disease.
    2. • Reservoir —the environment where the pathogen lives before it infects a person. Remember, a pathogen can live on or in a person or animal (a host), food, water, soil, or inanimate objects.
    3. • Portal of exit —the path by which the pathogen leaves the reservoir. Portals of exit in the human body are the body openings (mouth; nose; and rectal, vaginal, and urethral openings), breaks in the skin (a scrape, cut, or other wound), and breaks in the mucous membranes (in the mouth, eyes, nose, vagina, and rectum). Pathogens are carried through the portals of exit by blood, body fluids, excretions, or secretions. These include urine, stool, vomitus, saliva, mucus, pus, vaginal discharge, semen, wound drainage, and sputum (respiratory secretions).
    4. • Mode of transmission —how the pathogen travels from the portal of exit to the next reservoir or host. Microbes can be transmitted by physical contact (direct and indirect); droplets in the air; air currents; an infected vehicle, or vector (such as a mosquito); or a fomite (any nonliving material, such as bed linen). The mode of transmission depends on the type of microbe. Some microbes are transmitted in more than one way (TABLE 19.1).
    5. • Portal of entry —where the pathogen enters the new host’s body. Portals of entry are the same as the portals of exit (in the human body)—body openings and breaks in the skin or mucous membranes.
    6. • Susceptible host —a person at risk for infection, including people who have not been exposed to that particular pathogen previously. Whether or not the pathogen grows and multiplies in the new host depends on the state of the person’s immune system and the presence of memory cells from a previous exposure. For example, people who have had chicken pox usually do not have symptoms again if they are re-exposed. Remember, the immune system protects the body from infection. A person whose immune system is weakened is immunocompromised. (Immuno comes from immunity, which means protection against a certain disease. Compromised means weakened.) Such a person is more likely to get the infection.

    Medical Asepsis

    Asepsis is the practice of reducing or eliminating potential pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites). There are two levels of asepsis: (1) medical asepsis, for which the goal is to exclude all pathogenic microorganisms through medical aseptic technique, and (2) surgical asepsis, in which the aim is to exclude all microorganisms and their spores through surgical aseptic technique.7

    Medical asepsis (or clean technique) refers to the practices that:

    1. • Reduce the number of pathogens
    2. • Prevent the spread of pathogens from person to person or place to place

    Medical asepsis is the most common technique, and it should be practised by all health care workers, who have an important role in preventing the spread of infection to others. Hand hygiene is the most important part of medical asepsis that health care workers can practise to prevent the spread of illnesses caused by pathogens.

    In most health care units, the term clean is used to refer to anything that has no visible contaminants. For example, after we have washed our hands or our clothes, we refer to them as being “clean.” We use clean gloves (not sterile surgical gloves), and we change them between clients. We use the word contaminated (unclean) to describe anything that has been exposed to pathogens, whether or not the contamination is visible (such as soiled bed linen). Contamination is the process of exposure to pathogens.

    Sterile means being free from all microbes, including pathogens, nonpathogens, and their spores. A clean environment is different from a sterile environment. Because infection is a risk, microbes cannot be present during surgery or invasive procedures, so operating rooms and instruments inserted into the body must be sterile. Surgical asepsis (also called sterile technique) refers to the practices that keep equipment and supplies free of all microbes and their spores. Sterilization is the process of destroying all microbes, both pathogens and nonpathogens, and their spores.

    Hand Hygiene

    Handwashing with soap and running water, using friction, or applying waterless, alcohol-based hand rubs is the easiest and most important way to prevent the spread of infection. You use your hands in almost every task you perform as a support worker. Your hands can easily pick up microbes from any client, place, or thing, and then transmit the microbes to other clients, places, or things. If you touch your eyes, nose, or mouth with your contaminated hands, you can also transfer microbes into your own body. Hand hygiene should therefore be practised before and after client contact of any kind. Because hands are contaminated very easily, you need to practise hand hygiene often and properly. Although hand hygiene is a simple procedure, many health care workers do it incorrectly (see the Think About Safety: Four Moments for Hand Hygiene box).

    You should remember that gloves never take the place of hand hygiene. Your skin will breed microbes in the dark, warm, moist environment of your gloved hands. You should always wash or sanitize your hands after removing your gloves and before putting new ones on. BOX 19.5 lists guidelines for when to practise proper hand hygiene.

    Waterless, alcohol-based hand rubs clean hands effectively and are convenient to use. Some manufacturers of these hand rubs recommend that you wash your hands with water and soap after a maximum of 10 applications of their product to remove accumulated dead skin and bacteria on your hands. Always wash your hands when they become visibly soiled. Your hands must be dry before you apply a hand rub because moisture dilutes the alcohol in the rub and makes it less effective. The hand rub is also not effective if your hands are visibly soiled.

    Wearing Gloves

    Disposable gloves provide a protective barrier between your hands and the client’s blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes. Gloves protect you from acquiring microbes from the client and protect the client from acquiring microbes that are on your hands. However, gloves are used as additional protection and not as a substitute for handwashing.

    Gloves are not required for most routine support care activities during which contact is limited to a client’s intact skin. Do not wear gloves when they are not needed. Your hands will sweat and then become dry, and the skin will eventually crack if gloves are worn continuously. Remember, cracked skin is a portal of entry for microbes.

    Focus On Home Care

    Hand Hygiene

    A client’s home might not have everything you need for proper handwashing, such as soap or paper towels. Do not use the client’s cloth towels for drying your hands, but use paper towels instead. Check with your supervisor before visiting the client. Most agencies provide workers with needed basic supplies to take with them on visits. Bring paper towels with you for home visits. You should always carry waterless, alcohol-based hand rub when you go on home visits in case the home lacks available hand hygiene supplies.

    Care of Supplies and Equipment

    Remember, microbes live on people and on objects. Contaminated equipment and supplies can easily become a source of infection.

    Most items used in facilities and home care are disposable, which means they are used only once and then discarded. In these cases, cleaning them is not an issue. Some items, however, are reused—for example, bedpans, urinals, washbasins, water pitchers, and drinking cups. In facilities where reusable items are used, they are labelled with the client’s name, room, and bed number. They are never used or “borrowed” for another client.

    Reusable items must be decontaminated. There are three levels of decontamination: cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization.

    Cleaning

    Cleaning reduces the number of microbes present on an item. It also removes any visible organic material such as dirt, blood, body fluids, secretions, and excretions. For some equipment, periodic cleaning is all that is required. For example, items such as crutches and blood pressure cuffs need to be cleaned only when visibly soiled and between uses on different clients. Follow employer policy about when and how to clean items. The following guidelines apply when cleaning equipment:

    1. • Wear personal protective equipment when cleaning items that may be contaminated with blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions. Personal protective equipment (PPE) consists of special clothing and equipment that act as a barrier between microbes and your hands, eyes, nose, mouth, and clothes such as gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection (goggles or face shields) (see page 406). Follow your employer’s policies for the use of PPE when cleaning equipment.
    2. • Rinse the item first in cold water to remove any organic material. Warm or hot water makes the protein in organic material thick, sticky, and hard to remove, similar to the way the protein in an egg will permanently change texture when it is heated.
    3. • Wash the item with soap and hot water.
    4. • Scrub the item thoroughly. Use a brush, if necessary.
    5. • Rinse the item in warm water.
    6. • Dry the item.
    7. • Disinfect or sterilize the item.
    8. • Disinfect equipment and the sink used in the cleaning procedure.
    9. • Discard gloves and other PPE.
    10. • Practise hand hygiene.

    Disinfection

    Disinfection is the process of destroying all pathogens except their spores. Spores can be destroyed only by the sterilization process. An item must be thoroughly cleaned before it is disinfected.

    Reusable items are disinfected with chemicals such as alcohols or chlorines (chemical disinfectants). Such items include the following:

    1. • Metal bedpans
    2. • Commodes
    3. • Countertops
    4. • Tubs
    5. • Room furniture

    Follow your employer’s policies regarding when and how to disinfect equipment and supplies (see the Focus on Home Care: Disinfectants box).

    Chemical disinfectants can burn and irritate the skin. Wear utility gloves or rubber household gloves that are waterproof to prevent skin irritation. Do not wear disposable gloves when using disinfectants, as the chemicals in the disinfectants will degrade (break down) the gloves. Some chemical disinfectants have special measures for use and storage. Check the material safety data sheet (MSDS) before handling a disinfectant (see Chapter 20). Follow your employer’s policies.

    FOCUS ON HOME CARE

    Disinfectants

    Detergent and hot water are common disinfectants for utensils, linens, and clothes. Many commercial products are available for household surfaces, including sinks, countertops, floors, toilets, tubs, and showers. In home care settings, use the products preferred by the client’s family or as instructed by your supervisor.

    Vinegar is an effective and cheap disinfectant. You can use it to clean bedpans, urinals, commodes, and toilets. To make a vinegar solution, mix 250 mL (1 cup) of white vinegar and 750 mL (3 cups) of water. Make sure you label the container properly as “vinegar solution.” Also, put the date and your name on the container.

    Sterilization

    Sterilization destroys all nonpathogens and pathogens, including spores. Very high temperatures are used because most microbes can be destroyed only by heat.

    Boiling water, radiation, liquid or gas chemicals, dry heat, and steam under pressure are sterilization methods. Items that enter the body or are used during surgery—for example, IV catheters, urinary catheters, and needles—have to be sterilized. In facilities, specially trained workers are responsible for carrying out the sterilization process. Therefore, support workers are not responsible for sterilizing equipment. However, you may be required to clean the items in preparation for sterilization (see the Focus on Home Care: Sterilization box).

    See BOX 19.6 for guidelines on how to follow medical asepsis measures.

    FOCUS ON HOME CARE

    Sterilization

    In home care, almost all medical items that must be sterile are commercially prepared and are disposable. However, you may be required to sterilize noninvasive items such as cloth diapers or glass baby bottles.

    Cloth diapers and other colour-fast linens can be sterilized by soaking them in a solution of 1 part household bleach and 10 parts water. One cup of bleach can be added to the wash when laundering items soiled with blood and body fluids. Always check with your client and your supervisor for direction before using bleach. Some linens cannot be bleached, so check the labels on clothes before using bleach to wash them (see Chapter 44).

    Glass baby bottles and other such items can be sterilized in boiling water. Place the items in a large pot filled with cold water. Cover the pot and bring the water to a full boil. Boil the items for at least 15 minutes, then turn off the heat and allow the water and the items to cool. Use tongs to remove the items, and let them air-dry on a clean towel. Although boiling water is a convenient and inexpensive method of sterilization in the home, it does not always kill all the spores and viruses. Therefore, facilities do not use boiling water for sterilizing items.

    Isolation Precautions

    Along with medical asepsis, infection-control policies use isolation precautions to reduce the risk for infection. Isolation precautions are guidelines for preventing the spread of pathogens. The most recent isolation precautions were developed in 1996 in the United States by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). These precautions have been accepted by Health Canada and are currently used in most Canadian health care facilities and community care agencies.

    There are two types of isolation precautions: Standard Practices (also known as Routine Practices) and Transmission-Based Practices. Both types of precautions provide guidelines on how to contain microbes. Standard Practices are practised at all times, whereas Transmission-Based Practices are practised when caring for clients with certain communicable diseases.

    Standard Practices

    Standard Practices (sometimes called Routine Practices or Standard Precautions, depending on the province you live in) are guidelines to prevent the spread of infection from the following:

    1. • Blood
    2. • All body fluids, secretions, and excretions (except sweat), whether or not blood is visible
    3. • Non-intact skin (skin with open sores, wounds, cuts, scrapes, or other breaks)
    4. • Mucous membranes (including the membranes in the nose, eyes, mouth, vagina, and rectum)

    BOX 19.7 summarizes the Standard Practices. Because you cannot tell by looking at people whether or not they carry pathogens, everyone must be considered a potential source of infection. Therefore, Standard Practices should be used when caring for all clients in all settings .

    Transmission-Based Practices

    Some clients have (or are suspected of having) communicable diseases, which are highly contagious. In certain cases, additional isolation precautions are required to protect staff, visitors, and other clients from the infection. Transmission-Based Practices are guidelines to contain pathogens within one area, usually the client’s room. Only clients infected or colonized with certain pathogens are placed under Transmission-Based Practices. These precautions are followed in addition to Standard Practices. You will be told when Transmission-Based Practices are ordered for a client.

    There are three types of Transmission-Based Practices: (1) airborne precautions, (2) droplet precautions, and (3) contact precautions (BOX 19.8). The choice of precautions depends on how a particular pathogen is spread.

    When clients in a facility are under Transmission-Based Practices, they are usually placed in private or semi-private rooms in order to isolate their germs from infecting others. The isolation room must have handwashing and toilet facilities. It also must have wastebaskets and laundry and garbage containers lined with plastic bags. An isolation sign that states that precautions are in place is usually displayed on the outside of the room door.

    Sometimes, Transmission-Based Practices are necessary for home care clients to protect other household members and home care workers. For example, if a client is infected with an MRO, Transmission-Based Practices might be required. As a member of the home care team, you will be told of the need for these precautions. An isolation sign is not needed in the home.

    The rules presented in BOX 19.9 are a guide for giving safe care when Transmission-Based Practices are used. Your employer may follow these or other guidelines. Know your employer’s policies about isolation precautions.

    Protective Measures

    Standard Practices and Transmission-Based Practices involve wearing PPE (gloves, mask, gown, and eye protection). Your supervisor and employer policy will guide you about when to use PPE. BOX 19.10 lists the proper order for putting on and taking off a full set of PPE. Standard Practices also involve careful handling of sharps. Transmission-Based Practices involve extra measures when removing linens, garbage, and equipment from the room. Special measures are also needed when transporting clients.

    Wearing Masks and Respiratory Protection

    Masks (also called surgical masks), which are disposable, prevent pathogens from entering your mouth and nose; protect you from splashes or sprays of blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions; and prevent the spread of microbes from your respiratory tract. Breathing can cause masks to become wet or moist. A wet or moist mask is considered to be contaminated, so you must apply a new mask if yours becomes damp (see the procedure Wearing a Mask).

    There are different types of masks for different situations. If you are required to wear respiratory protection, you must wear a special face mask that filters the air—for example, the N-95 respirator used for clients under airborne isolation precautions (FIGURE 19.14). BOX 19.11 discusses different types of masks. It is your responsibility to be fitted for the correct type of mask and to wear it properly, following employer policy. When airborne and droplet precautions are in place, everyone—staff, clients, and visitors—needs to wear these masks.

    Basic Needs and Transmission-Based Practices

    All clients have love, belonging, and self-esteem needs, but often care is not taken to meet them when Transmission-Based Practices are used. Visitors and staff tend to avoid the client, as the extra effort required to put on PPE may discourage them from visiting. Some visitors may be afraid of getting the disease and feel anxious during the visit and unsure about what they can touch safely. Without intending to, visitors and staff can make the client feel ashamed and guilty about having a contagious disease. As a result, the client can feel lonely, unloved, and rejected. Self-esteem is easily damaged, and depression is a risk.

    It is important to understand the reasons for using Transmission-Based Practices. Do not be afraid of the client. Remember, the precautions enable you to interact safely with the client and help meet the client’s need for belonging and self-esteem. The following actions are helpful:

    1. • Remember that it is the pathogen that is undesirable, not the client.
    2. • Treat the client with respect, kindness, and dignity.
    3. • Allow the client to make choices and decisions whenever possible.

    5. • Provide hobby materials, if possible.
    6. • Place a clock or radio in the client’s room.
    7. • Encourage the client to telephone family and friends.
    8. • Provide a current television schedule.
    9. • Organize your work so that you can stay to visit with the client.
    10. • Say hello from the doorway often if the door can be left open.

    Surgical Asepsis

    As was previously mentioned in this chapter, there are two levels of asepsis: (1) medical asepsis, discussed on pages 389–401, and (2) surgical asepsis. Surgical asepsis (sterile technique) refers to practices that keep equipment and supplies free of all microbes—pathogens and nonpathogens. Surgical asepsis is required any time the skin or sterile tissues are penetrated.

    Procedures that require surgical asepsis include catheterizations, IV administrations, suctioning, sterile dressing changes, and blood collection. Support workers are not normally authorized to do these procedures. However, you could be delegated to do them, or you might assist with these procedures. You should, therefore, understand the principles of surgical asepsis.

    Principles of Surgical Asepsis

    To prevent introducing microbes into the client, sterile procedures require the use of sterile gloves and sterile equipment. If an item is contaminated, the client is at risk for infection, so maintaining a sterile field is very important. A sterile field is a work area free of pathogens and nonpathogens (including spores). Remember that clean is different from sterile! All items in contact with the client must be kept sterile. BOX 19.12 lists the principles and practices of surgical asepsis. Follow them to maintain a sterile field when assisting with a sterile procedure.

    Chapter Review

    Key Points

    • A microorganism (microbe), sometimes called a “germ” or “bug,” is a form of life (organism) that is so small (micro) it can be seen only with a microscope.

    • Normal flora contain microbes that naturally live and grow in certain locations on or in the human body.

    • Nonpathogens do not cause illness or disease, but pathogens do.

    • Microbes require warmth, moisture, and darkness to live and grow. Many microbes (but not all) also require oxygen.

    • The three types of microbes are (1) bacteria, (2) viruses, and (3) fungi.

    • Microbes are transmitted through (1) contact, (2) droplet, (3) airborne, (4) vehicle, and (5) vector modes of transmission.

    • Signs and symptoms of infection include (1) fever and chills; (2) increased pulse and respiratory rates; (3) aches, pain, or tenderness; (4) fatigue and loss of energy; (5) loss of appetite; (6) nausea; (7) vomiting and diarrhea; (8) rash; (9) sores on mucous membranes; (10) redness and swelling of a body part; (11) discharge or drainage from the infected area that may have a foul odour; (12) new or increased cough, sore throat, or runny or stuffy nose; and (13) burning pain when urinating, or the need to urinate more often or with increased urgency.

    • Older persons’ signs and symptoms of infection may differ in part because older people tend to have lower body temperatures, decreased pain sensation, and less immune response to infection. Sometimes, the only sign of infection is a change in behaviour.

    • A local infection is an infection that occurs in one body part, such as an infected cut on the arm. A systemic infection involves the whole body, and the affected person would have generalized (entire body) signs and symptoms of infection.

    • A multidrug-resistant organism (MRO) is a pathogen that has developed resistance to most (or all) available antibiotics.

    • The spread of infection involves a process known as the chain of infection. The chain consists of a (1) pathogen, (2) reservoir, (3) portal of exit, (4) mode of transmission, (5) portal of entry, and (6) susceptible host.

    • A pandemic is an epidemic that spreads quickly over a large region or even worldwide. Examples of pathogens that have caused a pandemic include influenza and COVID-19.

    • A health care–associated infection (HAI) is an infection acquired from a health care provider or in a health care facility.

    • Vaccinations have been proven to prevent many serious communicable (contagious) illnesses.

    • Hand hygiene is the most important part of medical asepsis that health care workers can do to prevent the spread of illnesses caused by pathogens.

    • Standard Practices and Transmission-Based Practices involve wearing personal protective equipment, also known as PPE (gloves, mask, gown, and eye protection).

    Accident Risk Factors

    Anyone can be accidentally injured, but children, older persons, and people living with illnesses or disabilities are especially vulnerable to experiencing accidents. Factors that increase their risk for accidental injury are given below.

    The Client With Awareness Challenges

    To protect themselves from injury, people need to be aware of their surroundings. Children are not old enough to be aware of the potential harm that many hazards can cause. As well, people who live with intellectual disabilities, some physical disabilities, or who are disoriented might not be able to detect and avoid dangers and could, therefore, be harmed unintentionally. For example, a disoriented client may wander outside in the middle of winter without wearing a coat, gloves, or boots. Children and people who are unaware of their surroundings (such as an unconscious person) must, therefore, rely on others for protection.

    People (including health care workers!) who are preoccupied with things other than the task they are doing are more likely to have accidents because of a decreased awareness of their surroundings. Some may just be tired; according to research, fatigue is a factor in many motor vehicle accidents along Canada’s highways.1

    Some prescribed medications can cause drowsiness and thus reduced awareness of surroundings. People should always follow the instructions provided by their health care providers and heed the warnings on prescription containers. The influence of drugs or alcohol (or both) can compromise a person’s reflexes, vision, balance, or coordination.

    Distracted or Fatigued Caregivers

    Children are at an increased risk for being accidentally hurt during times of stress or change in the family’s routine because the caregiver’s attention is divided, and supervision may not be as careful as usual. For example, when a family member is ill, the caregiver may be focused on the person who is ill rather than on the child. Support workers can provide care and support to clients and their families at such times of stress and at times of change.

    Caregivers can also be at risk for injury because of fatigue (see the Think About Safety: How Fatigue Can Increase the Risk of Injury for Health Care Workers box).

    Think About Safety

    How Fatigue Can Increase the Risk of Injury for Health Care Workers

    Health care workers are at risk for injury, especially if they must work changing shifts. According to the Canada Safety Council, fatigue is a critical occupational safety concern for shift workers, and exhaustion is one of the most common health complaints among Canadian workers, especially women. Losing as little as 2 hours of sleep can negatively affect one’s alertness and performance.

    Sleep deprivation affects a person’s attentiveness and ability to respond to an emergency. Symptoms can include decreased judgement, decision-making, and memory; slower reaction time; lack of concentration; and worsened mood. Ways to reduce fatigue include the following:

    1. • Proper nutrition. A healthy diet provides longer-lasting energy—concentrate on eating complex carbohydrates (starch) rather than simple carbohydrates (sugar), and avoid eating fatty foods and nutrient-deprived foods.
    2. • Stress control. Practise healthy habits to reduce your stress, which can affect your ability to concentrate and to sleep (see Chapter 8).
    3. • Regular exercise. Exercises that focus on cardiovascular strength, muscle strengthening, and flexibility (see Chapter 15) can improve a person’s ability to concentrate, thereby reducing their risk for injuries.
    4. • Choosing employment that best meets your lifestyle and personal demands. Today’s employers are aware of the effects that fatigue can have on client and worker safety, and many are willing to offer flexible work schedules to their employees. Many employers also offer shorter shifts and work rotation schedules that go in the direction of the sun (morning, afternoon, night), to reduce the negative effects of fatigue.

    The Client With Vision Challenges

    Poor vision can lead to falls. People may not be able to see the obstacles in their path (such as toys, small rugs, furniture, or electrical cords), or they may not be able to judge distances or depth correctly. They may also have difficulty reading labels on medicines, cleaners, and other containers, which can result in their ingesting poisonous agents.

    Some people who have decreased eyesight might require eyeglasses but refuse to wear them. Others might wear their eyeglasses all the time but not see clearly because they have not kept their prescriptions up to date. As a support worker, you should always ensure that your client’s glasses are properly cleaned (see Chapter 34) and are easy for the client to access. If you note that your client still cannot see properly, report this to your supervisor.

    The Client With Hearing Challenges

    People with impaired hearing have difficulty hearing explanations and instructions or may not hear warning signals or fire alarms. Since they are not alerted to danger, they do not know that they must move to safety. Chapter 34 discusses ways to communicate with people with impaired hearing.

    The Client With Taste, Smell, and Touch Challenges

    Diminished senses of taste and smell may cause someone to eat spoiled food or not be aware of a gas leak. A diminished sense of touch may make a person unable to feel heat and cold, as well as pain and discomfort. For example, Mrs. Gagnon has poor circulation to her legs and feet, and so she does not feel the presence of blisters on her feet that are caused by her new shoes. There is a risk for the blister becoming a serious wound.

    The Client With Mobility Challenges

    Mobility means the ability to move around. Aging, disease, and injury can affect mobility, so some people cannot walk or use wheelchairs. Being unable to move oneself out of danger is a serious safety risk, and even minor changes in mobility increase accident risks. For example, someone with a sore knee may not be able to recover her balance from a stumble and end up falling.

    Medications

    Some medications have adverse effects such as loss of balance, drowsiness, lack of coordination, reduced awareness, confusion, and disorientation, among others. Certain medications can even make people fearful and uncooperative. These effects increase the risk for accidents, so they must be reported to your supervisor.

    Age

    People of all ages who cannot detect potential hazards are at risk for injuries (see Chapters 33, 38, and 43 and the Think About Safety: Risk Factors for Accidents and Falls in Older Persons box). As a support worker, when you are working with or around children, you must always be aware of possible dangers to them. It is your responsibility to always practise safety measures to prevent falls, burns, poisoning, and suffocation. Use common sense and follow your agency’s safety rules and the client’s care plan at all times.     Risk Factors for Accidents and Falls in Older Persons

    One estimate suggests that approximately half of all continuing care clients fall in any 1-year period.
