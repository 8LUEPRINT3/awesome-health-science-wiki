Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS)
The Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) (Bruera
et al., 1991; Watanabe et al., 2011) shown in Figure 4 provides a
“snapshot” of the person’s perception of nine symptoms and
other problems they may be experiencing. It is a practical
tool that enables a rapid self-evaluation of symptoms, using
a horizontal visual scale from 0 to 10, in which 0 means the
symptom is absent, 1 means the symptom causes minimal
distress, and 10 means the symptom is the worst possible.
While the tool was developed originally for use with people
being treated for cancer, it is currently used across Can-
ada and the United States for people with any life-limiting
illnesses.
Instructions: The person experiencing symptoms needs to
provide answers to the questions in the ESAS, but the family
or an HCP can record the answers. The person can use the
body map in the ESAS to indicate where they feel the dis-
comfort or the symptom. When checking for changes in a
symptom, for example, after a medication change, it is help-
ful if the time between evaluations is consistent. It is also
helpful if the person considers the time when answering the
questions (e.g., the present moment, the last 8 hours, the
last 24 hours). The ESAS cannot be used when a person is no
longer able to self-evaluate.
When the person indicates that a symptom has worsened
since the previous evaluation or that a new symptom has
developed, share this information with the nurse or team. A
team member can follow up with a thorough assessment.

The word “adjuvant” means “helpful” or “a helpful person or

pose to treat one problem (e.g., depression, seizures) but
also relieve pain, as a secondary function. Below are some
examples of adjuvant medications:
• Steroid medications (e.g., prednisone, dexamethasone)
decrease inflammation and swelling around a tumour.
This relieves pressure on tissues and consequently
decreases pain. Steroids can prevent or decrease
post-radiation pain caused by swelling in the radiated
area, or headache from brain tumours.
• Anticonvulsant medications (e.g., gabapentin) work
by decreasing the excitability of nerves and thereby
decreasing the number of pain signals sent to the
brain. They are often used to decrease neuropathic
(nerve) pain.
• Antispasmodics (e.g., atropine) relieve pain by decreas-
ing muscle spasms. These medications are useful for
bladder spasms or intestinal spasms.

Fear of Being Judged
In some cultures, being stoic when experiencing pain is
an important characteristic. This may lead some people to
refuse pain medications, including opioids, to avoid being
judged as “weak” when they experience significant pain
(Narayan, 2010). Other people may fear they will not receive
appropriate pain relief with opioids because of their hist-
ory of drug or opioid addiction, the effects of stereotypes,
or past interactions in health care where individuals were
labelled as “drug seeking.” PSWs who address their personal
feelings and avoid judging will be prepared to provide cul-
turally safe, unbiased palliative care for the dying person and
family.

Rehydration
In some situations, dehydration can be treated by rehy-
drating the person, either through intravenous (IV) fluids or
by a less invasive procedure wherein fluids from an IV bag
enter the fatty tissue under the skin through a needle (hypo-
dermoclysis). This fluid is absorbed slowly into the circula-
tory system and distributed throughout the body. Fatty
open areas, such as the abdomen, thigh, upper back, or arm,
are used for this type of rehydration; however, the location
of the needle that delivers the fluid is determined by the
person’s comfort. This subcutaneous route, which is easier to
set up and less invasive than the IV route, may be preferred.

In order for me (the brain) to work, the rest of my body parts and
organs have to work. If they don’t do their job, then I can’t do
mine! When my body does not work well, then I get confused: I
can’t find the right words, pay attention, make clear decisions,
remember things, or sort out what is past, present, or future!
Sometimes I hallucinate and often I misunderstand. And worst
of all, no one seems to understand me. Sometimes I am sleepy
when I am supposed to be awake, and other times I am awake
when everyone else is trying to sleep. Sometimes I get agitated
and upset, and other times I am just sleepy and wonder what
the fuss is all around me. Sometimes I see things that no one else
sees. Sometimes the things I see make sense (like the time I mis-
took the nurse for my daughter, or the time I thought the bells
were the fire alarm when my house burned down), but other
times, the images that I see are scary, and I am scared.
