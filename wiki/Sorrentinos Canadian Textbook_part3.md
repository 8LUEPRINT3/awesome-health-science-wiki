### Decision Making in Different Health Care Settings

As a support worker, you will face similar kinds of decisions in most settings, even though some differences exist between facilities and private homes. In a facility, you will care for several clients and also assist nurses, as needed. Sometimes you have to decide which person’s needs are to be met first. For example, you see a client shouting angrily at her roommate. Another client needs to be shaved. You need to decide who should be helped first.

If working in home care, you must plan your time so that you can be punctual for the next client. Since your supervisor is not on site, you also have to make decisions on your own.

## Problem Solving

Problem solving is a process that requires identifying and analyzing a problem, finding a solution, and devising a plan to apply that solution.

### Identify the Problem

You must first determine if you have a problem and what it is. Ask yourself the following questions:

1. • Is the situation or issue affecting you, a co-worker, your supervisor, or one of your clients?
2. • Should you be concerned about the situation?
3. • Can you influence or contribute to a positive outcome?
4. • Does the issue require immediate attention?

Consider the following two examples:

* 1. Miles helps Mr. Rossi, 85, get dressed in the mornings. Most days, Mr. Rossi chooses to wear the same tattered sweater. Miles is tired of seeing it on him. He knows that Mr. Rossi has other sweaters. However, when Miles considers the above questions, he answers no to each. He knows that the sweater is clean, that Mr. Rossi enjoys wearing it, and that he has the right to choose what he wears. Miles decides that this situation is not a problem.
* 2. Cheryl assists Mr. McDonald, 88, with lunch in the dining room of a long-term care facility. He chooses tomato soup for lunch. After one spoonful, he refuses to eat. Cheryl is concerned. She knows that if Mr. McDonald does not eat, he tends to get dizzy and may fall. She knows this situation is a problem that requires her immediate attention.

#### Analyze the Problem

Once you know you have a problem, think about what kind of problem it is. Decide if it is one that you can solve on your own. Consult the assignment sheet or care plan to make sure you know what is expected of you. Remember to consult your supervisor when:

1. • There is an emergency
2. • You observe a change in the client’s condition or normal functioning
3. • The client becomes ill—for example, the client vomits, has diarrhea, or develops a fever
4. • The client is in distress
5. • You believe the client’s safety is at risk
6. • A problem involving medications exists
7. • The client complains about their condition or care
8. • The client asks you a question about their diagnosis, condition, or treatment plans
9. • The client or family member asks you to do something that goes against the care plan
10. • You have a conflict with a client or family member
11. • A question or problem arises and you need help to deal with it

Your supervisor is always available to provide guidance and help solve problems. Even in a community setting, your supervisor is just a phone call away.

Analyzing a problem involves communication. Ask the client questions about the problem, and listen attentively to the answers. Remember to pay attention to verbal as well as nonverbal messages (see the [Supporting Mrs. Kao: Asking, Listening, and Observing](#b0070) box). Do not make assumptions about the cause of a problem.

For instance, in example 2 above, when Cheryl asks Mr. McDonald why he does not want to eat, he says that he has a sore on the inside of his cheek where he bit himself the other day. She can tell by his expression that his mouth hurts. Cheryl knows that Mr. McDonald’s care plan does not specify a special diet. He is able to eat anything from the available menu. She therefore decides to try solving the problem by urging him to select softer foods until his mouth heals. She should notify her supervisor of her observations, as well as report and record Mr. McDonald’s complaints. She should also notify her supervisor if his discomfort increases.

### Find a Solution

Think of as many solutions as you can. Decide which is the most practical and helpful, but always be sure that it is safe. Try the solution to see if it works. For example, Cheryl thinks that the tomato soup may be too hot and acidic for Mr. McDonald’s sore mouth. She suggests he try a cooler, blander meal. He chooses macaroni and cheese. He is able to eat it without discomfort. Cheryl later reports to her supervisor what she did to try to remedy Mr. McDonald’s sore in his mouth and whether it was effective. She also needs to document this information in his chart (see [Chapter 26](../B9780323709392000262/B9780323709392000262.xhtml)).

You may occasionally experience conflict with clients or their families or with your co-workers or supervisors. In dealing with any conflict, remember that caring for the client’s needs is always your first priority.

It is important for you to identify your usual response to conflict.

1. • Do you wait and avoid dealing with the problem, hoping it will go away?
2. • Are you agreeable and nonassertive even at the expense of your personal work ethics?
3. • Do you feel you must win at any cost?

Remember that clients and family members can respond differently to conflict. In a conflict situation, you need to step back and think about how you, your client, or a client’s family member is responding to the conflict and talk to your supervisor about how to resolve it.

Conflict between you and a client could occur if a client is too tired, overstimulated, confused, or having difficulty communicating. For example, Mrs. Jones had been out to a doctor’s appointment in the morning and is refusing to eat her lunch. You know it is important that she eat something. If you do not recognize that Mrs. Jones is too tired and you persist in trying to get her to eat, the conflict could escalate. Perhaps you should allow Mrs. Jones to rest first and eat later, and you should report this decision to your supervisor. Report all conflicts with clients to your supervisor, including ones that you have resolved. You can prevent conflict from escalating by remaining calm and respectful, understanding the client’s needs and feelings, and recognizing the reason for the client’s behaviour.

Conflict can also occur between you and the client’s family members. It is important to remember that in most cases, they are only trying to ensure the best care for their family member who is ill. The conflict usually occurs because of a failure to understand the nursing care plan, the reasons for a change in the client’s condition or treatment, or the policies of the facility or agency. You should listen to the family’s concerns in a calm, nonjudgemental way. If your explanation does not satisfy them, contact your supervisor to ensure that the situation does not worsen. Even if you have made the situation better, you must still report the family members’ concerns to your supervisor.

Conflict between co-workers can have a negative effect on the care of clients. Unresolved conflict causes stress and hinders communication and teamwork. To some extent, such conflict is unavoidable since you are not always going to agree with your supervisors or team members. Applying some of the principles outlined in [BOX 8.7](#b0040) can help you resolve conflicts. You do not need to report conflicts with co-workers if they have been resolved. However, if you cannot resolve a conflict, discuss it with your supervisor. Communication and work ethics are essential in preventing and resolving conflicts. Identify and solve problems before they become major issues.

Different People Deal With Conflict in Different Ways

Differences in communicating may be influenced by a person’s culture, an individual’s discomfort in dealing with conflict, or the way the person’s family dealt with conflict. Some people value an indirect method of communication for reasons of face-saving, harmony, or long-term gains versus short-term gains.

At all times, when dealing with clients, families, co-workers, and supervisors, keep the following in mind:

1. • Depending on the culture, family, or specific situation, some people may simply deny that a problem exists (or may downplay the problem).
2. • Taking action could cause displeasure, so some people may choose not to address the conflict.
3. • Some may choose to blame others for the problem.
4. • Some people may not respond verbally at all.

Talk to your supervisor, who may have more knowledge of the background of your client or co-worker and may provide information and guidance.

BOX 8.7 Managing Conflict

1. • Approach the person with whom you have a conflict, and ask to talk with them privately.
2. • Be polite and professional in your approach.
3. • Agree on a time and place to talk.
4. • Talk in a private setting. Others should not see or hear you and another person having what might seem like an argument.
5. • Explain the problem and what is bothering you. Give facts, and describe specific behaviours. Focus on the problem, not on the person. For example, say, “I need to know when you cannot help me so that I can make other plans.” Avoid criticizing the person—for example, saying, “You are always late and never call to let me know.”
6. • Listen to the person’s response. Do not interrupt.
7. • Identify ways to resolve the problem. Offer your own thoughts, and ask for the other person’s ideas.
8. • Schedule a date and a time to review the situation.
9. • Thank the person for meeting with you.
10. • Implement the solutions.
11. • Review the situation, as needed.
12. • If you are unable to resolve the conflict, ask your supervisor for some time to talk privately. Explain the situation, and ask for advice in solving the problem. Give facts and specific examples.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• Stress is the emotional, behavioural, or physical response to an event or situation.

• Stress can affect all aspects of a person’s life, including physical, emotional, cognitive, social, and spiritual aspects.

• As a support worker, you need to recognize the signs of stress in yourself, your clients, and their families.

• Some common stressors affect us all: change, pressure, lack of control, conflict, school, and daily frustrations.

• Ways to manage stress include developing self-awareness, taking care of one’s own needs, thinking positively, asserting oneself, asking others for help and support, practising calming exercises, and learning to accept the things that cannot be changed.

• We all have defence mechanisms to deal with stress (see [Chapters 9](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml) and [37](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)).

• Setting SMART goals helps with managing time and stress. Goals should be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, and Timely.

• Support workers make many decisions each day so must be focused, flexible, and decisive.

• As a support worker, you will need to be able to identify problems that you can solve yourself and ones you need your supervisor’s help to solve. When faced with a problem, identify the problem, analyze the problem, find a solution, and then devise a plan.

• Conflict can occur between you and your co-workers, clients, or clients’ families or within your own family. You need to be aware of how to deal with conflict in any of these situations.

Through your communication with clients, you find out about their needs, feelings, likes, and dislikes and also express your own thoughts and ideas. ## Factors that Influence Communication

Interpersonal communication is the exchange of information between two people, usually face to face. A message is sent by one person (the sender) and is received and interpreted by another person (the receiver). Often, the receiver provides information in response to the message (feedback). During the exchange of information, each person usually acts as both a sender and a receiver.

Successful interpersonal communication occurs when the receiver understands the meaning of the message. However, sometimes the receiver does not interpret the message in the way the sender intended, and mistakes and hurt feelings can occur. At times, the sender may not be aware that the receiver does not understand the message as intended. As well, there is a possibility that the receiver has hearing difficulty or a cognitive problem. The relationship between the sender and the receiver also affects their communication. Communication is easiest when the people involved understand and respect each other ([FIGURE 9.1](#f0010)). Numerous factors can influence both the communication between members of the health care team, clients, and their family members and the interpretation of the message, such as the following:

1. •
   Perceptions.
    Perceptions are how a person views events and understands messages. They are based on an individual’s culture, education, and personal background. As a support worker, you need to understand that your client’s perceptions may be very different from yours and may require you to adjust your communication style.

## Verbal Communication

In verbal communication, messages are sent through the spoken word. Sometimes, symbols substitute for
spoken words; for example, sign language is used to converse with a person who cannot hear.

To effectively communicate with words, you need to do the following:

1. •
   Choose your words carefully.
    Words must have the same meaning for both you and the other person. Try to avoid words with more than one meaning. For example, the words small, moderate, and large mean different things to different people. Is “small” the size of a pea or the size of a walnut? Instead, use words that are specific and descriptive: Telling your supervisor that a client’s temperature is 37.9°C is clearer than saying, “His temperature is up.”
2. •
   Be aware that emotions can affect your communication.
    A person who is angry or upset can unintentionally convey these feelings when communicating with others. This display of emotion may, in turn, confuse or upset the listener, who is not aware of the background context of why you are communicating in such a manner. It is important to maintain a positive attitude at all times when speaking to clients, their families, or co-workers.
3. •
   Use simple, everyday language.
    You will become familiar with medical terminology as you study and gain experience in health care. However, do not use these medical terms when communicating with clients and their families because these terms may be unfamiliar to them. Also, use correct grammar, and avoid vulgar words and slang. Be aware that culture may influence people’s choice of words and how they use them. By using certain words, you may insult or confuse a person from another culture.
4. •
   Speak clearly, slowly, and distinctly.
    Do not mumble or speak quickly. Move your lips as you speak, slow down your speech, and pause between sentences. Do not, however, slow down your speech so much that your client feels embarrassed by it. Ensure the room in which you are communicating is well lit and quiet, so the client can see your facial expressions and body mannerisms. Do not shout or overexaggerate words; doing so will distort your speech.
5. •
   Use visual clues.
    Write your message down for clients who cannot hear but can read, and use objects or pictures to help them understand. For example, show the client the meals they can choose from.
6. ![Close-up of a woman's face shouting while talking on phone.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000092/main.assets/f09-03-9780323711654.jpg)

   FIGURE 9.3 Be aware that the tone of your voice (such as shouting) can suggest irritation or anger with the receiver, such as the woman in this picture.
   iStockphoto/airdone.
7. •
   Control the volume and tone of your voice.
    How your voice sounds sends a message. Do not shout, because shouting can suggest irritation or anger ([FIGURE 9.3](#f0020)). Similarly, do not talk in a harsh or abrupt manner. As well, avoid speaking to adults in high-pitched tones as you might when speaking to children.
8. •
   Be brief and concise.
    Do not add unrelated or unnecessary information. Focus on what you are saying, stay on the subject, and do not use too many words. Instructions that are too wordy may confuse the client and may reduce the possibility of the client understanding them. Speak in short sentences to emphasize your words, as short sentences are more clearly understood.
9. •
   Present information in a logical manner.
    Organize your thoughts before you speak, and present them in sequence (in the right order). Think about what has happened, or what is going to happen, step by step.
10. •
    Ask one question at a time.
     Give the client time to answer each question you ask, and do not rush them. Avoid providing the answer for the client.
11. •
    Determine understanding.
     Do not assume that the client understands what you are saying. Ask the client to repeat the message in their own words.
12. •
    Do not pretend to understand.
     If you do not understand what the client has said, ask them to restate or rephrase the message. Repeat the message, if needed, to indicate that you have understood it.

### Humour

Humour is an important, but often underused, technique in communicating with clients and co-workers. Research has shown that laughter shared among clients, family members, and the support worker can reduce tension, increase trust, and promote bonding. When caring for clients whose first language is not the same as your own, however, you may not be able to use verbal humour, because it may be misunderstood or misinterpreted. Gauge whether the use of humour would be both comfortable for you as a support worker and appropriate for your client.

## Nonverbal Communication

In nonverbal communication, messages are sent without words through body language, touch, and the use of silence. The meaning and interpretation of messages sent through nonverbal communication varies, depending on both the sender’s and receiver’s age, gender, life experiences, and culture (see [Chapter 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml)). Using active listening skills to observe your clients’ body language can help you understand them better. Active listening is a nonjudgemental communication technique that focuses not only on understanding the content of what is being said but also on the underlying emotions and feelings conveyed by the sender. A person listens actively by concentrating fully on what is being said and paying attention to the client’s nonverbal cues, as well as by conveying interest in the conversation by nodding, making eye contact, and responding when appropriate. This technique helps to develop rapport and fosters a trusting relationship. For example, a slumped posture and a slow, shuffling walk may indicate that a client is not happy or is not feeling well. Sometimes, clients may say they feel fine, but their facial expressions may indicate that they are in pain. Nonverbal clues often reflect a person’s true feelings, and because they are usually involuntary and unconscious, they may send messages more accurately than words can (see the [Supporting Mr. Reyes: Verbal and Nonverbal Behaviours Send Differing Messages](#b0030) box).

### Body Language

Body language includes the following:

1. • Posture
2. • Appearance (dress, hygiene, and adornments such as jewellery, perfume, visible tattoos, the presence of many piercings, and obvious use of cosmetics)
3. • Facial expressions
4. • Body movements
5. • Eye contact
6. • Gestures

Body language greatly affects communication and can change the meaning of a verbal message. For example, someone can say, “Yes, I can do that” while either smiling in a friendly manner or rolling their eyes and sighing. In both cases, the body language sends a message. A person who says one thing with words but another with body language is sending mixed messages, which are confusing and unhelpful.

Just as you can learn from your client’s body language, you need to be aware of the messages you send with your appearance and body language. Your facial expressions and how you stand, sit, walk, and look at a person all send messages. Your body language should show interest and enthusiasm for your work and caring and respect for your clients. For example, show respect to a client who is in a bed or wheelchair
by sitting or squatting so that you are at eye level. Your general appearance also communicates information. Some clients and their families may assume that you will provide poor care if you show up in a wrinkled or soiled uniform. Visible tattoos, especially with images such as skulls, dragons, or weapons, and body piercings may startle, frighten, or intimidate some clients.

In some situations, you may need to consciously control your body language when providing care to clients. Do not react visibly, for example, to body odours; often such odours are beyond the control of clients. Your reaction is likely to increase a client’s embarrassment and humiliation.

### Touch

Touch is a very important form of nonverbal communication. It can convey warmth, comfort, concern, affection, trust, and reassurance. For example, holding a person’s hand can provide comfort, and gently stroking a person’s shoulder or back can promote rest and relaxation. The use of touch is very personal. While most people respond well to a touch on their hand because it helps them feel less lonely, not everyone would feel comfortable being hugged or patted on the head. Your touch should be gentle, not hurried or rushed, and should be restricted to “public” areas of the body, such as the hands or shoulders. It is a good rule to ask permission from a client to hug them. Your employer and the care plan will direct your care.

Touch can mean different things to different people. Pulling away or tensing of the body may indicate that the client does not want to be touched because they are in pain and it hurts to be touched. Or they may be uncomfortable with touch because of their cultural background. For some people who have experienced torture, spousal or child abuse (physical, sexual, or both), rape, or bullying, any touching from another person may be beyond uncomfortable.
It is very important that you ask your clients for permission each time you touch their body or possessions.

### Silence

The use of silence can convey messages of acceptance, rejection, fear, or the need for quiet and time to think. As a support worker, you may find it difficult to accept silence when you want to comfort your client. Sometimes, however, especially during sad times, you do not need to say anything; just being there shows that you care. Silence can give you and others time to organize thoughts and choose words. It is also useful when the client is making difficult decisions or is upset and is trying to regain control. In these situations, silence on your part shows respect and empathy for the client (see the [Case Study: Silence and Touch During Sad Times](#b0035) box).

## Communication Methods

Certain communication skills help you share information more effectively with others and result in better relationships with people.

### Active Listening

As discussed on page 134, active listening means paying close attention to a client’s verbal and nonverbal communication—listening to the content, the intent, and the feelings behind the words. Remember, nonverbal clues may reveal the client’s true feelings. For example, Mrs. Gorecki tells you that her knees do not hurt today. However, you observe that she is rubbing her knees and grimacing. Her nonverbal behaviour indicates that she is in pain.

Active listening requires you to be interested in your client and to show that you care. The following are guidelines for active listening:

1. •
   Face the client.
    Your client will often need to see your facial expressions and body language to be assured that you are listening.
2. •
   Make eye contact.
    However, do consider cultural preferences in regard to eye contact.
3. •
   Lean toward the client.
    Do not sit back with your arms crossed. Crossed arms can be interpreted as an indication that you are not open to what the client is telling you.
4. •
   Respond to your client.
    Note, however, that sometimes your client just may not want to talk or may not respond to your normal communication style.
5. •
   Avoid communication barriers.
    See page 139.

### Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing is restating another person’s message in one’s own words. A paraphrase usually has fewer words than the original message. Paraphrasing serves three purposes:

* 1. It shows that you are listening.
* 2. It lets both you and the sender know that you understood the message.
* 3. It promotes further communication.

People usually respond well to a paraphrased statement. For example:

* Mrs. Cummings: I was a keen reader when I could see. I miss books so much. Those talking books are hard to follow.
* You: You love stories, but talking books are not as good as real books.
* Mrs. Cummings: Exactly. I wish you had time to read to me.

When paraphrasing, try not to interpret the client’s words. Guide the conversation in such a way that the client feels comfortable expressing thoughts or feelings. If you misinterpret a client’s meaning, you could put an end to the conversation or cause offence (see the [Case Study: Using Paraphrasing and Questioning Skills](#b0040) box for an example of effective paraphrasing).

### Empathetic Listening

Empathetic listening requires being attentive to the speaker’s feelings. Empathy means being open to and trying to understand the experiences and feelings of others. It involves acknowledging the other person’s point of view without judging. Clients need to know that they are understood. Empathy can help reduce feelings of loneliness and sadness and can create bonds of trust between a support worker and the clients they support.

When paraphrasing, you acknowledge the other person’s words. When empathizing, you acknowledge their feelings. To show empathy, follow the person’s lead. While they speak, listen quietly. Do not rush them or change the subject. Stay focused on them and not on your own opinions. For example, if the client mentions a difficult situation, you could say, “I can see you are upset. Do you want to talk about it?” This comment shows that you recognize and care about how the person feels.

Avoid quick, thoughtless responses such as the following:

1. • “I know how you feel.” (Nobody can ever know how another person feels.)
2. • “I feel sorry for you.” (This implies pity.)
3. • “I wouldn’t want to be in your shoes.” (This suggests superiority and implies pity.)

### Asking Closed Questions

Closed-ended questions focus on specific information, so use them when you need to learn something precise. Some closed questions require a “yes” or “no” answer. Others require a brief response. For example:

* You: Would you like butter on your toast this morning, Mrs. Cummings?
* Mrs. Cummings: Yes, please.
* You: Would you like strawberry jam or marmalade?
* Mrs. Cummings: Marmalade, please.

### Asking Open-Ended Questions

Open-ended questions invite a person to share thoughts, feelings, or ideas. Answers must be more than a “yes” or “no.” However, the person being questioned chooses and controls what is talked about and the information given. Consider these questions: “What was it like growing up in Scotland, Mrs. Cummings?” (open-ended question) and “Did you like living in Scotland?” (closed question). The first question encourages Mrs. Cummings to talk about herself. It shows her that you are interested in hearing about her life. The second question requires a “yes” or “no” answer. It does not encourage Mrs. Cummings to talk about herself, nor does it communicate as much interest in her life.

Use open-ended questions in combination with closed questions to find out about a client’s needs and preferences and to find out if a client is satisfied with your care. For example, a closed question (“Are you comfortable?”) can give you the necessary information. An open-ended question (“Is there anything I can do to make you more comfortable?”) can encourage a client to express thoughts or feelings. The [Case Study: Using Skills of Paraphrasing and Questioning](#b0040) box shows an example of a support worker using both types of questions to improve a client’s care.

### Clarifying

Clarifying helps you make sure that you have understood a person’s message. You can ask the person to repeat the message, say that you do not understand, or restate the message as a question. For example:

1. • “Could you say that again?”
2. • “I’m sorry, Mr. Hart. I don’t understand what you mean.”
3. • “Are you saying that you want to go home?”

### Focusing

Focusing is limiting the conversation to a certain topic. It is useful when a client rambles or wanders in thought. Consider these examples:

1. • Mr. Reyes talks at length about his favourite foods and restaurants. You need to know why he did not feel like eating dinner. You focus the conversation on the subject of dinner by saying, “Let’s talk about today’s dinner. You said you didn’t feel like eating.”
2. • The care plan for Mrs. Hooda directs you to provide two choices when helping her dress. She becomes distracted by the pattern on one of the dresses. You guide the conversation back to the task of dressing by saying, “Would you like to wear the dress with the pretty pattern?”
3. • Mrs. Cummings has just told you that she does not want to go for a walk. She then reminisces about her early life. Your response encourages her to focus on her reason for not wanting to walk:Mrs. Cummings: We used to walk for miles in the Lake District. It was usually raining. It rained constantly in Edinburgh, too.You: There is no rain today, and the sun is shining. Let’s go for a walk and not get wet.

## Professional Communication

Support workers are valued members of the health care team that delivers necessary care to clients. It is therefore important that they act professionally at all times. An important part of professionalism involves professional communication. Avoiding use of slang terms and swear words and using professional behaviours and body language in the workplace are essential. Workplace etiquette requires people to behave in a professional, ethical, friendly, and respectful manner to all staff and co-workers at all times, including while on breaks as well as, of course, at team conferences and when reporting in at the start and end of shifts (see [Chapter 10](../B9780323709392000109/B9780323709392000109.xhtml)). Professional etiquette also requires workers who normally speak a language other than English (or French, if the agency conducts care in French) to speak English in the workplace at all times, even when speaking to a co-worker who uses the same language. It is seen as disrespectful to use a language that excludes others in the room even if not talking directly to them.

Pay particular attention to the way you speak to co-workers. Many times, problems can erupt between co-workers when one person responds to the other and that response is interpreted as being made in a rude, irritated, or hostile manner. It is also important to remember to refrain from using slang expressions or offensive language, even when speaking to co-workers. Remember that it is important to act and speak professionally at all times while working.

Examples of breaches of workplace etiquette include gossiping about clients or other team members either verbally or through the Internet; breaching confidentiality; avoiding eye contact; making nonsupportive facial expressions such as eye-rolling; or speaking a language that others cannot understand. If you are on your break and need to use your cell phone to speak to or text someone, do so in private. It is important to be polite and respectful of co-workers during working hours, as well as on breaks. Another breach of workplace etiquette is bullying (also sometimes called horizontal violence) (see [Chapter 12](../B9780323709392000122/B9780323709392000122.xhtml)).

Even outside the workplace, people who work in health care, as well as those in many other public-service fields, have to be careful in their communications. For example, it will be increasingly important for you, as a professional, to become more selective about what you post on social media sites. Employers may visit sites when considering you for employment (see [Chapter 13](../B9780323709392000134/B9780323709392000134.xhtml)). As well, breaches of confidentiality, whether spoken or written, and undesirable behaviours have a way of surfacing and may negatively affect a person’s employment status.

When communicating with clients, their families, and the health care team, it is important to remain professional and to understand the difference between a professional relationship and a friendship. A friendship is a voluntary and informal bond between two individuals who share intimacies and seek to spend time together. In contrast, a professional relationship is essentially an assignment. Clients may start to consider support workers their friends as they share their thoughts and concerns. As a professional support worker, however, you should not share your thoughts, personal information, or worries with your client.

Other ways of showing professionalism and courtesy to clients include knocking on closed doors before entering, calling people by their names, and saying “please” and “thank you.” You should always introduce yourself to the client before taking any action and explain what you will be doing. Initially, it is respectful to address clients by a courtesy title (e.g., Mr., Ms., Mrs.) and their last name until they ask you to call them by their first name. Using terms such as “honey,” “dear,” or “sweetheart” is not appropriate and is disrespectful to the client. Some clients would prefer that you address them in a gender-neutral manner or with a preferred nickname, and the support worker should be respectful of this by addressing clients in the way they have requested.

### Confidentiality

Ensuring confidentiality is an important part of professional communication. In the course of your work, you will learn private and sensitive information about your clients, and this information should be protected. Only pertinent information should be shared with other team members providing direct care to the client.

Observing the need for confidentiality includes choosing where and when to discuss a client. For example, discussing a client with staff who are not involved in that client’s care is breaching confidentiality. In addition, discussing clients in stairwells, in a lunchroom, or in the parking lot are examples of confidentiality breaches. Confidentiality must extend to your personal time. It is inappropriate, for example, to post pictures of clients or to discuss clients on a social media site or in a personal conversation, even if you avoid using their names.

## Communication Barriers

Communication barriers prevent the sending and receiving of messages, limiting communication or causing it to fail completely. Some barriers cannot be avoided, so they must be worked around. For example, some clients have hearing and vision problems that interfere with communication, and some clients have nervous system disorders that limit communication. As a support worker, you must learn special techniques to communicate effectively in these situations ([FIGURE 9.4](#f0025); also see [Chapters 34](../B9780323709392000341/B9780323709392000341.xhtml) and [35](../B9780323709392000353/B9780323709392000353.xhtml)). Cultural differences can also interfere with communication since clients may attach different meanings to verbal and nonverbal communication (see [Chapter 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml)). Other barriers include factors in the environment, such as loud noises, lack of privacy, and distractions. As much as possible, try to ensure a calm, quiet setting when talking with a client.

Certain behaviours can also create communication barriers. Improve communication with clients by avoiding the following: interrupting, answering your own questions, giving advice, minimizing problems, using patronizing language, and failing to listen.

### Interrupting

Interrupting a person stops communication. People usually interrupt others when they:

1. • Jump to conclusions about what the speaker is trying to say
2. • Become impatient with the speaker or the way the story is being told
3. • Become bored and wish to change the subject to something more interesting
4. • Wish to change the subject because the topic is upsetting
5. • Feel hurried or stressed
6. • Are focused on a task, not on the person

### Answering Your Own Questions

Avoid answering your own questions. Some people do this in any conversation they have, and others do it only with people who take a long time to respond. Answering questions or completing thoughts for people discourages openness. Note the following different responses to the same question, phrased slightly differently:

* You: How did you sleep last night? Okay? (answer provided)
* Mrs. Cummings: Yes.
* You: How did you sleep last night? (answer not provided)
* Mrs. Cummings: I was pretty restless. It took me a long time to fall asleep. The last time I looked at the clock, it was 3:00 a.m.

### Giving Advice

Avoid giving advice to clients and their family members. Let people express their feelings and concerns without offering your opinion. You could create confusion, anxiety, and resentment. Your advice could go against the family’s wishes, the physician’s orders, or the care plan. Even if a client asks for your advice, do not give it. You could instead suggest that the client speak to your supervisor or the case manager. In the following example, the support worker tactfully avoids giving advice to Mrs. Van Doorne:

* Mrs. Van Doorne: I don’t feel ready to leave my home, but I’m too much of a burden on my daughter. I just don’t know what to do. Sometimes I feel that we’d all be better off if I moved into a nursing home. At other times I hate the thought of it. What do you think I should do?
* Support worker: I can see what a difficult decision it is, Mrs. Van Doorne. I wish I could help, but it’s not my role to give you advice. Is there anyone else you can talk to about this?
* Mrs. Van Doorne: I’ve tried to talk to Anne (her daughter), but she would never admit that I’m a burden.
* Support worker: What about talking to Mrs. Stainer (the case manager)? I’m sure she could help.
* Mrs. Van Doorne: That’s a good idea. I’ll do that.

### Minimizing Problems

Do not minimize a client’s problems. Avoid making comments like these: “Everything will be fine,” “Don’t worry—it’s really not that bad,” “Look on the bright side,” and “It could be worse.” These comments block communication and imply that the client is complaining or exaggerating the problem. They also show that you are judging the client or the situation when you have no right to do so. Minimizing problems makes people feel that you are ridiculing their concerns, feelings, and fears. Clients could believe that you do not care about what they think or feel. Consider these two responses to a hospital client’s concerns:

* Mr. Lam: I’m so nervous about this operation. I’ve never even been in a hospital before.
* Eduardo: Believe me, you have nothing to worry about. These surgeons could do this operation with their eyes closed. You will be just fine. (Walks away.)
* Helga: Having surgery is frightening, especially when it’s your first operation. The doctors and nurses will explain everything to you so that you know what to expect. (Reports Mr. Lam’s concerns immediately to the nurse, who reassures him about the surgery.)

Eduardo’s response minimizes Mr. Lam’s worries about his surgery, whereas Helga’s response is
empathetic. She uses paraphrasing to let Mr. Lam know that she understands his concerns and also reassures him by expressing confidence in the health care team.

### Failing to Listen

Communication is blocked if you fail to listen with interest and sincerity ([FIGURE 9.5](#f0030)). Pretending to listen is often obvious to the speaker and conveys a lack of interest and caring. Equally problematic, not listening means you can miss important complaints of pain, discomfort, or other abnormal sensations that must be reported to your supervisor.

### Defence Mechanisms

Defence mechanisms are used subconsciously in communications with others in order to delay—or avoid altogether—uncomfortable feelings such as anxiety, guilt, stress, or embarrassment.[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur93),[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur94) Defence mechanisms may be used for a short term or long term and may be used in positive ways or negative ways as a coping means. For example, a client who is awaiting a big surgical procedure may use denial and tell others that “everything will be all right” in order to reduce his own presurgery anxiety. In this case,
the client has used denial in a positive way. However, in another situation, someone may use denial to tell her family that she “is not a problem drinker.” In this case, her denial may lead to unhealthy lifestyle choices and interfere with her acceptance of health teaching from her family and friends.

As a support worker, you may experience difficulty when trying to communicate effectively with a client who is using a defence mechanism instead of accepting reality or facing a painful truth. Some examples of defence mechanisms are the following:[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur94)

1. • Denial: An unwillingness to accept the truth or a certain reality. For example, a person with a terminal illness refuses to believe that he is ill.
2. • Displacement: A transfer of feelings (usually of anger) onto another person or item instead of onto the person who is the source of the anger. For example, a woman who is abused by her partner abuses her children instead of dealing with the conflict between her and her partner.
3. • Fantasizing: Daydreaming as an escape instead of dealing with reality. For example, a person who wishes to be a singer one day daydreams about his future singing successes instead of practising his singing.
4. • Passive-aggressiveness: The practice of expressing negative emotions, resentment, or anger toward others through passive, indirect ways (such as through procrastination or manipulation) instead of by verbalizing feelings in an assertive manner. For example, a student who is not happy with the role her project group assigns her submits her part of the project late, affecting the rest of her team’s grades.
5. • Projection: Blaming someone else for one’s own behaviour. For example, a student blames her bad test marks on her roommate’s noise, when in reality, the student did not prepare adequately for her test.
6. • Rationalization: Justifying a behaviour or action by explaining it as being less harmful or hurtful than it really is. For example, a woman does not admit that her bad cough is a result of her heavy smoking but instead blames it on the dry air produced by the air conditioner.
7. • Regression: A return to an earlier developmental stage as a way to escape from stress. For example, a toddler who was already toilet-trained begins to wet her bed after a new sibling is born.
8. • Repression: The involuntary action of blocking painful memories from the conscious mind. It is not uncommon for people with repressed painful memories to suffer from depression or other mental health disorders. For example, a victim of an assault “forgets” that he was assaulted.
9. • Submissiveness: A show of obedience, meekness, and compliance in the face of conflict or a difference of opinion, instead of assertiveness.
10. • Suppression: A conscious refusal to acknowledge a painful or upsetting situation. For example, a teenager tells her friends that she “doesn’t want to talk about it” after her boyfriend ends their relationship.

## Communicating with Angry People

Anger is a common response of both clients and family members to illness and disability (see [Chapter 6](../B9780323709392000067/B9780323709392000067.xhtml)). The many underlying causes of anger include frustration, anxiety, fear, and pain. Another common cause is hurt feelings—people may react with anger if they feel their self-esteem is being attacked. Loss of body function and of one’s independence can also incite anger. People who are angry are often feeling helpless about a situation.

Anger also is a symptom of diseases that affect thinking and behaviour. For example, people who abuse alcohol or drugs can show anger. Some people are often angry or unhappy, and few things please them. There could be numerous reasons for their demeanour. Do not judge an angry client, but do report a client’s angry behaviour to your supervisor. Provide these clients with the same high-quality, compassionate care that you give all your clients.

Anger can be communicated verbally or nonverbally. Verbal expressions of anger include outbursts, shouting, using a raised voice, and rapid speech. An angry client may tell you what to do or may threaten you. Some clients may remain silent when angry, whereas others become uncooperative and refuse to answer questions. Nonverbal signs of anger include rapid movements, pacing, clenched fists, and a reddened face or neck. The angry client
may glare at you, get too close to you when speaking (see [Chapters 5](../B9780323709392000055/B9780323709392000055.xhtml) and 38), or display violent behaviours. Effective communication is important to prevent and deal with anger. Follow the guidelines in [BOX 9.2](#b0015) when communicating with an angry client.

BOX 9.2 Communicating With an Angry Client

1. • Recognize that the client is feeling frustrated or frightened. Put yourself in the client’s situation. How would you feel? How would you want to be treated?
2. • Treat the client with respect and dignity.
3. • Answer the client’s questions clearly and thoroughly. Tell the client that your supervisor will answer the questions that you cannot answer.
4. • Keep the client informed. Tell the client what you are going to do and when.
5. • Do not keep the client waiting for long periods. If you tell the client that you will do something for them, do it promptly.
6. • Stay calm and professional. Speak in a normal tone. Do not respond to a client’s anger with your own anger. Try not to take the client’s anger personally. The anger has more to do with the client’s own feelings than with you or the care you give.
7. • Do not argue with the client.
8. • Listen and use silence. The client may feel better after expressing angry feelings.
9. • Protect yourself from violent behaviours. Leave the client, and call your supervisor if you think you are in danger (see [Chapter 20](../B9780323709392000201/B9780323709392000201.xhtml)).
10. • Report the client’s behaviour to your supervisor. Discuss how you should deal with the client.
11. • There are courses available to teach you how to deal with angry clients. In most provinces, the course is called “Non-Violent Crisis Intervention.” Other courses may also be available, depending on your area.

## Communicating Assertively

Assertiveness is a style of communication in which thoughts and feelings are expressed positively and directly without offending others. An assertive person stands up for their rights while respecting the rights of others. When being assertive, a speaker conveys their opinion with the goal of ensuring that their needs are met. However, not all people will agree with that opinion, and the speaker must respect others’ right to disagree.

People who communicate assertively appear confident, calm, and composed. They speak gently, firmly, and positively. They do not hesitate or appear anxious. They are respectful.

Being assertive is different from being aggressive and from being passive. A person who communicates aggressively will appear upset, cold, or angry and may sound threatening. Aggressive communication is usually not respectful. A person who communicates passively will appear hesitant, apologetic, and timid. A passive person does not want to hurt or offend others. But passive behaviour can make others feel uncomfortable. Assertiveness rarely has this effect because people usually like direct, honest, and sincere communication.

Some people have trouble communicating assertively with people in authority. They feel intimidated. As a support worker, you will have regular contact with physicians, nurses, and other members of the health care team. You need to be confident and assertive when you communicate with them. Your observations are very important to ensuring that any changes in your client’s condition are addressed. Remember that suggestions you make in a care conference can help improve the care your client receives.

The [Case Study: Communicating Assertively](#b0045) box describes three responses to a situation that requires assertiveness.

* 2. Show the client how to do each step.

## Communicating with Clients Living with Dementia

It is especially important to pay attention to your verbal and nonverbal messages when communicating with people with dementia. As clients lose their language skills, they rely more heavily on following the nonverbal cues of others, especially their caregivers. You can assist clients to maintain their sense of dignity and their identity as people by observing the tips in [BOX 9.4](#b0025).

BOX 9.3 Guidelines for Assisting Clients With Their Tasks

1. •
   Put the client at ease.
    Relax and smile. Do not give the impression that you are in a hurry. If the client senses you are tense or rushed, learning will be difficult.
2. •
   Start with small steps.
    Break the task into small steps. Focus the client’s attention on one step at a time.
3. •
   Start with easy steps.
    Confidence increases with success. If possible, start with the steps the client is most likely to achieve.
4. •
   Observe and listen.
    Clients do not always tell you when they do not understand something. Or they may say they understand it when they actually do not. Watch body language, and listen actively. Be alert for signs of fatigue.
5. •
   Use positive statements. Positive statements are easier to follow than negative statements are. For example, saying “Bend your arm” is more effective than saying “Don’t use a straight arm.”
6. •
   Let the client set the pace. Be patient, and do not rush the client. Allow time for rest.
7. •
   Provide support and offer encouragement.
    Positive comments help the client feel successful and also encourage the client to continue trying. It is important to recognize what the client has achieved. Even small achievements deserve recognition and a positive comment.
8. •
   Give time for practice.
    Allow time for practising a task. Practice helps a client remember.

BOX 9.4 Tips for Communicating With Someone With Dementia

Before You Speak:

1. • When possible, if you are rushing or feeling stressed, try to take a moment to calm yourself.
2. • Consider what you are going to talk about. It may be useful to have an idea for a particular topic ready or to ask yourself what you want to achieve from the conversation.
3. • Make sure you have the person’s full attention.
4. • Make sure that the person can see you clearly.
5. • Try to make eye contact. This will help the person focus on you.
6. • Minimize competing noises, such as the radio, TV, or other people’s conversations.

How to Speak:

1. • Speak clearly and calmly.
2. • Speak at a slightly slower pace, allowing time between sentences for the person to process the information and to respond. This might seem like an uncomfortable pause to you, but it is important for supporting the person to communicate.
3. • Avoid speaking sharply or raising your voice, as this may distress the person.
4. • Use short, simple sentences.
5. • Don’t talk about people with dementia as if they are not there or talk to them as you would to a young child—show respect and patience.
6. • Humour can help to bring you closer together and may relieve the pressure. Try to laugh together about misunderstandings and mistakes—it can help.
7. • Try to include the person in conversations with others. You may find this easier if you adapt the way you say things slightly. Being included in social groups can help people with dementia to preserve their sense of identity. It can also help to reduce feelings of exclusion and isolation.

What to Say:

1. • Try to be positive.
2. • Avoid asking too many direct questions. People with dementia can become frustrated if they can’t find the answer. If you have to, ask questions one at a time, and phrase them in a way that allows for a “yes” or “no” answer.
3. • Try not to ask the person to make complicated decisions. Giving someone a choice is important when they can cope with it, but too many options can be confusing and frustrating.
4. • If the person doesn’t understand what you are saying, try to get the message across in a different way rather than simply repeating the same thing. You could try breaking down complex explanations into smaller parts and perhaps also use written words or objects.
5. • As dementia progresses, the person may become confused about what is true and not true. If the person says something you know to be incorrect, try to find ways of steering the conversation around the subject rather than contradicting them directly. Try to see behind the content to the meaning or feelings they are sharing.

Listening:

1. • Listen carefully to what the person is saying, and give them plenty of encouragement.
2. • When you haven’t understood fully, tell the person what you have understood and check with them to see if you are right.
3. • If the person has difficulty finding the right word or finishing a sentence, ask them to explain it in a different way. Listen for clues. Also pay attention to their body language. The expression on their face and the way they hold themselves and move about can give you clear signals about how they are feeling.
4. • If the person is feeling sad, let them express their feelings without trying to “jolly them along.” Sometimes the best thing to do is to just listen and show that you care.
5. • Due to memory loss, some people won’t remember things such as their medical history, family and friends. You will need to use your judgement and act appropriately around what they’ve said. For example, they might say that they have just eaten when you know they haven’t.

Body Language and Physical Contact:

1. • A person with dementia will read your body language. Sudden movements or a tense facial expression may cause upset or distress and can make communication more difficult.
2. • Make sure that your body language and facial expression match what you are saying.
3. • Never stand too close or stand over someone to communicate: It can feel intimidating. Instead, respect the person’s personal space (see [Chapter 11](../B9780323709392000110/B9780323709392000110.xhtml)) and drop below their eye level. This will help the person to feel more in control of the situation.
4. • Use physical contact to communicate your care and affection and to provide reassurance—don’t underestimate the reassurance you can give by holding or patting the person’s hand or putting your arm around them if it feels right.

Source: Alzheimer’s Society. (2016). Tips: Communicating with someone with dementia. <https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/about-dementia/symptoms-and-diagnosis/symptoms/tips-for-communicating-dementia>.

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• The communication process is the means by which people exchange information. Communication skills are used in all environments, including at work, school, and home.

• Interpersonal communication is the exchange of information between two people, usually face to face.

• Messages sent are sometimes misunderstood by the receiver because factors such as perception, experience, physical and mental health, emotions, values, beliefs, culture, gender, and age can influence understanding.

• To communicate effectively with words, you need to choose your words carefully; use simple, everyday language; speak clearly, slowly, and distinctly; control the volume and tone of your voice; be concise; present information in a logical manner; ask one question at a time; determine understanding; and do not pretend to understand.

• Before starting and while doing a task, explain to the client what you are doing, regardless of how routine the task might feel to you.

• Professional communication requires being courteous and ensuring confidentiality at all times.

• Workplace etiquette requires support workers to behave in a professional, ethical, friendly, and respectful manner to co-workers at all times, including while on breaks.

• Nonverbal communications are messages sent without words through body language, touch, and the use of silence.

• Communication methods include active listening, paraphrasing, empathetic listening, asking closed questions, asking open-ended questions, clarifying, and focusing.

• Communication barriers include interrupting, answering one’s own questions, giving advice, minimizing problems, using patronizing language, and failing to listen.

• People subconsciously use defence mechanisms to avoid uncomfortable feelings such as anxiety, guilt, stress, or embarrassment. Defence mechanisms include denial, displacement, fantasizing, projection, rationalization, regression, repression, and suppression.

• At times, support workers will be faced with angry clients. Effective communication is important to preventing and dealing with anger.

• Assertiveness is a style of communication in which thoughts and feelings are expressed positively and directly without offending others.

• When teaching clients new tasks, describe to the client the steps in the task, show the client how to do each step, have the client try each step, and review the client’s success with each step.

• It is especially important to pay attention to your verbal and nonverbal messages when communicating with people living with dementia. As clients lose their language skills, they rely more heavily on following nonverbal cues.

## The Health Care Team

In most health care settings, support workers work on a team. A team is a group of people who work together toward a common goal (see [Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml)). The goal of a health care team is to provide the client with the best possible care and support. When providing care, team members must consider the whole client and promote health in all five dimensions of the client’s life: physical, emotional, social, cognitive, and spiritual (see [Chapter 6](../B9780323709392000067/B9780323709392000067.xhtml)). Health care team members depend on each other to perform their roles to the best of their abilities. It is also important that all members of the team conduct themselves professionally at all times and use correct terminology when reporting observations about the client (see [Chapter 25](../B9780323709392000250/B9780323709392000250.xhtml)).

### Health Care Team Members

The members of a health care team vary, depending on the place of employment and the client’s needs, and usually include the client, unless they are not mentally capable of being involved in the teamwork or choose not to participate. Depending on the setting in which you work, the makeup of your team may vary considerably. Examples include the following:

1. • You may be responsible for working independently with a supervisor who is not present on-site but is available only through phone calls, a live visual chat application on a phone, or by texting on a mobile phone.
2. • You and one other person who is your supervisor may be the only health care providers on the team who are present at the same time. Your supervisor may be a regulated health professional, such as a nurse, social worker, physiotherapist, or an occupational therapist. In other settings, your supervisor may not be a regulated health care provider at all, but may be the owner of the facility in which you work.
3. • You may be part of a larger team of health care providers from a variety of backgrounds and specialties who work together to meet the client’s needs. Examples of team members include physicians, nurses, support workers, dietitians, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech therapists, and others who are providing alternative care.

### Benefits of Working on a Team

There are many benefits to the team approach in health care. A group of people is more effective at making correct decisions and solving problems than one person is. The many benefits of a team approach to care include the following:

1. •
   Opportunities for collaboration.
    All team members are encouraged to collaborate (to work together toward a common goal). Successful collaboration creates a positive atmosphere that even the client can sense. Staff and clients both benefit when team members share information. For example, as a support worker, you may find a way to ease a client’s discomfort during a bed bath. You then share this information with the nurse and your co-workers in the appropriate manner. The nurse, in turn, instructs other support workers to use your method by incorporating your information and suggestions into the client’s care plan.
2. •
   Opportunities for communication.
    Team meetings provide the opportunity for all team members to share experiences, opinions, and ideas. Without the meetings, valuable ideas might be missed. [BOX 10.1](#b0010) contains part of a dialogue from a team meeting. Note how each team member contributes to the complete picture of the client’s health.
3. •
   Opportunities for mentorship.
    Many newly hired support workers depend on the mentorship of more experienced co-workers to assist them in becoming familiarized with the agency’s clients and routine duties. A mentor is a person who shares their expertise and knowledge to create a safe, encouraging learning environment for the learner. Some people are assigned to be mentors to new employees, but many do so voluntarily.
4. •
   Bringing together a wide range of abilities, skills, and perspectives.
    Teams are made up of individuals who have a range of abilities, skills, training, and experience. Each team member, based on their scope of practice, brings ideas and viewpoints to the team. For example, Tom Brown, 15, has mental health issues. Tom, his parents, a nurse, a psychiatrist, a social worker, and a support worker all work together as a team with the common goal of supporting Tom.
5. Members of effective teams support one another, understand each other’s scope of practice, and communicate with each other effectively. If you work on a team with other health care providers, it may be necessary for you to clarify and advocate what your role is to the team. In [BOX 10.1](#b0010), note the support worker’s contributions. Because she is the only person who has daily contact with Mrs. Darby, she is able to provide important information. The other team members know more about the health and medical conditions that are discussed; however, only the support worker is in a position to report daily observations and basic assessment about the client.
6. •
   Opportunity for better decision making and problem solving.
    Many support workers have been taught how to perform certain tasks by memorizing and following a specific sequence of steps. However, it may be necessary in some circumstances to vary the order of steps while performing a task, as long as the principles of safety, client dignity, and legislation are followed. For example, a co-worker who is assisting you while bathing Mrs. Jones suggests that you change your sequence of bathing steps to reduce the need to turn Mrs. Jones from side to side, something that she does not easily tolerate. When team members discuss issues, they are more likely to make sound decisions and find appropriate solutions to problems.
7. •
   Teamwork can support a positive, trusting atmosphere.
    Trust develops when team members can be relied on to do their jobs well, to respect each other, to mentor each other, and to share responsibility. The team leader or case manager is responsible for fostering a high level of trust. The leader should encourage team members to openly discuss problems. Team members also play a role in creating trust. They must not blame others for their own mistakes and should take responsibility for their own actions. An effective team provides support to each other during difficult emotional situations, such as the death of a client.

Just as there are many benefits to working on a team, there are also challenges:

1. •
   Good teamwork respects role boundaries.
    In successful teams, team members understand one
   another’s role and appreciate and value each person’s role on the team. You will become familiar with tasks that support workers are not permitted to perform. You must be aware of your scope of practice and your employer’s policies and procedures and never take on any task that you are not allowed to perform.
2. •
   Teamwork promotes flexibility.
    Teams function best when members are flexible and willing to meet each other’s needs. For example, you can help ensure quality client care at your agency by agreeing to switch to a different unit when that unit is short-staffed due to staff illness.
3. •
   Teamwork supports conflict resolution.
    Within any group of people, there are bound to be disagreements. The way conflict is handled affects the whole team. The team leader or case manager plays a critical role in the resolution of conflict, and team members should feel comfortable discussing problems with their leader. It is always
   better to address conflict than hope it will go away. (See [Chapter 9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml) for more information on conflict resolution.)

Team functions and members vary from hospital to hospital and department to department. Many departments use a multidisciplinary team approach. ## Working Under Supervision

Support workers have a responsibility to their supervisor, client, and co-workers and are accountable to their supervisor. In facilities and agencies, the supervisor is usually a nurse. In some community care settings, the supervisor may be a nurse, a social worker, or another health care provider. Supervising means being responsible for monitoring and overseeing the activities of others on the health care team.

1. •
   Supervision in a facility.
    In many facilities, the team leader (usually a nurse) supervises the support workers. The team leader has overall responsibility and

   accountability for the client’s care and the work of the other team members. Accountability is the willingness to accept responsibility and to explain one’s actions, inactions or omissions, intentions, and decisions. For client safety, every team member is accountable for their own actions. However, the team leader may not be on duty when you are working. You would then report to the charge nurse (the nurse on duty for that shift). Depending on your provincial or territorial legislation, in some care homes, there may not be a nurse on duty on the night shift, but support workers would have a contact number for one.
2. •
   Supervision in a community setting.
    In a community setting, the support workers and supervisor work for an agency, which is accountable for the actions of all employees. The agency may be hired by a health district or community services organization. In this case, a case manager will arrange with the agency to provide care or support for the client. The case manager—usually, but not always, a nurse—communicates with the supervisor, who then gives you, the support worker, information and instructions about care for specific clients.

Sometimes, an agency’s services may be paid for privately by the client or the client’s family. In such a case, the client and the family will give your supervisor the overall instructions. Your supervisor will then give you information about the client and instructions about their care.

In some situations, clients hire a support worker directly. In these circumstances, there is no agency supervisor; rather, the client is the supervisor. If you work directly with clients, it is advisable to have a written contract outlining each person’s responsibilities, rate of compensation, hours of work, and so on. The contract must be acceptable to both you and the client and should be signed by both of you. Both should also keep a copy of the contract to refer to if necessary. This contract would prevent unethical clients and their families from taking advantage of you. Do not hesitate to have the contract reviewed by a lawyer.

### Conflict in the Workplace

Conflicts and disagreements between co-workers can occasionally arise in any workplace setting. If they are handled quickly in a respectful and assertive manner, they can lead to a comfortable and trusting working environment that is safer for clients. They do not need to escalate into a toxic or dysfunctional working environment. [BOX 10.2](#b0015) lists suggestions for how to deal with conflicts with others. Whatever the conflict, it is important to avoid discussing details with co-workers who are not involved. Avoid talking to others about your clients or co-workers. Try not to be negative, even if co-workers complain about a policy or a situation. Instead, if you are unhappy with a situation, talk to your supervisor.

You must respect your supervisor and your employer. If you have difficulties communicating with your supervisor, try some of the strategies discussed in [Chapters 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml) and [9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml). If you remain unhappy, it might be best for you to find a position with a different agency.

Most important, do not discuss your work problems with your clients. You represent your employer, and the client trusts the facility or agency to provide quality care. A negative, disrespectful attitude could destroy this trust and harm your client’s health.

## Assigning and Delegating

Assigning means appointing someone to take on a task or responsibility of providing client care or support that is within that person’s scope of practice and within the agency’s job description and scope of employment. A task is a function, procedure, or activity that a person assists the client with or performs for the client. For example, your supervisor assigns your daily tasks. Your assigned tasks are listed on your assignment sheet. You should never be asked to perform tasks that are not within the agency’s job description and your scope of employment. Any assigned tasks will not require a nurse’s education and professional judgement, as your tasks will be within the support worker’s scope of practice. For example, you may be assigned to assist with or perform the following tasks:

1. • Activities of daily living (ADLs)—dressing, personal hygiene, mobility, feeding, toileting
2. • Instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs)—handling finances, managing medications, handling transportation, shopping, preparing meals, using a telephone or other communication devices, doing housework and basic home maintenance (see [Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml))
3. • Social and recreational activities
4. • Household management—housecleaning, meal preparation
5. • Basic support care tasks—measuring height, weight, and vital signs

Only regulated health care providers, such as nurses, have the authority (the legal right) to perform certain tasks because they have the skill, knowledge, and training to perform them. These tasks are called controlled acts and are considered to be harmful if they are performed by unqualified individuals.

Delegated acts are identified by each province’s or territory’s regulated professional organizations (such as for the nursing profession). Examples of controlled acts include inserting catheters and giving insulin. In certain situations, controlled acts may be delegated to you. Delegation (also called transfer of function) is a process by which a nurse authorizes another health care provider to perform certain tasks. A controlled act procedure—normally performed only by a regulated health care provider—may be delegated to a member of the client’s household, a family member, a support worker, or another unregulated care provider. However, specific conditions, called exceptions, must be met for delegation to be permitted to those outside of a regulated health profession.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur101) It is important to remember that while the nurse maintains the authority to delegate to others, the support worker does not. Regulated health professions legislation states that if the controlled act is determined to be a routine ADL or IADL for a particular client, delegation can occur.[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur102)

Each client’s situation is different. As part of the delegation process, the nurse must first determine if it is appropriate to delegate the task. The nurse must feel confident that the support worker (1) understands what the responsibilities are when performing the task; (2) knows when and who to ask for assistance; and (3) knows when, how, and to whom to report the outcome of the task. After training a support worker to do the task, the nurse then supervises and monitors the performance of the task to make sure it is being done correctly. Tasks that may be delegated to support workers must be routine ones with predictable outcomes that require little supervision, and they can be delegated for stable clients only. It is important to note that the same procedure may be a routine ADL in one situation but not in another. Tasks that you may be delegated to perform include suctioning of a permanent tracheotomy; in-and-out catheterization; and administering glucometers, dressings, tube feedings, and medication.

Only some nursing tasks can be delegated. Your employer’s policies and guidelines, your job description, and provincial or territorial legislation determine what tasks can be delegated to you. They also determine when and how tasks can be delegated. Although there are many similarities across the country, each province and territory has its own rules for delegation (see examples in [BOX 10.3](#b0020)). For example, in a home care agency in Ontario, a nurse can delegate to a support worker the task of giving Mr. Kupper his insulin since he is medically stable and his insulin injection is part of his IADLs. However, upon the client’s admission into a long-term care facility, a nurse must administer his insulin because Ontario does not permit delegation to support workers to administer insulin in a long-term care facility or hospital.[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur103)

### Who Can Delegate?

Depending on the legislation of each province and territory, delegation to support workers is
the responsibility of a regulated health care provider, such as a registered nurse (RN), registered practical nurse (RPN), or licensed practical nurse (LPN). When making delegating decisions, the regulated health care provider must always protect the client’s health and safety. The delegating regulated health care provider remains accountable for properly following all the steps involved in delegation (see [BOX 10.4](#b0025) and [BOX 10.5](#b0030) on page 158). However,
you are also accountable to the employer and to the client for your actions and to ensure the task is done correctly
 (see [BOX 10.5](#b0030) on page 158).
You, therefore, must understand the regulations for delegation acts in your area and in your facility or agency.

BOX 10.3 Delegation in British Columbia, Alberta, Nova Scotia, and Ontario

All provinces and territories have legislation that guides nursing practice, usually called a nursing act. British Columbia, Alberta, Nova Scotia, and Ontario also have legislation that applies to all regulated health professions. This legislation prevents unqualified people from performing professional functions.

Regulated health professions legislation and nursing acts list tasks that only nurses are legally able (authorized) to perform. In British Columbia, these authorized tasks are called reserved acts, and delegation is known as transfer of function; in Alberta, they are known as restricted activities; and in Ontario, they are called controlled acts. Only nurses—and not support workers—are authorized to do the following:

1. • Perform a procedure below the skin or mucous membrane (e.g., cleaning and dressing an open wound)
2. • Administer a substance by injection or inhalation
3. • Insert an instrument, hand, or finger into a client’s body openings, including the client’s bladder, esophagus, trachea, nose, ears, bloodstream, or surgically created body openings (e.g., inserting urinary catheters and rectal tubes)

Unregulated health care workers (including support workers) are not normally allowed to perform authorized acts. However, unregulated workers may perform an authorized act if a nurse properly delegates it. In the delegation process, the nurse transfers authority to the unregulated health care worker. However,
as a support worker, you can be delegated an authorized act only if it is allowed within your job description and employer policy.
 It remains the responsibility of the nurse to determine how and when an unregulated care provider can perform these acts.

Unregulated workers can be assigned authorized acts if the task is a routine activity of living. A routine activity of living is an activity that:

1. • The client needs done on a regular basis
2. • Has already been done for the client by a nurse, with consistent and safe results

For example, administering an enema is an authorized act. Take these examples: (1) Mr. Patel is paralyzed. He requires regular enemas to aid elimination. Because the procedure is a predictable and safe part of his routine, his support worker is assigned to perform the procedure. (2) Ms. Wolfe requires an enema before her surgery. She has never had an enema before. In her situation, the enema is not routine. Therefore, in some provinces and territories, a support worker is not legally allowed to administer it. In this case, only a nurse is authorized to give the enema. Support workers are not responsible for deciding when to do a task. Support workers will be assigned or delegated the task, as appropriate.

### The Delegation Process

The regulated health care provider must consider factors that are unique to the client’s situation when delegating tasks to you, the support worker ([FIGURE 10.2](#f0015)). In most cases, it will be a nurse who delegates a task, but in some workplace settings it can be another regulated health provider, such as a social worker. In some provinces, a task that has been delegated is not transferable to another client. This rule varies among
provinces and territories. For example, you have been taught how to give an enema to Mr. Lau. Mr. Davis is also your client and requires an enema. You cannot give an enema to Mr. Davis without being taught again, as he is a new client.
It cannot be stressed enough that guidelines for delegation acts vary, so you must know the laws in your province or territory.

1. • Have the knowledge, skill, and judgement to perform the task competently.

### Delegation in the Community

In the community, your supervisor will be a nurse for health care needs. The nurse usually does not work in the same building, so as part of teaching you how to perform the controlled act, the nurse should provide you with written instructions on how to carry out the task, the predicted outcome, and what you need to record.

For example, you have a client who requires in-and-out catheterization (see [Chapter 29](../B9780323709392000298/B9780323709392000298.xhtml)). The regulated health professional (in this case, a nurse) determines that this is a routine ADL by answering the questions in [BOX 10.4](#b0025). The nurse will teach you the task; assess your performance; and provide you with written instructions on how to do the task, when to ask for assistance, and what you need to record (e.g., the results of the in-and-out catheterization). The nurse will then monitor your performance regularly, and you must be able to seek assistance and receive it in a timely manner. You are responsible for asking yourself the questions in [BOX 10.5](#b0030) on page 158.

Some agencies provide educational programs or workshops for support workers. These programs educate workers about specific ADL tasks. For example, you might attend a program given by a nurse on how to do catheterizations for clients with paraplegia. You graduate from the program only after the nurse is satisfied that you can perform the task safely and competently. The agency is responsible for monitoring your performance over time.

In home settings, when no other health care providers are present, clients and caregivers may ask a support worker to do certain tasks. You must never perform a task that is beyond your scope of practice. Explain that you are not allowed to perform the task without the authorization of your supervisor, and then call your supervisor to discuss the situation.

### The Support Worker’s Role in Delegation

Although the regulated health professional is responsible for teaching, supervising, and monitoring your performance, as the support worker, you are responsible for your own actions. You have the choice when you are delegated a task to either agree or refuse to do the task. Before accepting a delegated task, ask yourself the questions listed in [BOX 10.5](#b0030).

It is also possible, especially in the community, that a professional who is not a nurse and not your supervisor will ask you to perform a task that you do not usually do. Before taking on the task, use your judgement, and consider your agency’s policies. (If you need clarification of those policies, contact your supervisor). Usually, you can do a simple, noninvasive task that you have done for the client before.
But in any of the following circumstances, tell the person who made the request that you cannot fulfill it:

1. • You have concerns about your ability to do the task.
2. • It is beyond your scope of practice.
3. • The client’s condition changes.

Your responsibilities when asked to perform delegated tasks are summarized in [BOX 10.5](#b0030).

#### Accepting a Task

When you agree to perform a task, you are accountable for your own actions. Remember, what you do or fail to do can harm the client. You must complete the task safely. Do not hesitate to ask for help if you are unsure or if you have questions about a task. Always report what you did and your observations.

#### Refusing a Task

You have the right to say no. If you have a good reason for not doing a task, refusing to do it is your right and your duty. By answering the questions in [BOX 10.5](#b0030) as a guide, you will protect your clients as well as yourself by using common sense. Ask yourself if what you are doing is safe for the client.

That said, you must never ignore an order or request to do something. If you have concerns, communicate them to the delegating nurse. With good communication, you and the nurse should be able to solve the problem. If problems continue, talk to your supervisor, instructor, or another professional to help you sort them out (see [Chapters 9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml) and 10).

You must not refuse a delegated task simply because you do not like or want to do the task. You must have sound reasons for your refusal. Otherwise, you could place the client at risk for harm. You also risk losing your job.

## Providing Compassionate Care While Maintaining a Professional Relationship with the Client

[Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml) defined compassionate (or person-centred or client-centred) care as care that demonstrates concern for the dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, and safety (DIPPS) of clients and their families at all times. It is also important to remember that your relationship with your clients and their families is a professional supportive relationship that is established to benefit the client. It is different from a friendship, which is a personal social relationship that benefits both persons involved. A relationship is the connection between two or more people, and it is shaped by the roles, feelings, and interactions of those involved. Relationships can be either personal or professional. Mixing a professional relationship with a personal relationship is unethical.

### Professional Boundaries[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur120)

As a support worker, you must remember that you are a professional and must maintain professional boundaries. Professional boundaries are appropriate limitations on behaviour, meant to protect the vulnerable client from the caregiver who has access to private knowledge about him or her. In any relationship in which one person is dependent on the other, the balance of power may not be equal. Being aware and trying to make allowances for potential imbalances of power in your relationships with your clients is an important aspect of maintaining a professional boundary. An important part of the five principles of support work (DIPPS: Dignity, Independence, Individualized Care, Preferences, Privacy, and Safety) is understanding that you do not have a right to force a client to do anything they do not wish to do.

Another is maintaining confidentiality (see [Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml)). As a support worker, you will be required to care for clients when they are at their most vulnerable (e.g., washing them after toileting). You may learn
about their personal health history or financial situation. Some clients may wish to discuss private matters with you (see the [Supporting Mrs. Chase: Maintaining Professional Boundaries](#b0010) box), and they must feel confident that their information will remain private.

However, your clients or their family members may consider you to be a friend because of the intimate nature of the information they have disclosed to you, but you must not share your intimate information with them. Always remember to relate to a client as a professional helper, not as a friend. Friends share compassion, support, common experiences, problems, and advice and choose to spend time together. As a support worker, you are assigned the amount of time you will be providing care, although you may enjoy that time spent with your client. ### Independence, Dependence, and Interdependence

Independence, dependence, and interdependence are fundamental concepts in professional supportive relationships.

1. • Independence is the state of not relying on others for control or authority. People who are independent control and direct their own lives and can do things for themselves (see [Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml)).
2. • Dependence is the state of relying on others for support and being unable to manage without help.
3. • Interdependence is the state of relying on one another. In most interdependent relationships, each person relies on the other for some things.

The above terms must be considered in relation to one another. No one is completely independent, and only infants, very young children, and unconscious people are completely dependent. Most people’s relationships have elements of all three traits.

For example, Julie considers herself to be independent. She feels she is in control of her busy and rewarding life as a support worker, wife, and mother of two young boys. She works full time and drives her children to day care. Julie is independent because she is in control of her career and her home life. However, she also depends on others. Without reliable child care, she cannot work full time. Julie and her husband have an interdependent relationship—they rely on each other for emotional support and companionship and also for help with child-rearing, housework, grocery shopping, and cooking.

An important goal of most clients’ care is for the client to achieve or maintain as much independence as possible ([FIGURE 11.1](#f0010)). Everyone makes choices about when to do things for themselves and when to rely on others. These choices coincide with setting goals and priorities. As a support worker, you must respect your client’s choices to do some things independently and to accept help with other things, even though you may not fully understand the reasons for these choices.

For example, Elena is hired to help Ms. Godin, 31, who lives with cerebral palsy. Elena’s role is to help Ms. Godin get ready for work in the morning. Elena knows that Ms. Godin is capable of dressing, showering, and preparing breakfast without help. However, each task takes a long time for Ms. Godin. She chooses to put her energies into
her work, not into getting ready for work. Elena respectfully accepts Ms. Godin’s choices. Clients, like everyone else, make choices according to their wishes and capabilities and must never feel that you are judging their decisions.

### Self-Esteem

What makes you feel good about yourself? Working hard at your job or school? Playing a sport? Caring for your family? How would you feel if you could no longer do these things? As children, we start to develop self-esteem, thinking well of ourselves, as we attain control over our bodies and our environments. Self-esteem generally strengthens when people feel that their lives have meaning for themselves and for others. Self-esteem is also closely associated with independence (see [Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml)) and can suffer when independence is limited or lost and when roles and identities change, as they do when a person is no longer in control of their life (see [Chapter 6](../B9780323709392000067/B9780323709392000067.xhtml)). As a support worker, you need to be sensitive to how clients feel when they lose their independence because of illness or disability.

Clients who have lost their independence need to find ways to rebuild their self-esteem. Some clients are not able do this and, in turn, become frustrated or depressed, while others are able to find a new purpose in life. For example, at age 17, Ricardo and two friends had been drinking at a party. On the way home, the driver lost control of the car and hit a telephone pole. Ricardo’s two friends died at the scene, and Ricardo ended up with quadriplegia and was in the hospital for 7 months. After his accident, he lost physical abilities that he had taken for granted, such as toileting and transferring. He became so depressed that he wished he had died with his friends in the accident. Then his high school principal asked him to speak to the students about the dangers of drinking and driving. Ricardo agreed, and since then, he has spoken to students at every school in his community, which has given him a purpose in life.

You can reinforce a client’s self-esteem by offering encouragement and praising the client’s successes. If the client is not experiencing success yet, you can recognize the efforts made. You might say, “I can see how hard you are trying.” Give the client honest, constructive feedback in a gentle, supportive fashion.

### The Balance of Power in a Professional Relationship

Being aware of the balance of power in your relationships with your clients is important so that you avoid any controlling behaviour; it is easy to unconsciously become controlling. In some situations, controlling behaviour may lead to the stronger person abusing the dependent person (see [Chapter 12](../B9780323709392000122/B9780323709392000122.xhtml)). For example, Lynn’s client, Mrs. Kerr, insists on wearing a blouse with 10 tiny buttons and buttoning them herself. Mrs. Kerr takes 3 minutes to do the first button. Lynn does not have 30 minutes to help Mrs. Kerr dress. She suggests to Mrs. Kerr that she wear something else, but Mrs. Kerr refuses. She is expecting visitors and wants to look good. Lynn undoes the button, removes the blouse, and hands Mrs. Kerr another garment, saying, “You will look just as nice in this sweater.”

Instead of imposing your will on your clients, involve them in solving problems that may arise. For example, Lynn could have explained to Mrs. Kerr that her time was limited, and together they could have thought of a solution. Lynn might have suggested that they take turns doing the buttons. Or she might have suggested that she carry on with doing other tasks (like tidying the room) while Mrs. Kerr dressed herself. Or Lynn could
have set some guidelines at the very beginning of the day’s schedule: “Mrs. Kerr, I have 20 minutes today, and this is what we have to accomplish in that time frame.”

## The Client’S Family

Close personal and family relationships are central to the lives of most people and involve some forms of dependency. Spouses depend on one another for emotional support, companionship, and financial support. Children depend on their parents to meet their physical, emotional, and financial needs. Older parents may depend on their adult children to help them with physical and emotional needs.

As a support worker, you will be working with different types of families. The family is a biological, legal, or social network of people who provide support for one another (see [Chapter 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml)). Families can take many forms and may include people related by blood or marriage or unrelated people who have formed a close personal relationship. Examples of families include the following:

1. • A married couple of any gender with or without children or stepchildren
2. • An unmarried couple living together, with or without children
3. • Two members of the LGBTQ2 community married or living together in a same-sex relationship, with or without children
4. • A single parent with children
5. • Grandparents as primary caregivers raising one or more grandchildren
6. • A divorced parent living with a partner, who has children living elsewhere
7. • Two partners who each bring with them children from previous relationships
8. • Older parents, adult children, and grandchildren living together

You may have different ideas about what a family is. However, as a support worker, you must always respect your client’s definition of family. Your client will determine who they regard as family. Do not impose your values on the person.

In addition to different types of families, you will work with families with different dynamics. There will be functional families that support your client and work as a team, and there will be troubled families who are in conflict over many issues, some of which you may not be aware of. Every family has different dynamics that are influenced by culture, language, or role changes. In patriarchal families, the major decision maker in the home is the elder male, whereas in matriarchal families, it is the oldest female in the home who makes the family’s decisions. It can be very stressful on these families when the head of the household becomes ill. The care plan that the health care team recommends for the client—and that you, as the support worker, need to follow—adds to the family’s stress since having the health team in the client’s house interferes with the family’s privacy. Be sensitive and aware that the family dynamics may go through changes when illness occurs. Communicate any concerns to your supervisor.

### Your Role in Assisting the Family

There are many situations in which you help families in your role as a support worker. You may care for new birth parents and their babies. You may care for toddlers or older children when their parent is ill or unavailable. You may assist or provide needed respite for a primary caregiver, the person (usually a family member or a close friend) who assumes the responsibilities of caring for a person with an illness or a disability in the person’s home (see the [Focus on Home Care: Assisting the Primary Caregiver](#b0025) box). Whatever the situation, when working with a family, you indirectly support their relationships. By providing a family with basic care and support services, you enable family members to invest more time and energy in their relationships.

[Chapter 6](../B9780323709392000067/B9780323709392000067.xhtml) discussed how roles change when illness or disability strikes a family. Very often, one family member becomes the primary caregiver for another family member. They form a different relationship, with new patterns of dependency, and this shift is rarely easy. The person with the illness or disability may feel angry about having to depend on the caregiver. The caregiver may feel burdened by the new responsibility in addition to other family and work demands.

Do not take on tasks that have not been assigned to you. As well, adapt your support work to suit the family’s standards and preferences, not your own. Respect the family’s routines, schedules, and ways of doing things. Consult with your supervisor if you think the family’s wishes may affect safety.

Specific professionals on the health care team prepare family members to take on care responsibilities. When helping families cope, they consider the physical, emotional, social, spiritual, and intellectual health of all family members. They also consider relationships within the family, including any conflict and potential for conflict. They may help the family deal with stress by working on improving the communication skills and problem-solving abilities of family members. Sometimes bringing them together in a family conference is needed to discuss how the caregiving can impact the entire family. As a support worker, you will be asked to attend such family conferences.

### Families in Conflict

When illness or disability occurs, the stress on all family members may be great, and members may have to cope with conflict. Conflicts may take the form of expressions of irritation, anger, bickering, or arguments. Conflict may sometimes be hidden. Adult children may care for aging parents with whom they have unresolved conflicts. Siblings who have not spoken in years may be forced to see one another during a parent’s illness. Sometimes, the health care team can help families resolve their difficulties in such situations. Members of palliative care teams are specially trained to help people resolve emotional problems that are causing them distress (see [Chapters 8](../B9780323709392000080/B9780323709392000080.xhtml) and [9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml) for managing stress and conflict).

When working with a family, try to be aware of family relationships and any conflicts, communication difficulties, and stressful situations ([FIGURE 11.2](#f0015)). It is not part of your role to help families deal with their interpersonal problems, but there are some things you can do in a stressful situation. You could encourage communication without taking sides, or you could defuse (calm) a tense situation—for example, when angry words have been exchanged between the client and a family member, you may suggest to the family member to go out for a cup of coffee (see [Chapter 9](../B9780323709392000092/B9780323709392000092.xhtml)). Agencies and facilities have policies to guide you in dealing with conflicts you might encounter. You must observe and report on family interactions (see the [Case Study: Family Conflict](#b0030) box) and also be alert for signs of abuse (see [Chapter 12](../B9780323709392000122/B9780323709392000122.xhtml)).

Abuse results when physical or mental harm is deliberately caused by another person or persons. Abuse can be caused by anyone, such as a family member, friend, partner, classmate, co-worker, supervisor, or caregiver. Abuse can be caused by one person or by two or more people. Unlike random acts of violence, which take place in all societies, abuse implies a relationship between the abuser and the abused, and the abuser or abusers are able to exert power and control over the abused. Abuse occurs in all age groups, all genders, at all social and economic levels, and in all cultures and communities. Abuse can take place in private or in public, for example, through an information medium such as a phone or the Internet. As a support worker, you must know how to recognize and report suspected abuse. You need to realize that abuse can occur in workplaces, homes, and community settings as well as in health care facilities. In some case, the victim is physically, emotionally, or financially dependent on the abuser.

In [Chapter 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml), we discussed each person’s right to be treated with respect and the need to accept people’s differences at all times. Showing respect for all clients is especially important in the field of support work. It is an unfortunate truth, however, that some people (and especially some support workers) do not recognize that
all of us have the right to safe, respectful treatment
. When clients’ rights are ignored, the clients become victims of abuse.

In this chapter, we will discuss the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, which is the foundation for all of Canada’s laws that make abuse illegal. We will also discuss (1) the types of abuses, (2) persons who are at highest risk for abuse, and (3) ways to recognize and to report abuse.

## Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms

The Canadian government has enacted laws to protect all Canadians, immigrants, and even visitors to Canada, from abuse and discrimination of any kind ([BOX 12.1](#b0010)). Since 1982, the most important protection has been the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, which states that Canada values equality and diversity and that no one should be abused and no one has the right to treat others unfairly. Knowingly failing to comply with the Charter is illegal and can be considered abuse. The Charter also requires everyone—including government, the police, the courts, and all Canadians—to respect the basic human rights of others.

## Types of Abuse

There are many ways in which people can abuse others of all ages, in various settings. It is also important to note that more than one type of abuse can occur at the same time. Abuse sometimes comes in other forms, such as bullying, or cyberbullying.

The different types of abuse are as follows:

1. • Physical abuse is force or violence that causes pain, injury, and sometimes death. Physical abuse includes pinching, hair pulling, pushing, slapping, hitting, shaking, choking, biting, or kicking. It also includes burning, poisoning, using weapons, or throwing things (e.g., a chair or a hammer) at someone.
2. • Sexual abuse is unwanted sexual activity such as rape and attempted rape. Unwanted touching, fondling, kissing, and exposure (i.e., the abuser showing their genitals to the victim) are also considered sexual abuse. Sexual harassment—another form of sexual abuse—is any conduct, comment, gesture, threat, or suggestion that is sexual in nature.
3. • Emotional abuse (psychological abuse) occurs through words or actions that cause mental harm. It usually involves an attack on a person’s self-esteem—for example, constantly insulting, humiliating, or rejecting a person, or saying that the person is “stupid” or “ugly.” Insults can also be directed at the person’s sexual orientation, weight, or ethnic identity (see [Chapter 7](../B9780323709392000079/B9780323709392000079.xhtml)). All of these can harm the person’s sense of worth and self-confidence. Emotional abuse can happen to infants, children, teens, adults, and older adults. Emotional abuse lessens the victim’s dignity and self-worth because they are called names, yelled at, insulted, mocked, threatened, or sworn at. The victim may be ignored for long periods or may not be allowed to do preferred activities or visit with family, friends, or others. Threatening to harm someone or something that the victim loves (such as a child or a pet) is another example of emotional abuse.
4. • Financial abuse is the misuse of a person’s money or property, usually for the abuser’s financial gain. Financial abuse includes stealing, forging signatures, selling property or possessions without permission, and persuading or tricking victims to change their will or give up control of their finances. Some abusers do not allow their victims access to their own money.
5. • Neglect is failure to meet the basic needs of a dependent person. Victims of neglect are usually children, people with disabilities, and frail older persons. Neglect occurs when a clean, comfortable, and safe environment is not provided. In some cases, the caregiver may even withhold or deny basic needs such as food, water, or clothing. Personal care, medical care, education, and attention and love may also be denied or withheld. The caregiver may knowingly neglect the victim or unintentionally neglect the person because of an inability to provide adequate care.
6. • Bullying is a type of abuse in which one person or a group of people purposely and repeatedly says or does hurtful things to someone else. It is also known as harassment. The abuse can include (a) physical bullying, such as hitting, spitting, or breaking someone else’s belongings; (b) verbal bullying, such as name calling, threats, and teasing; and (c) social bullying, such as spreading rumours, gossiping, or making others look foolish.[1](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur121) It can take place anywhere, such as at school or even in a workplace (see Workplace Bullying on page 190).
7. • Cyberbullying (also known as cyberabuse) is a type of abuse that uses communication technologies like the Internet, social networking sites, text messaging, and instant messaging with the purpose of repeatedly intimidating, embarrassing, or harassing a person. Cyberbullying is not limited to any one age group. There are a number of types of cyberbullying ([BOX 12.2](#b0015)). It is illegal to post untrue statements about a person (see discussion of libel and slander on page 72 in [Chapter 5](../B9780323709392000055/B9780323709392000055.xhtml)).

BOX 12.1 The Rights and Freedoms of All Canadians

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982) ensures that all Canadians, new immigrants, and visitors to Canada have the following rights:

1. •
   Fundamental Freedoms.
    This section includes the rights to freedom of conscience and religion; freedom of thought, belief, opinion, and expression, including freedom of the press and other media of communications; and freedom of peaceful assembly and association. Therefore, Canadians, new immigrants, and visitors to Canada have the right to practise the religion of their choice and express their views without fear of being punished in any way. They can also voice their opinions in print (e.g., in a newspaper or pamphlet), in person, or on the Internet, radio, or television. We all have the right to choose who we want to communicate with (or not), and we can join together in a peaceful manner to express our opinions (e.g., to go on strike or to demonstrate in front of a building by holding up placards).
2. •
   Equality Rights.
    This section includes the rights to equal treatment before and under the law and equal benefit and protection of the law without discrimination based on race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, gender, age, and mental or physical disability. Other grounds of discrimination that were not specifically set out in the Charter, such as sexual orientation and marital status, were recognized in 1985. We are all considered equal, and nobody can get poorer (or special) treatment because of their differences.
3. •
   Democratic Rights. These sections contain the right to vote, the right to run for public office, and the maximum duration of legislative bodies.
4. •
   Mobility. This section ensures the right to move from place to place within Canada and ensures that Canadian citizens are free to enter and leave Canada as they please. It also gives citizens and permanent residents the right to move to and live in any province.
5. •
   Legal Rights.
    These sections set out the rights that protect us in our dealings with the justice system. These rights ensure that individuals who are involved in legal proceedings are treated fairly, especially those charged with criminal offences, and include the rights:
6. 1. • To have life, liberty, and security of the person
   2. • To be secure from unreasonable search and seizure
   3. • To be free from unnecessary detention or imprisonment
   4. • To be informed promptly of reasons for any arrest or detention
   5. • To retain and instruct counsel on arrest or detention
   6. • To be tried within a reasonable time by an impartial tribunal
   7. • To be presumed innocent
   8. • To have protection against self-incrimination
   9. • To be free from cruel and unusual treatment or punishment
   10. • To have the assistance of an interpreter
7. •
   Language Rights.
    These sections confirm that English and French are the official languages of Canada and assert the equality of the French and English languages in particular situations. The language rights include the right to use English or French in Parliament and in any court established by Parliament, including the Supreme Court of Canada. Federal laws must be published in both English and French.
8. •
   Minority Language Education Rights.
    This section establishes that provincial governments must provide certain English- or French-language minority rights. Minority language education applies when there is a sufficient number of eligible children to justify providing schooling in that language.

Source: Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, s. 2, Part I of the Constitution Act, 1982, being Schedule B to the Canada Act 1982 (UK), 1982, c 11.

Today’s advanced technology allows people to take pictures, edit them, and then post them electronically to a social media site. It is illegal to do so without the permission of those pictured ([BOX 12.3](#b0020)
). Unfortunately for many, hurtful or embarrassing images can easily be posted anonymously and distributed throughout the world, so it is difficult for victims to easily ignore them. This redistribution of images or messages revictimizes the person over and over, perhaps for weeks, months, or even years in extreme cases.

BOX 12.2 Types of Cyberbullying, Its Impact, and How to Stop It

Cyberbullying includes:

1. • Sending mean or threatening emails or text/instant messages
2. • Posting embarrassing photos of someone online
3. • Creating a website to make fun of others
4. • Pretending to be someone by using their name
5. • Tricking someone into revealing personal or embarrassing information and sending it to others

Cyberbullying can impact its victim in a number of ways, regardless of age, leading to:

1. • Stress-related health problems (headaches, stomachaches)
2. • Social anxiety, loneliness, and isolation
3. • Depression
4. • Low self-esteem
5. • Social withdrawal or aggressive behaviours
6. • Suicidal thoughts or attempts

If you or someone you know is a victim of bullying:

1. • Report criminal offences, such as threats, assaults, and sexual exploitation to the local police detachment.
2. • Report unwanted text messages to your telephone service provider.
3. • Report online bullying to the social media site and block the person responsible.
4. • Walk away or leave the online conversation.
5. • Keep track of the bullying (write it down and/or save a screenshot of the online message).

Source: Royal Canadian Mounted Police. (2019). Bullying and cyberbullying. Government of Canada. <http://www.rcmp-grc.gc.ca/cycp-cpcj/bull-inti/index-eng.htm>.

BOX 12.3 Cyberbullying Legislation

As of March 10, 2015, it is an offence under Canada’s Criminal Code to share intimate images of a person without the consent of the person appearing in the image. This law applies to everyone, not only to people under age 18. The purpose of this offence is to protect the privacy a person has with regard to nudity or sexual activity. An “intimate image” is defined as an image that depicts a person engaged in explicit sexual activity or that depicts a sexual organ, anal region, or breast. The image also has to be one in which the person depicted had a reasonable expectation of privacy at the time of the recording and never gave up any privacy interest at the time of the offence.

With digital technology rapidly changing, there has been an increase of cyberbullying in the form of distributing intimate or sexual images without the consent of the person in the photo or video. This type of behaviour can occur in a variety of situations. Often, it is used as a form of revenge: a person has willingly shared an intimate image of themselves with a partner and when the relationship ends, that partner distributes those photos publicly (this is sometimes called “revenge porn”).

Whatever the motivation, the impact of this kind of cyberbullying can be devastating to a person’s self-esteem, reputation, and mental health. In some cases, these acts may have played a part in teens taking their own lives. Judges now have the authority to order the removal of intimate images from the Internet if the images were posted without the consent of the person or persons in the image.

Anyone convicted of distributing an intimate image without consent can face serious legal consequences. For example:

1. • They could be imprisoned for up to 5 years;
2. • The computer, cell phone, or other device they used to share the image could be seized; and
3. • They could be ordered to reimburse the victim for costs incurred in removing the intimate image from the Internet or elsewhere.

Source: Government of Canada. (2018). What are the potential legal consequences of cyberbullying? [https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/ntnl-scrt/cbr-scrt/cbrbllng/prnts/lgl-cnsqncs-en.aspx.](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/ntnl-scrt/cbr-scrt/cbrbllng/prnts/lgl-cnsqncs-en.aspx)

## Abusive Relationships

Abuse can occur in many different kinds of relationships. An abuser can be a caregiver, such as a parent, child, friend, grandparent, step-parent, or relative. Sometimes, the abuser is a nonrelative who is close to the person, such as a family friend or a parent’s or adult child’s partner. All age groups are at risk and all are equally likely to be victims, although some people are at greater risk for abuse ([BOX 12.4](#b0025)). Abuse occurs in all types of families: rich and poor, educated and uneducated. Any individuals can be abused by their spouses or partners; children can be abused by their classmates or by significant others; older persons can be abused by their caregivers or their adult children; or clients can be abused by health care workers. Health care workers can also be abused by their clients.

BOX 12.4 Persons at Highest Risk for Abuse

Even though anyone could potentially be abused (or, for that matter, be an abuser, given the right circumstances), there are certain groups of people who are especially at risk for being abused. Abuse may damage such persons’ sense of self-worth and dignity and increase their social isolation, making them even more vulnerable to abuse. Also, abused persons may be unable to access (or not know about) support systems that are available to them. These people include:

1. •
   Marginalized persons (especially women).
    Those who (1) are unable to report abuse because they are not fluent enough in an official language, or (2) belong to a visible-minority group (for example, members of Indigenous communities or some ethnic groups, or perhaps members of the LGBTQ2 community), (3) are geographically isolated, (4) have a physical or intellectual disability, (5) live with a mental health disorder, (6) have (or had) a substance abuse problem, or (7) work in the sex trade, and are marginalized, which means they are usually shunned by mainstream society.
2. * People who are marginalized are usually treated like they are “invisible” and they often feel powerless to report abuse, or they may not be understood or believed when they do report it, in which case authorities may not give as much attention to their reports as to other people’s reports. Sexual assault against women is particularly prevalent in Northern Canada, where there is a much higher proportion of Indigenous people (e.g., more in each of the territories than in the provinces).[2](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur122)
3. •
   Children.
    Children are usually dependent on the person who is caring for them. They are too young to know how to defend or protect themselves. Some parents or caregivers think that they have the right to “discipline” their children by using abusive force or words. Others are overwhelmed with the stress and responsibilities of being a parent or guardian, or they very likely were abused themselves during their childhood. Some abuse is punitive, but it can also be sexual in nature.
4. •
   Socially or geographically isolated older persons.
    Older people who cannot get out of the places where they live may not have an opportunity to tell anyone that they are abused. Bruises and other obvious physical signs might also go unnoticed if these older persons are prevented from having contact with others. Older people may not know how to escape from an abuser or may lack the strength to seek safety and shelter.
5. •
   Older persons with reduced cognitive capacity.
    These people may not know or remember that they have been abused. They may no longer have control over their finances, so they may not know that their money or belongings are being taken.
6. •
   Dependent older persons with disabilities.
    If older persons do not have opportunities to interact with others, abuses can often go unrecognized. In the case of physical abuse, bruises are often explained away by the abuser as being caused by the disability, age, or general health condition of the older person.
7. •
   Older persons cared for by people with substance abuse problems. People with substance abuse problems may lack impulse control—that is, the ability to control their angry outbursts or to deal with the older person’s challenging behaviours in a nonviolent manner. Others may have financial problems and steal from the older person they care for to support their own substance addiction.

Source: Adapted from Government of Canada. (2011). Elder abuse is wrong. Department of Justice. <http://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/rp-pr/cj-jp/fv-vf/eaw-mai/pdf/eaw-mai.pdf>.

Abusive relationships are complex, and there is usually no single cause for the abuse. In most cases of abuse by a caregiver, the abuse has been triggered when the abuser felt overwhelmed and felt that no help was available to care for their physically or mentally challenged child or dependent parent. However, in all relationships, a person is more likely to be abusive if they are dealing with the following:

1. • Problems with alcohol or drugs
2. • Mental illness (for example, major depression) or severe personality flaws (for example, an explosive temper, inability to control impulses, or lack of empathy for others)
3. • Abuse as a child
4. • Going through a period of high stress, such as divorce, unemployment, poverty, or illness.

## Recognizing the Signs of Abuse

As a support worker, you are responsible for recognizing the signs of abuse. These signs generally apply to all abusive relationships, in all settings. However, you are not qualified to judge whether or not your client is being abused. You may think you recognize one or some of the signs (see the [Think About Safety: Signs and Symptoms of Abuse](#b0035) box), but such signs do not always mean that abuse has occurred. These signs might, however, suggest that abuse is a possibility.

Watch for signs of abuse or neglect in the course of your daily work with your clients. Immediately report any suspicions or observations to your supervisor. Your supervisor will ensure that the situation is investigated. Know your employer’s procedures for reporting observations or suspicions of abuse.

Signs and Symptoms of Abuse

Victims of abuse can be subjected to one or more of the following types of abuse:

Physical Abuse

1. • Physical injuries (such as burns, bumps, bruises, scratches, cuts, or fractures) that frequently go untreated and either are unexplained or have unlikely explanations
2. • New injuries that appear while older injuries are still healing
3. • Frequent injuries (such as burns, bruises, welts, or cuts) on the face, neck, inner arms, back, upper arms, and inner thighs (people who are abused often try to hide their injuries under clothing or makeup)
4. • Welts, bruises, or burns that have the shape of the object that caused the injury; for example, the shape may be of a handprint, belt, or wooden spoon
5. • Unexplained missing or loose teeth
6. • A very groggy or overly sedated client

Sexual Abuse

1. • Irritation or injury (such as cuts and bruises or scarring) of the thighs, perineum, or breasts
2. • Intense fear of bathing or perineal care
3. • Torn, stained, or bloody underwear
4. • Vaginal discharge, genital odour, and painful urination
5. • Difficulty walking or sitting
6. • Avoidance of touching (see the [Supporting Emmy Lou: One Abuse Survivor’s Struggle](#b0040) box on page 182)

Emotional Abuse

1. • A change in behaviour, especially if the client seems depressed or unusually quiet or withdrawn
2. • Fearful behaviour, especially in the presence of the suspected abuser; for example, the client cowers, avoids eye contact, or trembles
3. • Unwillingness to talk or answer questions
4. • Change in behaviour when the suspected abuser enters and leaves the room
5. • Withholding of permission to socialize with family or friends or client’s withdrawal from contacts
6. • Private conversations not allowed—the suspected abuser insists on being present or within hearing distance of all conversations
7. • Change in behaviour of the suspected abuser (who may be the caregiver)—for example, on one occasion she is pleasant and cooperative and, on another, is defensive or hostile
8. • Caregiver’s failure to show any affection toward the person in their care, or shows of affection seem strained or insincere
9. • Frequent statements by the caregiver that the person in their care is difficult or demanding

Financial Abuse

1. • Caregiver’s refusal to spend money on caring for the person
2. • Unpaid bills (such as utilities, telephone, or rent) or bounced cheques, even though client has money
3. • Lack of adequate food, clothing, personal care items, or furnishings, even though client can afford them
4. • Suspected abuser’s greater concern is the cost, rather than the quality, of the care
5. • Missing personal belongings, with no explanation
6. • Suspected abuser does not have a job and is secretive about their source of income
7. • Client has to ask for permission to write cheques or spend money

### Spousal Abuse

Spousal abuse is abuse that occurs between intimate partners in a marriage or common-law relationship and may be physical, sexual, emotional, or financial. Usually, more than one type of abuse is present in the relationship. Spousal abuse is abuse in any love-partner relationship, including same-sex relationships.

Individuals of all genders can be abused by their partners. However, women are statistically more severely injured than men are. The incidence of male injury from abuse in a man–woman relationship is not as well known because, in many cases, the male is less likely to report this abuse. Domestic violence rates among intimate couples usually increase when the woman is pregnant or caring for an infant.[3](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur123)

#### Why Do They Stay?

People who are abused by their partner often deny the abuse. They also often choose to stay with their partners in spite of serious physical violence. Some victims may stay because they are afraid of leaving their abuser, who might have threatened them with harm, death, or denial of child custody. In some circumstances, the victim may stay so that the children can continue to have the financial and other comforts of a two-parent home. Some victims stay because they do not know where to go or how to get help.

## The Cycle of Abuse

Abuse often follows a pattern, known as the cycle of abuse ([FIGURE 12.1](#f0010)). The cycle of abuse can take place in any type of relationship, and is not limited to a romantic relationship. Abuse usually occurs over and over again, as the abuser tries to dominate the victim with power and control. There are three phases in the cycle.

![The cycle of abuse shows honeymoon phase abuser apologizes and promises to change; a period of relative calm leading to tension-building phase abuser becomes increasingly angry and aggressive, to abusive phase abuse can be physical, sexual, emotional, or financial or be a form of neglect, to again honeymoon phase. The whole cycle is centralized by power and control.](../../IMAGES/B9780323709392000122/main.assets/f12-01-9780323711654.jpg)

FIGURE 12.1 The cycle of abuse.

* 1.
  The tension-building phase.
   Tension starts to build between the abuser and the victim. Everyday events and comments irritate and anger the abuser. As the abuser becomes more angry and stressed, the abuser grows more aggressive. The victim may try to calm, soothe, and please the abuser, stay out of the abuser’s way, or try not to say or do anything that might anger or upset the abuser.
* 2.
  The abusive phase.
   Tension explodes into an abusive event. The abuse may involve neglect or physical, sexual, emotional, or financial abuse. Often, the abuse is triggered by an event unrelated to the victim’s behaviour.
* 3.
  The honeymoon phase.
   The abuser feels ashamed or sorry, often apologizing and promising never to do it again. The abuser may offer gifts or be very loving and attentive. At this stage, the abuser and victim may both believe that the abuser can change.

If specific measures are not taken to stop the cycle of abuse, it continues. There may be days, weeks, months, or years between abusive events. Usually, the time between episodes gradually shortens, and the abuse becomes more frequent and more intense. It is important for the victim and abuser to recognize the problem and get help to stop the cycle.

### Child Abuse

Child abuse occurs when a parent, guardian, or caregiver mistreats or neglects a child, which may result in the following:

1. • Significant emotional or psychological harm
2. • Serious risk of harm to the child

Child abuse is a gross misuse of a caregiver’s position of trust and authority over a child. Children who are abused tend to experience more social problems and perform less well in school than other children. As a result, as adults, they can remain poorly adjusted socially and lack success in life. Additionally, victims of childhood abuse are at greater risk for becoming violent abusers and criminals themselves. Child abuse can take many different forms:

1. •
   Physical abuse
   —the deliberate application of force on any part of a child’s body, which may result in a nonaccidental injury. Physical abuse also includes behaviours such as shaking, choking, biting, kicking, burning, or poisoning a child; holding a child under water; or any other harmful or dangerous use of force or restraint. Some people confuse punishment with physical abuse.
2. •
   Emotional abuse
   —see general description of emotional abuse on page 180. Other forms of emotionally abusive treatment include forcing a child into social isolation or intimidating, exploiting, terrorizing, or routinely making unreasonable demands on a child. Emotional abuse is often part of a pattern of family stress and dysfunctional parenting. In fact, some provinces in Canada now include exposure of a child to violence between the parents as a form of emotional abuse.[4](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur124) A recent study of wife assault found that children witness violence against their mothers in almost 40% of violent marriages.[5](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur125)

Children who are both emotionally and physically abused exhibit the greatest degree of aggression, delinquency, and interpersonal problems. Women who were abused in childhood are more likely to suffer from depression, low self-esteem, and suicidal thoughts. While physical abuse will put a child in immediate danger of injury, psychological or emotional abuse can have even more negative and long-term consequences for the child.

1. •
   Sexual abuse
   —the use of a child for sexual purposes by an adult or adolescent. Exposing a child to any sexual activity or behaviour constitutes sexual abuse. It may involve fondling, inviting a child to touch or be touched sexually, or even engaging the child in sexual intercourse, juvenile prostitution, or sexual exploitation through pornography. Sexual abuse is also considered a form of emotional abuse.
2. • Child neglect—failure by a parent or other caregiver to meet a child’s basic needs for emotional, psychological, and physical development. Physical neglect occurs when a child’s needs for food, clothing, shelter, cleanliness, medical care, and protection from harm are not adequately met. Emotional neglect occurs when a child’s need to feel loved, wanted, safe, and worthy is not met. Emotional neglect can range from the context of the abuser’s simply being unavailable, to the abuser’s open rejection of the child. Physical assaults are more likely to be reported, but neglect can have equally serious consequences for a child.
3. • If you witness or suspect child abuse, you are legally required to report it!

#### Situations That Increase the Risk for Child Abuse

1. •
   Family crisis.
    Divorce, unemployment, moving, poverty, and crowded living conditions all cause stress. Stress can lead to abuse (see the [Supporting Taylor: Understanding Contributing Abuse Factors](#b0045) box).
2. •
   Nonbiological or transient caregivers in the home. Children are more likely to be abused if they live in a household in which the biological parent shares parenting responsibilities with a partner (or partners) who does not have a committed and loving relationship with the children. The risk increases if either the partner or parent was abused or neglected as a child.[6](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur126)
3. •
   Isolation.
    Parents who mistreat or abuse their children are more likely to experience feelings of isolation, loneliness, and less social support as compared to other parents.[7](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur127)
4. •
   Caring for children with special needs.
    Children living with physical or mental disabilities or chronic illness are especially at risk for abuse. Children with personalities or behaviours that the abuser considers “different” or unacceptable also face greater risk.

#### Failure to Thrive

Failure to thrive is a term that is often used to describe infants, babies, or children who are below the norms for body weight, growth, or cognitive development. It is also used when referring to older persons who are losing weight and experiencing a health decline for no apparent reason. One of the many known causes of failure to thrive is caregiver neglect. In extreme cases, it can lead to permanent developmental delays and even the death of the client. Unfortunately, many of the mothers of infants described with failure to thrive were themselves likely also abused in the past.

#### What Can Be Done to Prevent Child Abuse?

Most abusive parents do not consciously set out to harm their children. If there are more and better efforts to assist troubled families, parents at risk for abusing may be reached and helped before they resort to violence. Prevention is a good investment, in terms of both personal and social costs to individuals. There are many ways to prevent abuse, including the following:[8](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur128)

1. •
   Parenting education. With education, parents can better understand normal child development and how to have a more nurturing and enjoyable relationship with their children. Positive approaches to parenting can help parents deal with children of any age.
2. •
   Child abuse prevention programs. Abused children tend to repeat the pattern of abuse when they become adults, so identification of potential abusers and prevention are the most effective means to stop the cycle of violence.
3. •
   Supportiveness. If a child tells you, the support worker, about an abusive situation or experience, you need to show the child that you believe them and ensure that the information is promptly reported to the appropriate authorities.
4. •
   Education.
    Children should know how to recognize and say no to abusive or exploitative behaviour and be taught that they have the right to be free from abuse. As a support worker, you can help children and adults find information and assistance to prevent an abusive or neglectful pattern from developing.

### Abuse of Older Persons

Older people are at risk for all types of abuse—physical, sexual, emotional, and financial—as well as neglect. Financial and emotional abuses are most commonly reported by older people. Abusers are usually family members, such as adult children or grandchildren. Often, the abuser is the older person’s primary caregiver. Most abusers of older people depend on their victims for financial help or
housing[9](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur129)—for example, an adult son who moves in with his parent after losing his job.

Abused older persons sometimes choose not to complain about the abuse. Like abused spouses, they may fear the abuser but are also dependent on them, or may not know where or how to get help. They may fear being forced to move into a long-term care facility if they report their caregiver. Some older people are not able to report abuse, as they may have physical or mental disabilities. Victims of neglect especially are often unable to report their situation (see [BOX 12.4](#b0025)).

In addition to the factors identified earlier in this chapter, some other factors that might increase an older person’s risk for being abused include the following:

1. •
   Stress.
    Family caregivers are more likely to be abusive when they resent their role. Taking care of an older person can be extremely stressful, especially when the person receiving care is physically or mentally disabled. Caregivers who feel unprepared or unable to fulfill the required duties can feel overwhelmed.
2. •
   Ageism.
    Ageism is another cause of abuse (see [Chapter 43](../B9780323709392000432/B9780323709392000432.xhtml)). Ageism is bias and discrimination against older persons. Caregivers who do not respect the dignity or abilities of older people are more likely to be abusive. For example, a caregiver who thinks that older people are incapable of managing money may take control of the person’s finances without permission (financial abuse).
3. •
   Vulnerability.
    Many of the factors that increase older persons’ vulnerability to abuse may also compound the effects of the abuse. According to the Department of Justice for the Government of Canada, older persons are vulnerable to abuse because the following factors may exist:[10](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur130)
4. 1. • Poor living conditions (inadequate housing, geographical isolation)
   2. • Unemployment (not employed or retired from an employer)
   3. • Dislocation (new to the area)
   4. • Racism (prejudice because of skin colour)
   5. • Homophobia (prejudice because of sexual orientation)
   6. • Disability (physical, mental, or developmental limitations)
   7. • Economic vulnerability (few financial resources or poverty)
   8. • Social isolation, lack of language or literacy skills (isolated because of geographic location; inability to speak, read, or write the local language)
   9. • Lack of access to community and health services
   10. • Lack of access to housing or long-term care facilities
   11. • Lack of access to the justice system

### Abuse of Clients by Health Care Workers

Abuse of clients can happen in a facility or home care setting, and clients can be abused by any member of the health care team. In facilities, they can be abused by visitors or a member of the staff. Clients are at risk for all types of abuse—physical, sexual, emotional, and financial—as well as neglect. All agencies have policies about what to do in the case of client abuse. Examples of abusive behaviours from a health care worker to a client include the following:

1. • Threatening to harm the client or trying to scare the client
2. • Denying meal breaks, drinking water, bathroom use, or handwashing facilities
3. • Swearing, name calling, and using racial or cultural slurs
4. • Inappropriate touching
5. • Sexually assaulting or harassing
6. • Using restraints inappropriately or when not ordered
7. • Handling the client roughly
8. • Isolating the client in their room
9. • Stealing from the client (including food or personal items such as razors or deodorant)
10. • Not reviewing the care plan regularly
11. • Not responding to a call for help
12. • Putting the client to bed as a punishment
13. • Moving the call bell away from the client’s reach
14. • Not checking on the client for long periods
15. • Using incontinence briefs instead of toileting the client, in order to save time
16. • Leaving the client lying in soiled linen or clothes
17. •
    Violating a client’s rights is another form of abuse
    —for example, providing care against the person’s wishes, failing to provide privacy, failing to keep personal or medical information confidential, and not letting residents visit or use the phone in private.

Abuse can be very obvious, such as roughly handling or force-feeding a client. Or it can be very subtle (not obvious); for example, staff can demean or belittle clients by calling them “grandma” or “slugger” or by referring to their incontinence briefs as “diapers.” These comments are embarrassing for the client and violate the DIPPS (dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, safety) principles. Health care workers can be abusive by not responding to requests for toileting, or by ignoring call bells at a facility or placing the call bell beyond the client’s reach.

#### Stress Can Trigger Client Abuse

As with all abusive relationships, stress sometimes leads to or triggers the abuse of clients (see the [Supporting Mrs. Finnie: Witnessing Client Abuse](#b0050) box). Some workers may not have received enough training or education to cope with the situations they are put in, or some may have too much work because of too few workers. As well, some clients can be uncooperative or abusive toward the staff—for example, they may hit or yell at the worker. Some workers respond to abusive clients by being abusive to them in return.

However, there is no excuse for abusive behaviour from a support worker. Sometimes, you will work with difficult clients in stressful situations, and you must recognize when you are feeling stressed. Take time out if you begin to lose your temper. Always treat your clients gently and with respect, even when they resist your care.

To prevent your behaviour from becoming abusive, immediately discuss difficult or aggressive clients with your supervisor. Your supervisor will help you decide how to handle them. If necessary, you can ask for a change of assignment (see [Chapter 8](../B9780323709392000080/B9780323709392000080.xhtml) for a discussion on how to manage stress).

## What to do when Clients Speak of Abuse

You should be prepared for the possibility of a client or child telling you about being abused. In such situations, it is important that you support the person and know how to offer immediate help. Follow your employer’s guidelines and policies. The following are some general guidelines on how to be supportive:

1. • Listen attentively. Let your clients tell you what happened in their own words. Recognize their feelings.
2. • Reassure clients that you believe what they have said. Stay calm and do not show anger or disgust. Do not deny or ignore the problem. Do not ask what they did to make their abuser angry. Such questions only make the victim think that the abuse was their fault.
3. • Assure your clients that you will do what you can to help. Notify your supervisor at once. Your employer’s guidelines and policies will note what should be provided to people who are living with abuse. Helpful community resources include the police, women’s shelters, counselling services, telephone helplines, and legal clinics.
4. • Provide emotional support to your clients no matter what they decide to do. Remember, you cannot force adults to make a certain decision or take a particular action. People who are capable of making their own informed decisions have the right to decide for themselves whether to live with the abuse or to accept help. Some people choose not to accept help; this choice is their right, and you must accept their decision. People who are not capable of making informed decisions must get the help of a professional.

## Your Legal Responsibilities in Reporting Abuse

Every Canadian province and territory has legislation that imposes a duty on members of the public and professional organizations to report cases in which they have reasonable grounds to suspect that a child, a person with physical or mental challenges, or an older person is being abused by a parent, adult child, caregiver, or guardian. As a support worker, you have this obligation as well.

Some people think that abuse is a private family matter, and it should remain behind closed doors. Others believe that they cannot do anything about it, so they should not even try. Neither belief is true. If you have reasonable grounds to suspect that someone is being abused or neglected, promptly report your concerns to the child welfare agency, the provincial or territorial social services department, or the police force in your community. If necessary, the report can be made anonymously.

Reporting is not difficult or time-consuming. In all cases, the person reporting is protected from any kind of legal action, provided the report is not falsely made and motivated by malice.

### Suspected Child Abuse

If you witness or even suspect child abuse, you must report it directly to child-protection authorities. (Look up your local child welfare agency or social service agency online.) Your name will be kept confidential. Do not rely on your supervisor to make the report for you. However, consult with your supervisor and get advice before making your report. In some provinces,
failing to report child abuse can result in fines or imprisonment
.

### Suspected Older-Person Abuse

All provinces and territories have laws that require health care workers to report witnessed or suspected abuse of older persons or those with disabilities within facilities. In these provinces, workers must report abuse directly to a public authority. Even if your province or territory does not require public reporting, inform your supervisor about any cases or suspicions of abuse. Be sure to write down your observations, including as many details as you can, and keep this information in a safe place. It will help you remember details, if needed, in the future. Know your employer’s policy and provincial or territorial law concerning the reporting of abuse within a facility.

In Canada, the Department of Justice has a fact sheet that describes what constitutes abuse of older adults. It is understood that mentally capable adults can make their own choices and decisions about seeking help. However, immediately report your
suspicions to your supervisor, according to your employer’s policies.

## How to Report Abuse

Your employer will have specific rules regarding how to report your observations. It is very important to record all your observations and make your reports in writing. Keep all your notes in case you are asked to remember details later on. In general, when you report abuse—whether to your supervisor or to a public authority—you should record the following:

1. • The alleged victim’s name, address, phone number, age, and gender
2. • The alleged abuser’s name, address, phone number, and relationship to the victim
3. • Description of abuse or neglect, suspicions, and evidence obtained to date; the date, time, and place; only the facts that you know or were told by the victim—do not make assumptions

Whenever you report your suspicions, it is essential that you respect and protect your client’s right to privacy. Tell only those who need to know. Do not gossip or tell anyone who is not directly involved.

## Abuse of Health Care Workers

Sometimes, health care workers, including support workers, are abused while on the job. You are at risk for physical or verbal abuse from your clients, your clients’ family members, or other members of the health care team.

### Abuse of Health Care Workers by Clients

Health care workers are especially at risk for abuse when they work with clients living with a mental health disorder or a condition that affects behaviour, such as schizophrenia or dementia (see [Chapters 37](../B9780323709392000377/B9780323709392000377.xhtml) and [38](../B9780323709392000389/B9780323709392000389.xhtml)). Clients who are addicted to drugs or alcohol, who have a history of abusive behaviour, or who are being placed in restraints are also more likely to be abusive toward you.

They are demonstrating responsive behaviours to how they are feeling when they are unable to tell you.

Staff abuse by clients should never be ignored or accepted as “part of the job.” Your employer will have policies on dealing with abusive clients. The [Think About Safety: What to Do When a Client Is Abusive](#b0055) box lists general safety measures to follow when dealing with abusive clients.

3. • Inappropriate touching
4. • Following you home or finding out your phone number and calling you at home

### Sexual Harassment in the Workplace

Sexual harassment—a form of sexual abuse—happens in the workplace when clients, their families, or co-workers make sexual comments, gestures, threats, or
suggestions. If another person’s behaviour offends you or makes you uncomfortable while you are at work, it is harassment. Sexual harassment is not only directed toward women; it can also be directed toward men, especially in a female-dominated workplace, and toward individuals of all genders (e.g., transgender)..

If a co-worker’s comments make you uncomfortable, you must remember that it is your responsibility to firmly but politely explain to the person that their conduct is unwelcome and unacceptable. Never assume the co-worker is aware that you do not approve of this behaviour. Be assertive but never rude. Most people stop the harassing behaviour once they realize it is unacceptable.

Try to have a reliable witness when you make your objections, and always make a note of the details—when and where it happened and what was said. Keep this documentation in a safe place so that you will have a record if it ever happens again. If the harassment continues, tell your supervisor about it. In Canada, employers are legally required to prevent sexual harassment at work. All employers have policies on how to deal with sexual aggression and harassment.

### Workplace Bullying

Earlier in this chapter, bullying and harassment were discussed. While many workplaces strive to create a friendly and respectful atmosphere, other workplaces have allowed staff members to bully and be disrespectful, rude, and hurtful toward some of their co-workers. It should be remembered that it is not just the character of the incidents but the number, frequency, and especially the pattern that can reveal bullying or harassment. Health care workers feeling bullied may also compromise client safety.[13](../B9780323709392160018/B9780323709392160018.xhtml#fur133)

## Chapter Review

Key Points

• All clients have the right to safe, respectful care.

• Abuse results when physical or mental harm is caused by someone in a position of trust, such as a family member, a partner, or a caregiver. The main types of abuse include (1) physical, (2) sexual, (3) emotional, (4) financial, and (5) neglect.

• Abuse occurs repeatedly, as the abuser tries to dominate the victim.

• The three phases in the cycle of abuse are (1) the tension-building phase, (2) the abusive phase, and (3) the honeymoon phase.

• All health care workers should be aware of the signs and symptoms of abuse in both adults and children (see pages 180 to 181).

• People at highest risk for abuse are (1) marginalized people, especially women; (2) children; (3) older persons; (4) socially or geographically isolated older persons; (5) older persons with reduced cognitive capacity; (6) dependent older persons with disabilities; and (7) older persons cared for by people with substance abuse problems.

• Situations that increase the risk for child abuse include (1) family crisis, (2) presence of nonbiological or transient parental partners, (3) isolation, and (4) caring for children with special needs.

• All health care workers should be aware of what to do if they suspect that one of their clients has been abused or if a client admits to being abused: (1) follow your employer’s rules on how to report your observations; (2) record all your observations and make your reports in writing; and (3) keep all notes in case you are asked to remember details in the future.

• When reporting abuse, record only the facts you know or were told by the alleged victim—do not make assumptions. Record the following: (1) the alleged victim’s name, address, phone number, age, and gender; (2) the alleged abuser’s name, address, phone number, and relationship to the victim; (3) your description of abuse or neglect, suspicions, and evidence obtained; (4) the date, time, and place of the abuse.

• When reporting abuse, it is essential to respect and protect your client’s right to privacy. Tell only those who need to know. Do not gossip or tell anyone who is not directly involved.

• Health care workers should be aware of how to reduce the chances of being injured in an abusive situation involving a client (see pages 188 to 190).

• Bullying in the workplace can have very negative effects on staff. It is the employer’s duty to protect employees from risks at work.

## Preparing Your Resumé and Professional Portfolio

Regardless of where you are applying to work, it is a good idea to prepare both resumés, which would be individualized for each position applied for, and a professional portfolio, which you could bring to show interviewers or potential clients.

### Resumé

A resumé is a concise summary of your experience, education, work-related skills, and personal qualities and is the first step toward a job interview. Depending on your work experience, the resumé is usually not longer than three pages. A resumé introduces you to possible employers, so it must look and sound professional. Employers use resumés to decide which job applicants to interview, and even small errors such as spelling mistakes may negatively influence the employer’s decision. Some employers may ask for a curriculum vitae, or CV, which is a resumé that is lengthened to include a description of achievements, such as teaching experience, research, awards, publications, and degrees. Here are some important tips for resumés:

1. • Prepare your resumé well before you begin your job search. This gives you time to do it properly without rushing.
2. • Remember, however, that each job is unique, so make adjustments to your resumé to fit the needs of each employer and setting. Consider what most employers look for in support workers (see [Chapter 1](../B9780323709392000018/B9780323709392000018.xhtml)).
3. •
   Remember to update your resumé frequently to reflect any new courses that you have taken or work experience that you have had since the last time you updated it.
