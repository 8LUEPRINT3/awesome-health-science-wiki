    Eliminating waste is one of the important functions of the body. The body’s waste products are removed through various body systems—digestive, respiratory, integumentary, and urinary. The digestive system gets rid of solid wastes from the body; the lungs get rid of carbon dioxide; the skin sweats, thus getting rid of water and other substances; and the urinary system removes waste products from blood (which come from body cells after breaking down and metabolizing food for energy) and maintains the body’s water balance (see Chapter 32 to review the urinary system).

    When assisting a client with elimination, you may be exposed to urine or stool (feces) directly or indirectly—for example, on linen and clothing that are soiled with these bodily fluids. Protect yourself and your client by following the rules of medical asepsis and Standard Practices (see Chapter 18). Follow the infection control precautions in BOX 29.1 when assisting the client with elimination.

    Normal Urination

    Urinary output refers to the amount of urine a person excretes. Depending on the care plan, the client’s urinary output is measured and recorded after every hour, at the end of each shift, at the end of each day, or not at all. The healthy adult excretes about 1 500 mL (millilitres) of urine a day. Factors such as age, disease, the amount and kinds of fluid ingested, dietary salt, and medications affect urine production. Some substances such as coffee, tea, alcohol, and some medications increase urine production. A diet high in salt causes the body to retain water; when water is retained, less urine is produced. Dehydration is a decrease in the amount of water in body tissues, which occurs when fluid output exceeds intake (see Chapter 33). Common causes are low fluid intake, vomiting, diarrhea, bleeding, excess sweating, and increased urine production (see Chapter 28).

    Urination, micturition, and voiding all mean the process of emptying urine from the bladder. The amount of fluid intake, personal habits, and available toilet facilities, as well as activity, work, and illness, affect frequency of urination. People usually void at bedtime, after getting up, and before meals. Some people void every 2 to 3 hours, and some others void less frequently. The need to void at night causes sleep disturbance in many people. As a support worker, you should be aware that although many of the clients in facilities are incontinent of urine, incontinence is not a normal part of aging .

    BOX 29.1 Standard Practices When Assisting With Elimination

    1. • Perform hand hygiene before and after wearing gloves.
    2. • Wear gloves whenever there is a risk for contact with urine, feces, secretions, or mucous membranes—for example, when doing the following:
      1. • Assisting clients who have diarrhea, urinary incontinence (page 640), or fecal incontinence (see Chapter 30)
    4. • Handling or cleaning bedpans, urinals, commodes, toilets, or bathroom floors
    5. • Handling soiled clothing or linens
    6. • Changing incontinence products
    7. • Measuring clients’ output
    8. • Collecting specimens
    9. • Change gloves between procedures on the same client, as the gloves may be contaminated. For example, change gloves after giving perineal care and before cleaning an in-dwelling catheter.
    10. • Remove contaminated gloves before touching a clean surface. For example, remove soiled gloves before raising the bed rails.
    11. • Remember that soiled incontinence briefs should never be placed on the floor. Placing briefs on the floor is both unhygienic (see Chapter 18) and a source of offensive odour. If you cannot leave the client while you are changing incontinence briefs, you should place the soiled briefs at the foot of the bed (never on the floor!), ensure the client’s safety and privacy, and then discard the briefs into the proper container. Ensure that the lid is placed on the container for odour control.
    12. • Wear a protective apron or gown if there is a chance that you might be sprayed or splashed with blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions—for example, when you are emptying bedpans or commodes.
    13. • Cover bedpans and tightly cap urinals when carrying them. Dispose of body wastes immediately and carefully to avoid splashing. If splashing occurs, clean the area immediately. Clean and disinfect bedpans, urinals, and commodes immediately after use.
    14. • Place soiled disposable materials in leak-proof plastic bags. Seal them tightly. Immediately discard the bags, according to employer policy.
    15. • Place soiled linen and clothing in the correct laundry hamper. Launder it as soon as possible, according to employer policy. In a client’s home, wash soiled linen separately from other laundry.
    16. • Follow guidelines listed in the Think About Safety: Guidelines for Safely Assisting Clients Who Are on Cytotoxic Drugs box in Chapter 44 (page 1034) for handling of linens and surfaces when supporting a client on chemotherapy (cytotoxic) medications.

    Clients may need help with urinary elimination. When assisting a client with toileting, follow Standard Practices (see BOX 29.1). Some clients may need help getting to the bathroom; many must use a raised toilet seat due to mobility or hip challenges (FIGURE 29.1), and others may use bedpans, urinals, or commodes. Follow the guidelines listed in BOX 29.2 to help maintain normal elimination. Remember that it is your responsibility to follow the client’s care plan when assisting with urinary elimination.

    Observations to Make About Urine

    Urine normally appears pale yellow, straw-coloured, or amber and is clear with no particles. A faint odour is normal. Observe urine for colour, clarity, odour, amount, and particles.

    BOX 29.2 Guidelines for Maintaining Normal Elimination

    1. • Practise medical asepsis and follow Standard Practices.
    2. • Provide fluids as instructed by the care plan.
    3. • Monitor the client’s normal voiding routines and habits. Check with your supervisor and the care plan.
    4. • Help the client to the bathroom when the request is made, or provide a commode, bedpan, or urinal. The need to void may be urgent, so be considerate of that.
    5. • Help the client assume a normal position for voiding, if possible. Women sit or squat, and most men stand.
    6. • For the client’s comfort, warm the bedpan or urinal under warm water.
    7. • Cover the client for warmth and privacy.
    8. • Ensure privacy. Pull the curtain around the bed, close room and bathroom doors, and pull drapes or window shades. Leave the room if the client can be left alone.
    9. • To ease the discomfort of clients who feel embarrassed about voiding with others close by, tell them that running sink water, flushing of the toilet, or sounds from the TV or radio mask urination sounds.
    10. • Remain nearby if the client is weak or unsteady.
    11. • Place the call bell (in facilities) and toilet tissue within reach.
    12. • Allow enough time to void; do not rush the client. However, do not allow the client to lie on a bedpan for a long time, as it may cause skin excoriation (irritation) or pressure sores.
    13. • Promote relaxation. Some people like to read during elimination.
    14. • If the client has difficulty initiating urination, you might try wrapping a warm blanket around the client.
    15. • Provide thorough perineal care as needed.
    16. • Assist with proper hand hygiene after voiding by providing a wash basin, soap, washcloth, and towel.
    17. • Assist the client to the bathroom or offer the bedpan, urinal, or commode at regular times in case the client is too embarrassed or too weak to ask.

    Some foods normally affect urine colour. Red food dyes, beets, blackberries, and rhubarb will make urine look red, and carrots and sweet potatoes cause bright yellow coloration. Certain medications also cause changes in urine colour. Asparagus causes a change in the odour of urine. Urine that is dark in colour may indicate that the person is dehydrated (see Chapter 28).

    Report to your supervisor if you observe urine that looks or smells abnormal (TABLE 29.1). Do not discard the urine, but cover it with a labelled paper towel, and keep it in the client’s bathroom or in the soiled utility room until your supervisor can observe it or gives you permission to dispose of it.

    Report client complaints of urgency, burning on urination, or dysuria—painful or difficult (dys) urination (uria)—usually caused by urinary tract infection (UTI). Refer to BOX 29.3, on page 642, for some common causes of UTIs.

    BOX 29.3 Common Causes of Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs)

    The urinary system is structured to prevent infections, but occasionally (or frequently in some people), people do get UTIs. UTIs can occur when bacteria enter the urinary tract through the urethra, the body’s urine portal to the outside.

    One type of UTI, specifically infection of the bladder (cystitis), is more common in women than men because the distance between the urethral opening to the anus is shorter in women, as is the distance from the urethral opening to the bladder.

    Another type of UTI, infection of the urethra (urethritis), can occur when bacteria from the gastrointestinal system and anus are spread to the urethra, usually through sexual practices. Clients of all genders can get infections of the urethra.

    In older persons, UTIs can be caused by inadequate emptying of the bladder, sometimes because of blockages in the urinary tract, neurological causes (such as stroke), certain medications, or the presence of a urinary drainage catheter that has introduced hostile bacteria into the bladder.

    Ways to Prevent UTIs

    For all people:

      1. Ensure proper hydration. Water will keep urine diluted. Also drink antioxidant drinks such as cranberry or blueberry juice.
      1. Empty the bladder at regular intervals.
      1. Provide good perineal care after each voiding and especially after each bowel movement.
      1. Follow agency or facility guidelines for care of catheters.
      1. Avoid unnecessary urinary catheterizations.

    For clients with female genitalia:

      1. Wipe from front to back.
      1. Urinate soon after vaginal intercourse to clear out bacteria that may have entered the urethra.
      1. Do not use douches, sprays, or powders that can alter the normal flora in the vaginal area.
      1. Avoid bubble baths, bath oils, and other perfumed products that can irritate the urethra.
      1. Wear breathable fabrics and loose-fitting clothing that allow the vaginal area to stay dry.

    Bedpans

    Bedpans are used for clients who are not able to get out of bed. Clients with female genitalia use bedpans for voiding and bowel movements, and clients with male genitalia use them only for bowel movements. Bedpans are made of plastic, stainless steel, or even sometimes a biodegradable cardboard. Stainless steel bedpans are often cold, so it is best to warm up the bedpan before use by holding it under warm running water. Wipe it dry with a paper towel. You may also want to protect the bed and linen by placing a waterproof pad or incontinence pad under the bedpan before use. Note: The use of powder to dust the bedpan rim in order to prevent sticking is no longer practised because it can cause breathing difficulties.

    A fracture pan—commonly called a slipper pan—has a thinner rim and is only about 1 cm deep at one end (FIGURE 29.2). The smaller end is placed under the buttocks (FIGURE 29.3). Fracture pans are used by the following:

    1. • Clients in casts
    2. • Clients in traction
    3. • Clients with limited back motion
    4. • Clients with fragile bones or painful joints
    5. • Clients who have had a hip fracture

    Follow medical asepsis and Standard Practices when handling bedpans and their contents. Bedpans must be thoroughly cleaned after each use. Follow employer policy.

    • 14 If the client cannot assist in getting on the bedpan:
      • a Turn the client onto the side away from you.
    • b Place the bedpan firmly against the buttocks (FIGURE 29.5, A).
    • c Push the bedpan down and toward the client (FIGURE 29.5, B).
    • d Hold the bedpan securely. Turn the client onto the back.
    • e Make sure the bedpan is centred and under the client.

    Urinals

    Clients with male genitalia use urinals to void (FIGURE 29.7) by placing their penis into the urinal and voiding. Some plastic urinals have caps at the top and hook-type handles. Some have a one-way spout, which prevents the urine from spilling out. The urinal hooks to the bed rail, the back of a chair, or any place within the client’s reach that is sturdy enough to support a filled urinal. The urinal should never be placed on the client’s overbed table, bedside table, or stand, as these surfaces are used as work areas, for eating, or for keeping the client’s items. Follow employer policy on where to place urinals.

    FIGURE 29.7 A urinal.

    While using the urinal, the male client should stand (the best position for bladder emptying), sit on the side of the bed, or lie in bed. For some clients who cannot stand without the support of one or two people, you may have to place and hold the urinal for them.

    Remind clients to cap the urinal after voiding if there is a cap, to prevent urine from spilling. You should also remind clients to hang used urinals on bed rails or the backs of chairs and to call you when their urinals need emptying. Follow medical asepsis and Standard Practices when handling urinals and their contents. A filled urinal spills easily, causing safety hazards. Also, it may be embarrassing for the client to have the filled urinal nearby. You should always empty urinals promptly and rinse and clean them thoroughly (in the same manner as bedpans) after each use to prevent odours and the buildup and spread of microbes.

    Commodes

    A commode is a portable chair or wheelchair with an opening for a bedpan or container, which allows a normal position for elimination (see FIGURE 14.31, page 247). Some commode chairs have wheels so that they can be moved. Clients who are unable to walk to the bathroom often use commodes. The commode arms and back provide support and help prevent falls. The bedpan or container should be cleaned after each use, as with the regular bedpan, following Standard Practices. If a specimen is not required, placing a small amount of water in the commode container or bedpan will allow for easier cleaning after use.

    The client’s safety must be a primary consideration whenever the client is being assisted either onto or from a commode, as well as while the client is using the commode. Some clients cannot be left unattended because they are at risk of falling (see Chapter 20). It is your responsibility to ensure that the commode wheels are locked and that the commode is positioned correctly over the toilet.

    For clients who cannot sit unsupported on the toilet, a commode may be wheeled into the bathroom and positioned over the toilet, without the bedpan or container in place. The position of the commode will depend on the client’s functional ability, as well as the space limitations in the client’s room. Clients who are able to walk to the bathroom but need extra support have privacy and safety during elimination.

    In some cases, the client may be lowered onto a commode chair, which is then wheeled into the bathroom and positioned over the toilet. To prevent injury to the client, you need to ensure that the client’s buttocks do not rub against the toilet while you are positioning the commode chair over it. In addition, you must ensure that the commode chair’s wheels are locked so that they do not roll. Securing the wheels helps stabilize the client and prevent falls.

    Clients should never be tied or restrained to a commode. While commodes are very useful, they can tip easily, so clients who cannot sit up on their own should not be placed on a commode. The longest time a client should be on a commode chair is 20 to 30 minutes.

    Urinary Incontinence

    Urinary incontinence—the loss of bladder control—may be temporary or permanent. The different types of incontinence are as follows:

    1. • Stress incontinence—the leaking of urine during exercise and certain movements. Urine loss is small (less than 50 mL). Often called dribbling, it occurs with laughing, sneezing, coughing, lifting, or other activities. Late-in-life pregnancy and obesity are other causes. The condition is common in individuals who have given birth, as pelvic muscles weaken due to pregnancies and aging. This is known as pelvic organ prolapse (POP) (see Chapter 33).
    2. • Urge incontinence—the loss of urine in response to a sudden, urgent need to void. The client cannot get to a toilet in time. Urinary frequency, urinary urgency, and nighttime voidings are common. Causes include UTIs, nervous system disorders, bladder cancer, and an enlarged prostate.
    3. • Overflow incontinence —the leaking of urine when the bladder is too full. The client feels as though the bladder is never completely empty and experiences only dribbling or a weak urine stream. Diabetes, enlarged prostate, and some medications are causes.
    4. • Functional incontinence—the loss of urine that occurs when the client has bladder control but cannot get to the toilet in time. Immobility, restraints, unanswered calls for help, lack of a call bell within reach, confusion, disorientation, difficulty removing clothing, and not knowing where to find the bathroom are all causes of functional incontinence.
    5. • Reflex incontinence—the loss of urine at predictable intervals. Urine is lost whenever the bladder becomes full, even when the client does not feel the need to void. Nervous system disorders and injuries are common causes.

    Sometimes incontinence results from surgery to the intestinal, rectal, or reproductive system. The presence of more than one type of incontinence is called mixed incontinence.

    Challenges Related to Urinary Incontinence

    Incontinence is embarrassing for the client. Clothing and linens get wet, odours develop, and the client is usually cold and uncomfortable. The client can develop skin irritation, skin breakdown, and infection. The urge to urinate can be so strong that some clients may rush to the washroom and sustain injury by slipping or falling on the way.

    As a support worker you can minimize incontinence by ensuring the client has the opportunity to urinate regularly—for example, before and after each meal, before and after activities, and before going to sleep and upon wakening. Incontinence can affect clients’ pride, dignity, and self-esteem and cause them to avoid participating in activities, going out, or visiting with others, out of fear of being incontinent in public. These clients need your support, understanding, and compassion.

    Stress for the Caregiver

    Caring for people with incontinence can be stressful. Some family members who are caregivers cannot cope with difficulties associated with incontinence and often seek long-term care for the client. As a professional caregiver, even you may find caring for these clients stressful, as they may need frequent care that includes changing wet garments and linens and giving skin care. Do not lose patience. The client’s needs are great, and your role is to meet these needs. If you find yourself becoming short-tempered and impatient, discuss it with your supervisor immediately. Remember, the client has the right to be free from abuse, mistreatment, and neglect. The incontinence is beyond the client’s control and is not something they choose to let happen. Showing kindness, empathy, understanding, and patience is very important.

    Incontinence Pads

    Incontinence pads help keep bed linens dry. Some facilities (mainly hospitals) use disposable incontinence pads, and some others use multipurpose items as both transfer sheets and incontinence pads, since these items have two layers of padding and a waterproof backing. Incontinence pads are placed over the bottom sheet from the client’s head to mid-thigh (see Chapter 14, page 222). Fluid passes through the first layer and is absorbed by the lower layer. When the pad becomes wet or soiled, you need to change it and any damp bed linens and place them directly into the correct linen hamper, according to your employer’s policies. Just placing a dry incontinence pad over wet bed linen to save time could be considered neglect.

    Catheters

    A catheter is a tube (plastic, latex, or other material) used to drain or inject fluid through a body opening. A urinary catheter is a tube that is inserted into the bladder to drain urine. Catheter insertion (catheterization) is done by a nurse or physician or even by the client if the client learns how to do it.

    Occasionally, a physician might order a client to be catheterized after the client has voided, to determine how much urine is left in the bladder. A client who was catheterized for 300 mL but voided only 200 mL would need medical treatment for inadequate emptying of the bladder and urinary retention.

    1. • A straight catheter is a tube that drains the bladder and is removed after one use. It is inserted through the urethra into the bladder.
    2. • An in-dwelling catheter (also called a retention or Foley catheter) is a tube (plastic, latex, or other material) that is inserted into the bladder through the urinary meatus and urethra (FIGURE 29.9). A balloon within the catheter is inflated after insertion, preventing the catheter from slipping out of the bladder (FIGURE 29.10). Tubing connects the catheter to the drainage bag.
    3. • A suprapubic catheter is a catheter that is surgically inserted through the abdomen, above the pubic bone, into the bladder (FIGURE 29.11). It is a type of in-dwelling catheter; that is, it is left in the bladder and is attached to a drainage bag with tubing. Suprapubic catheters may be used in clients needing long-term catheterization or after certain types of surgeries. The insertion site (the opening on the abdomen) and the tubing must be cleaned daily with soap and water and covered with dry gauze. To do this, follow the care plan and your assignment sheet.

    Purposes of Catheters

    1. • Catheters often are used before, during, and after surgery to keep the bladder empty. Catheterization reduces the risk of accidental bladder injury during surgery, and after surgery, it prevents the pressure on nearby organs that a full bladder could cause.
    2. • Catheters allow hourly urinary output measurements in clients with critical illnesses.
    3. • Catheters are the last resort for managing incontinence, since they do not treat the cause of incontinence, and the risk of infection is high. However, some clients have wounds and pressure sores that must be protected from contamination by urine.
    4. • Clients who are extremely weak or have disabilities and are unable to use the bedpan, commode, or toilet—for example, those who are dying—can be made more comfortable by having incontinence managed by a catheter.
    5. • Catheters have diagnostic uses, such as collecting sterile urine specimens and measuring residual urine. For such purposes, the catheter is inserted after the client has voided.

    As a support worker, you will be required to care for clients with in-dwelling catheters. Follow the guidelines listed in the Think About Safety: Guidelines for Caring for Clients With In-Dwelling Catheters box, on page 654, to promote comfort and safety.

    Think About Safety

    Guidelines for Caring for Clients With In-Dwelling Catheters

    1. • Follow the rules of medical asepsis and Standard Practices.
    2. • Make sure urine flows freely through the catheter or tubing. Tubing should not have kinks, and the client should not lie on the tubing.
    3. • When the client is being moved, the catheter tube must remain slack, never taut or pulled.
    4. • Make sure the catheter is connected to the drainage tubing. If the catheter and drainage tube become disconnected, follow your facility’s policy to cleanse the drainage tube cap and reattach it.
    5. • Urine provides the ideal environment for the growth of microbes. If the drainage bag is at a higher level than the bladder, urine can flow back into the bladder, and an infection can develop. Keep the drainage bag below the level of the bladder to prevent urine from flowing backward into the bladder.
    6. • If the client is in bed, attach the drainage bag to the bed frame; if in a chair, attach it to the back of the chair. Never attach the drainage bag to the bed rail because the drainage bag would be higher than the level of the bladder if the bed rail is raised.
    7. • Move the drainage bag to the side of the bed to which the client will be turned. You must remember to move the drainage bag before turning the client.
    8. • Do not let the drainage bag rest on the floor because microbes on the floor can contaminate the system.
    9. • Coil the drainage tubing on the bed. Secure it to the bottom linen (see FIGURE 29.13). Use a clip, tape, or a safety pin and rubber band, according to your employer policy. Tubing must not loop below the drainage bag.
    10. • In clients with female genitalia, secure the catheter to the inner thigh, as in FIGURE 29.13, A, and in clients with male genitalia, secure it to the thigh (see FIGURE 29.13, B) to prevent excessive movement of the catheter and reduce friction at the insertion site. Secure the catheter with tape or another device, as ordered by the care plan, to prevent the catheter from pulling on the urethral opening. Many agencies and facilities do not tape the catheter to the client, as some adhesive tapes can be irritating to the client’s skin. Always check your client’s care plan and your employer’s policy.
    11. • Check for leaks at the site where the catheter connects to the drainage bag. Report any leaks immediately.
    12. • Provide catheter care, if ordered, once or twice a day. It includes cleaning the part of the tube that extends outside of the body. Catheter care helps prevent bladder or urethral infections (see the procedure Giving Catheter Care, on page 652). Some employers consider it sufficient to provide only perineal care. However, catheter care is sometimes needed after fecal elimination and when there is vaginal drainage. Follow the care plan.
    13. • Provide perineal care daily, after bowel movements, and when vaginal drainage is present. Follow the care plan.
    14. • Empty the drainage bag at the end of the shift or at intervals specified in the care plan. Measure and record the amount of urine (see the procedure Emptying a Urinary Drainage Bag, on page 658). Report increases or decreases in the amount of urine.
    15. • Use a clean, dry measuring container to measure each client’s urine.
    16. • Do not let the drain or the drainage bag touch any surface. Wipe the end of the drainage spout with an antiseptic wipe each time after draining the bag.
    17. • Immediately report any client complaints of pain, burning, urge to urinate, or irritation. Also report the colour, clarity, and odour of urine and the presence of particles.
    18. • Encourage fluid intake, as instructed by the care plan.
    19. • Always check the care plan before changing a client’s in-dwelling catheter bag to a leg bag. Changing from one bag to another places the client at risk for a urinary infection. If it is in the client’s care plan, proper aseptic technique should be practised at all times.

    Drainage Systems

    A closed drainage system is used for in-dwelling catheters (including suprapubic catheters) so that nothing enters the system from the catheter to the drainage bag. The urinary system must remain sterile; otherwise, infection can occur when microbes enter the drainage system and travel up the tubing or catheter into the bladder and kidneys. A UTI can threaten the client’s health and even life.

    The drainage system consists of tubing and a drainage bag (FIGURE 29.20). Tubing attaches to the catheter at one end and the drainage bag at the other end. The bag must not touch the floor.

    Clients may feel embarrassed if the drainage bag can be seen by visitors. Carefully drape a bed sheet or robe over the drainage bag when visitors are present. Some clients wear leg bags when not in bed. The bag is attached to the thigh or calf (FIGURE 29.21), and the pant leg or robe covers it.

    As stated earlier, it is important to never hang the drainage bag on a bed rail because when the bed rail is raised, the bag is at a higher level than the bladder. When the client walks, the bag must remain at a lower level than the bladder.

    The Condom Catheter

    Condom catheters—also called external catheters and urinary sheaths—are often used for clients with male genitalia who have urinary incontinence.     Bladder Training

    Bladder training programs have been developed for clients with urinary incontinence. Some clients need bladder training after removal of an in-dwelling catheter. After having an in-dwelling catheter for some time, they may have lost muscle tone in the bladder, so the bladder may no longer be able to stretch sufficiently to hold urine. These clients may have urinary incontinence until bladder tone is restored. Control of urination is the goal of the bladder training program, which would be part of the client’s care plan. As a support worker, you are required to assist with bladder training, as directed by your supervisor and the care plan.

    There are two basic methods of bladder training:

      1. The client uses the toilet, commode, bedpan, or urinal at scheduled times. The client is given 15 or 20 minutes to initiate voiding. The guidelines for maintaining normal urination (see page 639) are followed. The normal position for voiding is assumed if possible. Privacy is important. Help the client relax, and provide encouragement. These techniques can help the client succeed.
      1. The client has a catheter. The catheter is clamped to prevent urine from draining out of the bladder (see FIGURE 29.14 on page 656). Usually, the catheter is clamped for 1 hour at first, and eventually it is clamped for 3 to 4 hours at a time. Urine drains from the bladder when the catheter is unclamped. When the catheter is removed, you encourage the client to void every 3 to 4 hours or as directed by your supervisor and the care plan.

    The Midstream Specimen

    The midstream specimen is also called a clean-voided specimen or a clean-catch specimen. The perineal area is cleaned before any voiding begins so as to reduce the number of microbes in the urethral area. The client starts to void into the toilet, bedpan, urinal, or commode.     Stopping the stream of urine midway is hard for many people. You may need to hold the container in place and collect the specimen after the client starts to void.

    Testing Urine

    You may be asked to perform simple urine tests—for example, measuring pH, glucose, ketones, and blood—using reagent strips. Ensure that the reagent strips are not outdated by checking the expiry date on the container. Straining urine for stones is another simple test. A physician orders the type and frequency of urine tests, and you must ensure accuracy when performing the tests. Promptly report the results, according to employer policy.

    1. • Testing pH —Urine pH measures indicate whether urine is acidic or alkaline. Changes in normal pH (4.6 to 8.0) occur due to illness, foods, and medications. A routine urine specimen is needed.
    2. • Testing for glucose and ketones —In clients with diabetes, the pancreas does not secrete enough insulin (see Chapter 33), which is needed by the body to use sugar for energy. If not used by the body, sugar builds up in the blood, and some of it will appear in urine. Glucosuria means glucose, a type of sugar (glucos, glycos), in urine (uria). The client with diabetes may also have ketones in the urine. Ketones appear in urine because of the rapid breakdown of fat for energy, which happens when the body cannot use sugar. The client’s care plan will determine when the client’s urine needs to be tested for glucose (or if the urine needs to be tested for both glucose and ketones). It is usually done four times a day: 30 minutes before each meal (ac) and at bedtime (hs). The physician uses the test results to regulate the client’s medication and diet. To test for glucose and ketones, follow the instructions printed on the side of the reagent bottle. (See Using Reagent Strips below.)
    3. • Testing for blood —Normal urine is free of blood. Injury and disease can cause blood (hemat) to appear in urine (uria), a state called hematuria. Sometimes, blood can be seen in urine, and at other times it is not visible (occult), so a routine urine specimen is needed.

    Using Reagent Strips

To use a reagent strip, dip the strip into the urine specimen, wait the specified length of time, and compare the colour on the strip with the colour chart on the bottle (FIGURE 29.25). You will probably need a watch or a clock to keep track of the time, according to the instructions. Instructions vary, depending on the test. Your supervisor will tell you how to perform the urine test that has been ordered by the physician. Read the manufacturer’s instructions before you begin. Always check the date on the container. Do not use the product past its expiry date.

    The Client With A Ureterostomy Or An Ileal Conduit

    The bladder is sometimes surgically removed as part of treatment for cancer or bladder injuries. Since provision must be made for urine to leave the body, a new pathway—called a urinary diversion—is created.

    Urinary diversions can be of various types. The most common one is an ostomy (see Chapter 28), which is the surgical creation of an artificial opening. A ureterostomy is an artificial opening (stomy) between the ureter (uretero) and the abdomen. The artificial opening is called a stoma (FIGURE 29.27). Stomas do not have nerve endings, so they are not painful.

    Some clients may have an ileal conduit, an artificial bladder fashioned out of a section of the ileum (part of the small intestine). Urine drains from the ureters into the newly created artificial bladder and then through the stoma.

    An ostomy appliance (which some call a “pouch”) is applied over the stoma. The ostomy appliance is a disposable plastic bag that adheres to the skin around the stoma and that urine drains into. The ostomy appliance is replaced any time it leaks, as leaking urine can cause skin irritation, skin breakdown, and infection. Leaks also often cause clients to worry about odours and embarrassing wetness. Immediately after this type of surgery, the client will receive care from a nurse, but you may be required to care for clients with long-standing ureterostomies. The client assists with care as much as possible.

    Compassionate Care

    Elimination is a very personal and private act. It is important to be sensitive to the client’s feelings of embarrassment or discomfort. Try to make the client feel better about accepting your help. Tell your supervisor if the client refuses care. It is your responsibility to promote the client’s dignity, independence, individualized care, preferences, privacy, and safety (see the Providing Compassionate Care: Assisting Clients With Elimination box.)

    Providing Compassionate Care

    Assisting Clients With Elimination

    1. • Dignity. Never do anything to increase the client’s embarrassment. Always be encouraging and positive when providing care. Do not show disgust at the sights or odours associated with elimination and ostomy care. Remember that your nonverbal behaviours and expressions reveal your feelings. For example, do not wrinkle your nose or grimace while you are assisting the client. Never refer to incontinence products as diapers, as this term can humiliate the client.
    2. • Independence. Respond promptly to all requests for help with elimination, as the need may be urgent. Believe clients when they ask for help, even if they just used the toilet, bedpan, urinal, or commode. Your prompt response gives the client some sense of control.
    3. • Individualized care. For care to be truly client-centred, it must be planned or adapted to fit the client’s choices, physical and cognitive strengths and limitations, personality, and care plan.
    4. • Preferences. Respect the client’s preferences, whenever possible. For example, some male clients may want to use the urinal sitting up, and some others may prefer to use it lying down.
    5. • Privacy. Provide as much privacy as possible. Lack of privacy may inhibit the client and interfere with elimination. Pull privacy curtains and close doors, shades, and drapes. Ask visitors or family members to step out of the room. If allowed by the care plan, leave the room after assisting the client to the bathroom or providing a bedpan, urinal, or commode. Or, to be immediately available, stand just outside the door, stand on the other side of the privacy curtain, or stay by the client’s side but look away if safe to do so. Ask your supervisor for advice on ways to protect the client’s privacy.
    6. • Safety. Do not leave clients who are weak or dizzy. If you can leave the client and a call bell is available, make sure it is always within reach. Stay close enough so that you can hear the client’s call for help. Remember to follow Standard Practices.

    Key Points

    • The body’s waste products are removed through the digestive, respiratory, integumentary, and urinary systems. The urinary system removes waste products from blood and maintains the body’s water balance.

    • Urination, micturition, and voiding all mean the process of emptying urine from the bladder.

    • The amount of fluid intake, personal habits, and available toilet facilities, as well as activity, work, and illness, affect urinary frequency.

    • Urinary incontinence is not a normal part of aging.

    • Urine should be observed for colour, clarity, odour, amount, and particles. It normally is clear, has no particles, and appears pale yellow, straw-coloured, or amber. A faint odour is normal.

    • Some foods naturally affect urine colour. Certain medications also cause changes in urine colour. Some foods such as asparagus will change the odour of urine.

    • Report to your supervisor if you observe urine that looks or smells abnormal. Report client complaints of urgency, burning on urination, or dysuria (painful or difficult urination), usually caused by a UTI.

    • You should be familiar with the various common urinary conditions.

    • You should be familiar with the types and causes of urinary incontinence.

    • Clients with urinary incontinence need frequent, proper skin care and should be changed into clean, dry garments after each episode. Promptly changing wet bed linen is also essential; failure to do so is considered a form of neglect.

    Normal Bowel Movements

    Foods and fluids that are partially digested in the stomach are called chyme, and these pass from the stomach into the small intestine and enter the large intestine (large bowel or colon), where the fluids are absorbed. Chyme thus becomes more solid in consistency. Once it is in the colon, this semi-solid mass of waste products is referred to as feces.

    Feces move through the intestine by peristalsis—the alternating contraction and relaxation of intestinal muscles. Feces move through the colon to the rectum, where they are stored until they are excreted from the body. Defecation (bowel movement, or BM) is the process of excreting feces from the rectum through the anus. Stool is another term for excreted feces.

    On average, people have a bowel movement every day or every 2 to 3 days. Some people have two or three bowel movements a day. People defecate at different times—for example, after breakfast or in the evening. Many older people expect to have a bowel movement every day, and the slightest irregularity causes them concern. Reassurance from all team members can help relieve their anxiety.

    Stools are normally brown in colour from bile that is produced in the liver and is important to the digestive process (see Chapters 27 and 32). However, some medications, such as some iron-replacement medications, or health challenges, such as bleeding in the stomach, small intestine, or both, will cause the stools to appear black or tarry (black, unformed, and sticky, similar to tar); bleeding in the lower colon or rectum or eating beets will make the stools appear red. A diet that contains a lot of green vegetables can cause green stools. Various diseases and infections can cause clay-coloured, white, pale brown, orange, or green stools.

    Stools are normally soft, formed, moist, and shaped like the rectum. They have a characteristic odour, which is caused by bacterial action in the intestines. Normal flora bacteria are necessary for stools to form (FIGURE 30.1). Certain foods (for example, eggs, onions, and garlic) can cause odours in the stools. Medications (such as some multivitamins) can also cause stools to be foul-smelling.

    Observations

    Whether you work in the community or in a facility, it is essential that you accurately record your client’s bowel movements during your shift so that your team can decide if adjustments in diet or medications are necessary. It is important for the support worker to know the client’s regular bowel routine and to adhere to the routine whenever possible. For example, if the client prefers to use the commode chair for a bowel movement, help the client to do so.

    Your careful observations about the client’s stools are important for the care-planning process. Observe the stool for the following: colour, amount, consistency, odour, shape, and size. When you observe abnormal stools and if you work in a facility, ask a nurse to observe the bowel movement. Also report any abnormalities in bowel movement frequency and client complaints of pain.

    Factors Affecting Bowel Movement

    Normal defecation, as gauged by frequency, consistency, colour, and odour of stools, can be affected by many factors:

    1. • Privacy. Like urination, defecation is a private act. Lack of privacy deters many people from defecating despite having the urge. Clients who ignore the urge to defecate because others are present can become constipated. Odours associated with defecation can be embarrassing.
    2. • Personal habits. Many clients routinely have a bowel movement after breakfast, as food in the stomach may stimulate the bowels to move. Some people do relaxing activities—for example, drinking a hot beverage, reading a book or newspaper, or taking a walk—which make defecation easier.
    3. • Diet. A well-balanced diet and bulk are needed to promote regular bowel movements. High-fibre foods such as fruits, vegetables, and whole grain cereals and breads leave a residue that provides needed bulk. However, many people do not eat enough fruits and vegetables. Some clients refuse to eat fruits and vegetables, thinking they will not be able to digest them. For adults who do not have teeth or have poorly fitting dentures, chewing some foods can be difficult. Bran, prunes, and juices help prevent constipation, but some other foods can cause either diarrhea or constipation; for example, milk causes constipation in some people and diarrhea in others. Other foods, including chocolate, can cause similar reactions. Spicy foods can irritate the intestines, resulting in frequent stools or diarrhea. Gas-forming foods such as onions, beans, cabbage, cauliflower, radishes, and cucumber stimulate peristalsis, and increased peristalsis results in defecation. But many people avoid gas-forming foods because they cause stomach ache or bloating.
    4. • Fluids. Stool consistency depends on the amount of water absorbed in the colon. The amount of fluid ingested, urine output, and vomiting can affect stool consistency. Feces become hard and dry when large amounts of water are absorbed by the colon, as a result of dehydration (see Chapters 28 and 33), and when fluid intake is poor. Hard, dry feces move through the intestines at a slower rate, resulting in constipation. Drinking 6 to 8 glasses of water every day promotes regular bowel movement. Warm fluids—coffee, tea, hot cider, and warm water—increase peristalsis.
    5. • Activity. Exercise and activity maintain overall muscle tone, which can stimulate peristalsis. Irregular elimination and constipation often occur from inactivity and bed rest, which can result from disease, surgery, or injury. The care plan lists comfort and safety measures to meet the client’s elimination needs. The actions in BOX 30.1 are routinely practised to promote comfort and safety during defecation.
    6. • Medications. On the one hand, medications can prevent constipation or control diarrhea; on the other hand, some medications can cause lose stools, diarrhea, or constipation as adverse effects. Medications for pain relief often cause constipation. Antibiotics, which are normally prescribed to fight or prevent infection, often cause loose stools or diarrhea. Diarrhea occurs when the antibiotics kill normal flora (bacteria that are always present) in the large intestine.
    7. • Aging. As people age, feces tend to pass through the intestines at a slower rate, resulting in constipation. However, older people who are active and eat a well-balanced diet may not suffer from constipation. While older clients may lose bowel control, this loss of control is not a normal consequence of aging. Some older persons may not completely empty the rectum and will need to defecate again 30 to 45 minutes after the first bowel movement.
    8. • Disability. Some clients with disabilities are not able to control their bowel movements and defecate whenever feces enter the rectum. Such clients need a bowel training program (see page 684), with the goal of having a bowel movement at the same time each day.

    Common Challenges Affecting Bowel Movement

    Common conditions that affect normal bowel movement include constipation, fecal impaction, loose stools, diarrhea, fecal incontinence, and flatulence.

    Constipation

    Constipation is a condition in which bowel movements are less frequent than usual, and the stool is hard, dry, and difficult to pass, causing the client to strain to have a bowel movement. Clients may complain of abdominal discomfort, a feeling of fullness, or increased gas (flatus). Stools could be large or marble sized. Large stools cause pain as they pass through the anus. Constipation occurs when feces move through the intestine slowly, allowing more time for water absorption in the colon. Common causes of constipation include a low-fibre diet, ignoring the urge to defecate, decreased fluid intake leading to dehydration, inactivity, medications, aging, and certain diseases. Constipation can lead to a number of health issues (see the Think About Safety: Preventing Constipation box).

    Think About Safety

    Preventing Constipation

    A client may be too embarrassed to tell you about their constipation. If you note that the client is having fewer bowel movements than normal, inform your supervisor. If a client is prone to constipation, it is important to keep a record of its frequency.

    Common measures used to prevent or relieve constipation include changing the diet (increasing fibre, increasing fluids) and encouraging physical activity. The designated health care provider may order medications, suppositories, or enemas to encourage bowel evacuation for prolonged constipation.

    Hemorrhoids, which are swollen veins in the anal canal, are often associated with constipation and straining at bowel movements, as well as other causes, such as pregnancy.

    As a support worker, you play a very important role in preventing constipation in your clients by ensuring adequate activity, fluids, and privacy. Prevention of constipation is a much better option than treatment. It is essential to accurately record your client’s bowel movements during your shift to enable team members to recognize and treat constipation in a timely manner.

    Fecal Impaction

    Fecal impaction is the prolonged retention and accumulation of feces in the rectum. Feces become hard or puttylike in consistency and grow increasingly so as more water is absorbed. The constipated stool can form a large, solid mass or several small, hard masses. Fecal impaction results if constipation is not relieved. Once the feces are impacted, the client will be unable to defecate easily without intervention. If left untreated, a fecal impaction can lead to complete bowel obstruction, which will require surgery.

    The client may try many times to defecate. If a client shows any of the following signs or symptoms of a fecal impaction, report it at once:

    1. • Abdominal discomfort, which can be severe or cause swelling or both
    2. • Cramping
    3. • A feeling of fullness or pain in the rectum
    4. • Nausea or vomiting; vomitus that smells like stool
    5. • Fever
    6. • Increased urge or decreased ability to urinate
    7. • Liquid feces seeping from the anus. Seepage may look like diarrhea but is, in fact, liquid feces passing around the hardened fecal mass.

    A designated health care provider performs a digital (finger) exam to check for impaction. In Alberta, support workers’ competencies include rectal touch, which is done by inserting a lubricated, gloved finger into the rectum and feeling for a hard mass. The client may feel uncomfortable after the procedure. The designated health care provider may order medications; a rectal suppository, which will lubricate the stool for easier passage; and enemas to remove the impaction. On occasion, the designated health care provider or nurse may remove the fecal mass manually with a gloved finger, a process called digital removal of an impaction.

    Diarrhea

    Diarrhea is the frequent passage of liquid stools. Feces move through the intestines rapidly, thereby reducing the time for fluid absorption in the colon. The need to defecate is urgent, and some clients may not be able to get to a bathroom in time. Abdominal cramping, nausea, and vomiting may also occur. Excessive loose stools or diarrhea can lead to dehydration (see Chapter 33), especially in infants, children, and older persons. In some severe cases, dehydration can even lead to death.

    Causes of diarrhea include infections, certain medications, foods that irritate the stomach, a contagious illness (such as influenza), and pathogens in food and water. Diet and medications reduce peristalsis and stop the diarrhea. In many agencies, if a client passes three or more loose stools in a day, enteric transmission precautions (see Chapter 19) are begun. Strict hand hygiene must always be observed.

    Skin breakdown and pressure ulcers are a risk because of liquid feces and frequent wiping with toilet tissue. Products such as barrier creams are often applied to the anal area after each cleaning. It is important to follow your employer’s policies regarding the application of any topical product (see Chapter 45). When a client has diarrhea, as a support worker, you need to do the following:

    1. • Assist with the client’s elimination needs promptly
    2. • Dispose of stools promptly to prevent odours and the spread of microbes
    3. • Provide good skin care, as directed by your agency’s or facility’s policies

    Fluid lost through diarrhea must be replaced in order to maintain the body’s homeostasis. In a case where diarrhea is extreme and the client requires rapid or a large volume of fluid replacement, the client would receive intravenous (IV) therapy (see Chapter 28), usually after being admitted to hospital.

    Fecal Incontinence

    Fecal incontinence (or anal incontinence) is the inability to control the passage of feces and gas through the anus due to intestinal and nervous system diseases, injuries, fecal impaction, diarrhea, or some medications. Fecal incontinence can also occur because of delayed requests for help to use the bathroom, commode, or bedpan.

    Clients living with mental health disorders, dementia, or cognitive disorders (see Chapters 37 and 38) may not recognize the urge to defecate, and as a result, some may soil themselves, the furniture, and the walls. In such situations, a common challenge that support workers encounter is resistance to care, making washing and changing the client difficult. In such cases, follow the client’s care plan. Also, talk to your supervisor if you have difficulty providing care to a client who has been incontinent of stool and is resisting care.

    Flatulence

    Gas and air are normally found in the stomach and intestines and are expelled through the mouth (eructating, or belching) and the anus. Gas and air passed through the anus are called flatus. Flatulence is the excessive formation of gas or air in the stomach and intestines. Common causes are as follows:

    1. • Swallowing air while eating and drinking, especially when chewing gum, eating quickly, drinking through a straw, and drinking carbonated beverages (people who are tense or anxious also tend to swallow large amounts of air when drinking)
    2. • Bacterial action in the intestines
    3. • Gas-forming foods (onion, beans, cabbage, cauliflower, radishes, cucumber)
    4. • Constipation
    5. • Bowel and abdominal surgeries
    6. • Medications that decrease peristalsis

    If flatus is not expelled, the intestines distend; that is, they swell or enlarge from the pressure of the gases. Abdominal cramping or pain (sometimes severe), shortness of breath, a swollen abdomen, belching, and burping can occur. “Bloating” is a common complaint. Exercise, walking, and the left-side-lying position often produce flatus, which will relieve the bloating. Designated health care providers may order suppositories, enemas, medications, or rectal tubes to relieve flatulence.

    Bowel Training

    Bowel training is the process of establishing a regular fecal elimination pattern. It has two goals:

      1. To gain control of bowel movement
      1. To prevent fecal impaction, constipation, and fecal incontinence

    The urge to defecate is usually felt after a meal, often after breakfast. The client’s usual time of day for a bowel movement is noted on the care plan, and the use of the toilet, commode, or bedpan should be offered at this time. Factors that promote elimination are part of the care plan and bowel training program. These include a high-fibre diet, increased fluids, warm fluids, activity, and privacy. Your supervisor will tell you about a client’s bowel training program.

    The designated health care provider may order a rectal suppository to stimulate defecation. A suppository is a cone-shaped, solid medication that is inserted into a body opening. It eventually dissolves at body temperature. Rectal suppositories are preferred over enemas as they are usually more comfortable for the client. Depending on the agency’s policy and provincial or territorial standards, the support worker or nurse inserts the proper suppository into the client’s rectum. In some cases, it can be self-applied by the client, and you may assist as needed (see Chapter 45). It takes time for the suppository to dissolve and work in the body, but a bowel movement typically occurs after about 30 minutes.

    Enemas

    An enema—the introduction of fluid into the rectum and lower colon—is ordered by designated health care providers to remove feces and to relieve constipation or fecal impaction. While most enemas are commercial enemas, which are pre-packaged and ready to administer, some (such as a saline enema) must be prepared prior to administration. Clients with severely limited mobility or who are paralyzed are at risk for constipation or fecal impaction. Enemas also are ordered to clean the bowel of feces before certain surgeries or X-ray procedures. Sometimes, enemas are ordered to relieve excessive flatulence and intestinal distension and constipation.

    Enemas are usually safe procedures, and often clients can give themselves enemas at home. However, enemas are invasive and involve inserting a tube into a body opening, so they can be dangerous for any adult and are more so for those with certain heart and kidney diseases. Therefore, giving an enema is a controlled act, meaning the authority to perform it should usually be delegated to you by a nurse (see Chapter 10). If you are delegated to perform an enema, you first must answer the following questions:

    1. • Do your provincial or territorial laws and employer’s policies allow you to perform the procedure?
    2. • Do you have the necessary education and training?
    3. • Have you reviewed the procedure with a nurse?
    4. • Is a nurse available to answer questions and to supervise you?

    If having an enema is part of the client’s activities of daily living (ADLs), this task is exempted from being considered a controlled act. However, permission for you to give an enema must always be in your employer’s policy.

    The Commercial Enema

    A commercial enema is a small-volume, pre-packaged enema that is ready to administer and is designed to stimulate and distend the rectum and cause defecation. They are often ordered to relieve constipation, specifically when complete cleansing of the bowel is not needed. Children should be given only the amount of enema stated in their care plan.

    The solution is usually administered at room temperature. To give the enema, squeeze the sides of the bottle with enough pressure to push the enema contents into the client’s rectum. As the bottle empties, roll it up from the bottom. Do not release pressure on the bottle, as that would cause the solution to be drawn from the rectum back into the bottle.

    Encourage the client to retain the solution until the urge to defecate is felt. The enema usually takes 5 to 10 minutes to take effect, but some people may immediately feel the urge to defecate; others may become incontinent of stool. It is therefore very important to have all your equipment on hand prior to performing the procedure. Remaining in Sims’ position on the left side may help the client to retain the enema longer.

    The Client with an Ostomy

    Sometimes, surgical removal of part of the intestines is necessary as part of the treatment for cancer, diseases of the bowel, or trauma (such as stab or bullet wounds). Following the surgery, an ostomy—the surgical creation of an artificial opening (stoma)—is sometimes needed (see Chapters 18, 28, and 29). The client wears a pouch over the stoma to collect feces and flatus. Having an ostomy may have a profound impact on the client’s self-image and may result in their reluctance to engage in activities they previously enjoyed, such as swimming, exercise, and even intimate contact. This may affect their mental health and lead to depression in some people (see Chapter 37). There are a number of Canadian, provincial, or territorial websites available for clients with ostomies and for their caregivers, such as https://www.ostomycanada.ca.

    Colostomy

    A colostomy is the surgically created opening (stomy) between the colon (colo) and the abdominal wall. Part of the colon is brought out onto the abdominal wall, and a stoma is created. Feces and flatus pass through the stoma instead of through the anus. Colostomies may be permanent or temporary. The colostomy is permanent if the diseased part of the colon has been removed. A temporary colostomy may be created during surgery to give the diseased or injured bowel time to heal if the colon does not require removal. Eventually, another surgery would reconnect the bowel.

    Stomas usually appear pink in colour, but some can appear red and “angry.” Both are, in fact, healthy stomas. In general, the colour of the stoma should resemble mucous membranes in other parts of the client’s body, such as inside the mouth. Some support workers may needlessly worry that touching or cleaning a red stoma would cause pain. A stoma that appears to be greyish (dusky) or blue-tinged could indicate lack of oxygen to that tissue. This can be the sign of a life-threatening condition and must be reported.

    The location of the colostomy depends on the site of colon disease or injury (FIGURE 30.3). Stool consistency is affected by the nature of the surgery and ranges from liquid to formed stools. The more colon that remains to absorb water, the more solid and formed the stools will be. If the colostomy is near the beginning of the colon, stools will be liquid, whereas the colostomy near the end of the large intestine results in formed stools.

    Feces can irritate the skin, so skin care is essential to preventing skin breakdown around the stoma. Skin is washed and dried whenever the pouch is removed, and a skin barrier is applied around the stoma. The skin barrier prevents feces from coming into contact with the skin. The skin barrier may be part of the pouch or a separate device.

    Ileostomy

    An ileostomy is the surgically created opening (stomy) between the ileum (small intestine [ileo]) and the abdominal wall. Part of the ileum is brought out onto the abdominal wall, and a stoma is created. A portion or entire section of the colon is removed. As a result, water is not absorbed, and liquid feces drain constantly from the ileostomy that has a pungent odour.

    Feces in the small intestine is very acidic and contains digestive juices that are extremely irritating to the skin. The ileostomy pouch must fit well so that feces do not touch the skin, to avoid excoriation and breakdown. Good skin care is essential.

    Ostomy Pouches

    The pouch has an adhesive backing that is applied to the skin (FIGURES 30.4 through 30.6). Clients who have had a colostomy for many years may have the pouch attached to a belt. Many pouches have a drain at the bottom that is closed with clips, clamps, or wire closures. The drain is opened to empty the pouch of feces and also when the bag balloons or bulges with flatus. The drain is wiped with toilet tissue before it is closed.

    3. • Avoiding gas-forming foods

    Testing Stools for Blood

    Blood can appear in stools for many reasons, including ulcers, colon cancer, and hemorrhoids. Often, blood in stools is clearly visible. Bright red blood could be from hemorrhoids or cuts or abrasions in the anal area and can usually be seen if bleeding is low in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. Stools are black and tarry (melena) if there is bleeding in the stomach or upper GI tract. When reporting blood in the stool, you must describe the colour, as it may be an important clue when investigating the source.

    Sometimes, bleeding may occur in very small amounts, which will be difficult to detect just by observing the stools. So, stools are tested for the presence of occult (hidden or unseen) blood, often done to screen (test) for colon cancer.

    Numerous types of tests are available. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions for the test ordered, and follow Standard Practices. The care plan and your assignment sheet will tell you when to collect the specimen. Many factors can affect the test results—for example, eating red meat. Therefore, the client cannot eat red meat for 3 days before the test. Bleeding from hemorrhoids or menstrual periods also affects test results.

    Key Points

    • Each client will have different patterns and frequency of bowel movements, and it is important that you report to your supervisor any changes in your client’s normal elimination routine or in colour, amount, odour, shape, size, or frequency of stools.

    • Always follow Standard Practices when you come into contact with feces, as it may contain pathogens.

    • A number of factors can affect bowel movements—for example, lack of privacy, personal habits, diet, fluids, activity, medications, aging, and disability.

    • Constipation, if not treated, can lead to fecal impaction; if fecal impaction is not treated, it can lead to a bowel obstruction, which is a very serious condition.

    • Fluids lost through loose stool, diarrhea, vomiting, or all of these must be replaced or dehydration—which can be a very serious condition in infants, children, and older persons—can result. Accurate reporting and recording of the frequency and amount of stools and vomitus you observe is very important, as the health care team needs this information to adjust the care plan to replace the fluids lost. This is a very important role, as you, as the support worker, most closely observe how much fluid the client is losing.

    • Bowel training can help your client gain control of bowel movements and develop a regular pattern of elimination, which will prevent fecal impaction, constipation, and fecal incontinence.

    • Administering enemas is a delegated act, which you must be taught by a nurse.

    • Dealing with all aspects of bowel elimination with your clients can be very embarrassing for them, so always remember the DIPPS principles.

    As a support worker, you will care for clients in different stages of their development. Having a basic understanding of human growth and development will help you better understand the client’s needs and give better care. This chapter presents the basic changes that occur in most healthy people from birth through old age.

    Human growth and development are presented here in nine stages; for each stage, the age ranges and typical characteristics are outlined. The stages overlap, so it is hard to see clear-cut endings and beginnings of each. In addition, people vary in their rate of growth and development, especially if their health has affected their everyday activities. For example, you may care for young clients who are residents in a long-term care facility; they may exhibit very different developmental characteristics than other residents who are much older.

    One area of understanding around human growth and development that is being challenged in modern Western society is that of gender differences and identification—specifically, what society dictates versus what a child feels their personal gender affiliation to be. As researchers understand more about society’s influences on gender role assignments, our definitions of these may change and evolve.

    Children grow and develop within the structure of the family. In Canadian society, the family can take many forms (see Chapter 11). Sometimes, when parents are unable to raise their children, another person—for example, a grandparent, aunt, uncle, or court-appointed guardian—takes on the parental role. In this chapter, the term primary caregiver refers to the person who is most responsible for providing for the child’s basic needs, such as a parent or another person assuming the parental role.

    Principles

    Growth refers to the physical changes a person experiences that can be measured and that occur in a steady and orderly manner. Growth is measured by changes in height and weight, as well as by changes in physical appearance and body functions.

    Development refers to changes in psychological and social functioning. A person behaves and thinks in certain ways at different stages of development. A 2-year-old thinks in simple terms and needs a primary caregiver for meeting many basic needs. A 40-year-old thinks in complex ways and meets most of their own basic needs without help from others.

    1. • Growth and development occur from the moment of fertilization until death.
    2. • The process goes from the simple to the complex. A baby learns to sit before standing, to stand before walking, and to walk before running. An adolescent learns to develop from dependence to independence.
    3. • Growth and development occur in certain directions:
      1. • From the head (cephalocaudal) to the foot (proximodistal)—babies learn to hold up their heads before they learn to sit. After learning to sit, they learn to stand.
    5. • From the centre of the body outward—babies learn to control shoulder movements before they can control hand movements.
    6. • Growth and development occur in a sequence and pattern. Certain developmental tasks must be completed during each stage, and no stage can be skipped, as each stage lays the foundation for the next stage. Children all over the world, whatever their culture or living conditions, must go through each stage of development before they can progress to the next task.
    7. • The rate of growth and development is uneven—it does not occur at a set pace. Growth is more rapid during infancy. Also, children have growth spurts: Some children grow rapidly, whereas some others grow slowly. The rate of development is also uneven. Each stage of growth and development has its own characteristics and developmental tasks.
    8. • Not all people fit into the developmental “norm” for each age category. The rate of development is determined by many things, including quality of stimulation, nutritional diet, and environment. Developmental stages are more like guidelines than rules.

    Selected Theories of Human Development

    Scholars have published vast numbers of theories about various aspects of human development. A few of the main theorists and their theories are highlighted here.

    Piaget’s Stages of Cognitive Development1

    Jean Piaget was a Swiss developmental psychologist who studied children in the early 20th century. His theory of intellectual or cognitive development, published in 1936, is still used today in some branches of education and psychology. It focuses on children, from birth through adolescence, and characterizes different stages and aspects of development, including language, morals, memory, and reasoning. The stages cover a range of ages, from birth to 2 years of age to young adulthood. Piaget made basic assumptions about children while developing his theory:

    1. • Children build their own knowledge based on their experiences.
    2. • Children learn things on their own without influence from adults or older children.
    3. • Children are instinctively motivated to learn. They do not need rewards as motivation.

    There are four stages in all:

    1. • Sensorimotor stage (ages birth to 18–24 months old): Characteristics include motor activity without use of symbols. All things learned are based on experiences, or trial and error.
    2. • Preoperational stage (ages 2 through 7 years): Memory and imagination are developing. Children at this age are egocentric, which means they have difficulty thinking outside of their own viewpoints.
    3. • Concrete operational stage (7 to 11 years old): The main goal at this stage is for a child to start working things out mentally in order to solve problems without physically encountering things in the real world.
    4. • Formal operational stage (11 years old and older): In this stage, people continue to develop intellectually, building on knowledge without changing how it is acquired or understood.

    Erikson’s Stages of Psychosocial Development

    Erik Erikson (1902–1994) was a developmental psychologist and psychoanalyst who studied the psychological development of human beings. He is famous for his eight stages of psychosocial development (TABLE 31.1). Each stage involves a crisis of two opposing forces (e.g., trust versus mistrust). For example, if an infant has developed trust, the infant successfully moves on to the next psychosocial task. But if that infant has developed mistrust, this can influence the infant’s ability to form trusting, intimate relationships while moving through the other psychosocial stages of life. Another example is a toddler who is not allowed to learn by doing, and thus develops doubt about their own abilities. This doubt can complicate the toddler’s later attempts at independence.

    According to Erikson, factors that influence psychosocial health include the following:

    1. • Personality. Personality is the blend of thought patterns, feelings, characteristics, and behaviours that make a person unique.
    2. • Family background. Children who grow up in caring, loving families are more likely to have good psychosocial health than those who do not. On the other hand, children growing up in a family with serious problems may be harmed psychosocially through abuse, neglect, distrust, anger, and substance abuse. As they grow older, these children may have issues with trust and intimacy. They may repeat the patterns learned in childhood. Adults who were abused as children may abuse their own children. Likewise, children of substance abusers may develop their own substance abuse issues in adulthood.
    3. • Environment. Experiences outside the family setting also strongly influence psychosocial health. For children and adolescents, such experiences include school, the influence of the media, and interactions with friends and acquaintances. For adults, they include experiences at work and in the community. Access to social support systems such as health care and social welfare can also influence psychosocial health.
    4. • Life circumstances. Some people experience devastating losses or tragedies in their lives—for example, the death of a parent during one’s childhood or the death of one’s child. People who experience such losses may struggle with enjoying strong psychosocial health afterward.

    Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs2

    Abraham Maslow (1908–1970) was an American psychologist who influenced ideas about psychosocial health. Maslow is best known for his theory of needs. A need is something that is necessary or desirable for maintaining life and psychosocial well-being. According to Maslow, certain basic needs must be met for a person to survive and function. These needs are arranged in a hierarchy, or order of importance (FIGURE 31.1). Lower-level needs are normally met before higher-level needs. However, some people will deliberately ignore a particular need for a certain period of time in order to meet another need. For example, a person with an addiction may choose to spend money on street drugs instead of spending it on food.

    Physical Needs

    Physical needs refer to basic needs that are the most essential of our needs for survival. Examples include the need to drink when thirsty, to get sleep when tired, or to eat when hungry. According to Maslow, some of these needs involve our efforts to meet the body’s need for homeostasis—that is, maintaining consistent levels in different bodily systems (such as maintaining a normal and constant body temperature). If someone is lacking in more than one need, they are likely to try to meet these physiological needs first. For example, if someone is extremely hungry, it is hard to focus on anything else besides food.2

    Safety Needs

    Once people’s physiological requirements are met, the next need that arises is a safe environment. Our safety needs are apparent even early in childhood, as children have a need for safe and predictable environments and typically react with fear or anxiety when these needs are not met. Maslow pointed out that in adults living in developed nations, safety needs can be more apparent in emergency situations (e.g., during war and disasters), but this need can also explain why we tend to prefer the familiar or why we do things like purchase insurance and contribute to a savings account.2

    Love and Belonging Needs

    Love is a powerful human emotion that includes deep affection, tenderness, and devotion. Romantic love also involves physical desire. The need for belonging includes the need for a rightful place in society, in a peer group, and in a family. A peer group is a group of friends or acquaintances. Human beings are social creatures who need to be around others. When love and belonging needs are unfulfilled, people often feel lonely and rejected. Many cases have been reported of clients being slow to recover or dying because of lack of love and belonging. This risk is greatest among children and older people.

    Maslow believed that unfamiliar surroundings create a greater need for love and belonging. Clients in long-term care facilities have left their homes, friends, neighbours, pets, belongings, and familiar surroundings. As their support worker, you must be sensitive to the needs of those clients who are struggling with settling into their new environment.

    Self-Esteem Needs

    Esteem is the worth, value, or opinion one has of a person. Self-esteem is thinking well of oneself and being well thought of by others. When self-esteem needs are fulfilled, a person feels confident, adequate, and useful. Unmet self-esteem needs can result in feelings of inferiority, worthlessness, helplessness, and, possibly, depression. Clients often lose their self-esteem when they become ill or injured. Think about the following:

    1. • How do ill parents feel when they cannot support or care for their children?
    2. • Does a woman feel whole and attractive after a breast has been removed?
    3. • Does a person who underwent a leg amputation feel complete, useful, and attractive?

    As a support worker you can help meet clients’ self-esteem needs by being sensitive to their feelings and encouraging them to be as independent as possible.

    Self-Actualization Needs

    Self-actualization means realizing one’s full potential. It involves learning, understanding, and creating to the best of one’s ability. It is the highest need in the hierarchy and is rarely met. Most people constantly try to learn and understand more. However, the need for self-actualization can be postponed, and life will continue.

    Growth and Development

    Infancy (Birth to 1 Year)

    Infancy is the first year of life, when rapid physical, psychological, and social growth and development occur. The developmental tasks of infancy are the following:

    1. • Learning to walk
    2. • Learning to eat solid foods
    3. • Beginning to talk and communicate with others
    4. • Beginning to have emotional relationships with primary caregivers and siblings
    5. • Developing stable sleep and feeding patterns

    The neonatal period of infancy is the first 28 days after birth, and during this time, a baby is called a neonate or a newborn.

    The average newborn is 48 to 53 cm (19 to 21 in.) long and weighs 3 200 to 3 600 g (7 to 8 lb.) at birth. However, genetics, ethnicity, and social and economic factors such as poverty and lack of prenatal care are important determinants of the differences in birth weight, length, and head circumference among babies. Birth weight usually doubles by the age of 5 to 6 months and triples by the first birthday.

    The newborn’s head is large compared with the rest of the body, and the skin is wrinkled. Arms and legs seem short compared with the trunk. The abdomen is large and round. Skin and eye colour vary, depending on the baby’s genetic background, and may change during the first year. The newborn has fat, pudgy cheeks; a flat nose; and a receding chin (FIGURE 31.2).

    The infant’s central nervous system is not well developed. Movements are uncoordinated and lack purpose. Infants can see at birth, although vision is not clear. They seem attracted to patterns, and as they develop, they prefer colours. Infants can hear well—they are startled by loud noises and soothed by soft sounds. They respond more often to higher-pitched voices than to lower-pitched voices. Infants react to touch, and the senses of smell and taste are well developed.

    Newborns have certain reflexes (involuntary movements in response to a stimulus). These reflexes decline and then disappear as the central nervous system develops:

    1. • The Moro reflex (startle reflex) occurs when an infant is frightened by a loud noise or a sudden movement. The arms are thrown apart, the legs extend, and the head is thrown back.
    2. • The rooting reflex is stimulated when the infant’s cheek is touched at or near the mouth. The infant’s head turns toward the touch. The rooting reflex is necessary for feeding; it helps guide the infant’s mouth to the nipple.
    3. • The sucking reflex is produced by touching the cheeks or the sides of the lips.
    4. • The grasping reflex occurs when the infant’s palm is stimulated, causing the fingers to close around the object. This reflex begins to decline around the second month and disappears by the third month.

    Infants sleep most of the time during the first few weeks of life. They wake up when hungry and fall asleep right after eating. The time between feedings lengthens as infants grow and develop. They stay awake more and sleep less as growth and development occur.

    Body movements are initially uncoordinated, without purpose, and generally involuntary. As the central nervous system and muscular system develop, infants develop specific, voluntary, and coordinated movements.

    Developmental observations and milestones for the first year include the following:

    1. • Newborns cannot hold their heads up. During the first 6 months, the infant’s diet is mainly breast milk or formula milk.
    2. • 1 month: Infants can hold their heads up when held and can lift and turn their heads when lying on their stomachs.
    3. • 2 months: At this age, infants can smile and follow the movements of objects with their eyes.
    4. • 3 months: At this age, infants can raise their heads and shoulders when lying on their stomachs. They can sit, supported, for a short period and can hold a rattle.
    5. • 4 months: At this age, infants are usually able to roll over and sit up, if supported. They may also sleep through the night by this time. The Moro and rooting reflexes disappear at this stage. They can hold objects with both hands, put objects into their mouths, and babble when spoken to.
    6. • 5 months: At this age, infants can grasp objects and play with their toes. Teeth start to appear. Solid foods (strained fruits and vegetables) are usually added to their diet at 5 to 7 months. The doctor or a nurse will advise dietary changes.
    7. • 6 months: At this age, infants usually have two lower front teeth and start to chew and bite finger foods. They can hold a bottle for feeding and can sit alone for a short time.

    Toddlerhood (1 to 3 Years)

    Physical growth during the second year of life is not as rapid as during infancy. The developmental tasks during toddlerhood are as follows:

    FIGURE 31.4 A toddler is able to use a spoon to eat food.

    1. • Tolerating separation from the primary caregiver
    2. • Gaining control of bowel and bladder functions
    3. • Using words to communicate with others
    4. • Becoming less dependent on the primary caregiver

    Toddlers have the need to assert independence. With their increasing ability to move about and walk, plus their avid curiosity, toddlers get into everything: They touch, smell, and taste anything within reach. As toddlers become more coordinated, they start to climb onto things, and their new and increasing skills allow them to explore the environment. The child begins to learn that some things can be done without the help of the primary caregiver. By age 3 years, the toddler can run, jump, climb, ride a tricycle, and walk up and down stairs.

    Increased hand coordination gives toddlers new skills. Their need to feel, smell, and taste things is shown in their increasing ability to feed themselves. They progress from eating with fingers to using a spoon (FIGURE 31.4). Toddlers can drink from cups. They can scribble, build towers with blocks, string beads, and turn book pages. Right- or left-handedness is seen during the second year.

    Toilet training is a major developmental task for toddlers. Since bowel control and bladder control are related to central nervous system development, children must be psychologically and physically ready for toilet training. The process starts with bowel control, which is easier because bowel movements are less frequent in a day than urination. Bowel training may be completed at about 2.5 years of age. Bladder control during the day is achieved before bladder control at night. Bladder training may be completed around 3 years of age.

    Speech and language skills increase: Speech is clearer, and vocabulary increases. Words are learned by imitating others. Toddlers understand more words than they use. They are capable of forming two- or three-word sentences. By 3 years of age, children should be able to speak in short sentences.

    Play ability increases. The 2-year-old plays alongside other children but does not usually play with them; this is commonly called parallel play. The toddler is very possessive and does not understand the concept of sharing; the word “mine” is often used.

    Temper tantrums and saying “no” are common during this stage. When disciplined, the toddler may kick and scream. The temper tantrum is the child’s way of objecting to having their independence challenged. It can frustrate the primary caregivers. The toddler years have many such power struggles between caregiver and child and are commonly referred to as the “terrible twos.”

    Another developmental task for toddlers is tolerating separation from the primary caregiver. As toddlers start to explore their environments, they tend to venture away from their primary caregivers. However, when they experience discomfort, frustration, or injury, they quickly return to their primary caregivers or cry for their attention. If the primary caregiver is consistently present whenever needed, the child learns to feel secure and to tolerate brief periods of separation.

    Preschool (3 to 5 Years)

    Ages 3 to 5 years are considered the preschool years (early childhood). At this stage, children grow taller but gain little weight. Preschoolers are thinner, more coordinated, and more graceful than toddlers. The developmental tasks of the preschool years include the following:

    1. • Being better able to communicate and understand others
    2. • Performing self-care activities
    3. • Learning the differences between the genders
    4. • Learning to distinguish right from wrong and good from bad
    5. • Learning to play with others
    6. • Developing family relationships

    The 3-Year-Old

    Three-year-olds are more coordinated and can walk on tiptoe, balance on one foot for a few seconds, and run, jump, and climb with ease. Personal care skills increase—they can put on their shoes, dress themselves, manage buttons, wash their hands, and brush their teeth with help. They can feed themselves, pour from a bottle, and help set the table without breaking the dishes. Hand skills also include drawing circles and crosses.

    Most 3-year-olds know roughly 1 000 words, imitate new words, and talk and ask questions constantly. A favourite question repeated many times a day is “Why?” Sentences are brief—usually three or four words long. Three-year-olds can name body parts, family members, friends, and animals.

    Play is important at this stage, and 3-year-olds are able to play with other children and not just beside them. Because they are developing an understanding that others have feelings, they are more cooperative than toddlers. With an adult’s encouragement, they are able to share toys and take turns. They play simple games and can follow simple rules. They enjoy using colouring books and crayons, doing crafts with child-safe scissors and paper, doing age-appropriate puzzles, and participating in role-playing games such as “house” and “dress-up” (FIGURE 31.5).

    Three-year-olds have vivid imaginations and often play make-believe games. Imaginary friends are common at this age. They may be so involved in their make-believe worlds that they sometimes confuse fantasy and reality. For example, a child may believe that a character in a picture book is real. Their developing imagination may cause some children at this age to have nightmares or daytime fears and insecurities. Some may need a night light by their beds.

    At 3 years of age, children know that there are two genders and begin to identify with their own gender. During role-playing games, some children will often pretend to be women (such as a mom or a princess), and others will pretend to be men (such as a dad or a cowboy). This type of play is their way of exploring the gender differences seen in their families and in the world around them (such as in books, television, and videos).

    The concept of time develops, and 3-year-olds may speak of the past, present, and future. However, the words yesterday and tomorrow are still confusing to them.

    Three-year-olds are less fearful of strangers and can tolerate separation from primary caregivers for short periods. They are less jealous of a new baby than toddlers are. They show that they want to please their primary caregivers.

    The 4-Year-Old

    Four-year-olds can hop, skip, and throw and catch a ball. They can draw faces, copy a square, and try to print letters. They can bathe with some help and usually tend to their own toileting needs with help.

    Vocabulary increases to about 1 500 words, and the 4-year-old can sing simple songs, repeat four numbers, count to three, and name a few colours. The child continues to ask many questions and tends to exaggerate when telling stories. Many 4-year-olds can tell long and complex stories. Their constant chatter shows that they are learning the power of words. Sometimes, they may seem bossy as they use their developing language skills to tease, tattle, or tell others (including caregivers) what to do.

    A 4-year-old’s hand and finger coordination are well developed. They like to make crafts, build with blocks, and try to “write” with a pencil or pen. Children in this age group still enjoy playing “dress-up” and imitating adults (FIGURE 31.6). They still have vivid imaginations but are now better able to differentiate between reality and fantasy. They are more social than they were at 3 years of age and usually enjoy playing in groups of two or three. They also begin to develop close friendships and may try to be like their friends or show off to impress them.

    At this age, many children express curiosity about sexuality. They may ask persistent questions about how babies are made and why boys’ and girls’ genitals are different. Playing “doctor and nurse” is common as curiosity about sexuality continues.

    Four-year-olds may show a strong preference for the parent of the opposite gender to how they identify themselves. They show rivalries with their brothers and sisters, especially when they take their possessions or when the older children have more and different privileges.

    The 5-Year-Old

    Coordination continues to develop, and 5-year-olds can jump rope, skate, tie shoelaces, dress, and bathe. They can use a pencil and copy diamond and triangle shapes. They can print a few letters and numbers and their first names. Drawings of people include the body, head, arms, legs, and feet.

    Communication skills also increase. Vocabulary consists of about 14 000 words, and sentences have six to eight words. Five-year-olds ask fewer questions than they did when younger, but now the questions have more meaning. They want definitions for unknown terms and wish to take part in conversations. They can name four or more colours, coins, days of the week, and months. They can identify the objects that they draw and give detailed descriptions of the drawings.

    Five-year-olds are generally responsible and truthful, and they quarrel less than when younger. There is greater awareness of rules and an eagerness to do things the right way, and they are proud of their accomplishments. They want to please others, especially teachers and caregivers, and display good manners. They are independent and can be trusted, within limits. Five-year-olds have fewer fears but may continue to have nightmares.

    Many 5-year-olds enjoy simple number and word games. Although they may cheat to win, they like having rules and try to follow them. They imitate adults during play and show a greater interest in watching television. They also enjoy activities with the primary caregiver of the same gender (FIGURE 31.7). Such activities include cooking, housecleaning, shopping, yardwork, and sports. Children at this age tolerate their brothers and sisters well. Although they often consider younger children to be a nuisance, 5-year-olds usually are protective of them and enjoy playing with them.

    Middle Childhood (6 to 8 Years)

    Preschoolers often have nursery school and kindergarten experiences. However, middle childhood is the time when school experiences begin in earnest. Children enter the world of peer groups, games, and learning.

    The developmental tasks of middle childhood are the following:

    1. • Developing the social and physical skills needed for playing games
    2. • Learning to get along with other children of the same age and background (peers)
    3. • Learning behaviours and attitudes considered normative to one’s gender

    The 6-Year-Old

    Between the sixth and seventh birthdays, a child will grow about 5 cm (2 in.) and gain 1 to 3 kg (2.5 to 6.5 lb.). Baby teeth are lost, and permanent teeth begin to erupt. Children at this stage are very active—they are skilled at running, jumping, skipping, hopping, and riding a bicycle and seem to be constantly on the go. They are content to sit only for a short time.

    Six-year-olds enter Grade 1 and the world of school, activities, and interactions with other children. Children this age are often described as bossy, opinionated, charming, argumentative, and “know-it-alls.” They can have set ways of doing things and like to have their own way, so when they are frustrated, they may have temper tantrums. Six-year-olds play well with children of both genders. However, most begin to prefer playing with children of the same gender (FIGURE 31.8). They share more with others and may have a “best friend.” They may cheat to win or may leave a game before it is over to avoid losing. Tattling is common at this stage.

    Six-year-olds have a vocabulary of about 16 500 words. They know the alphabet and begin to read and spell. They have an improving ability to communicate thoughts and feelings.

    Play interests range from rough play to quiet activities such as playing with cards, paints, and clay or playing computer games. They start collections of odds and ends rather than specific things such as stamps, rocks, or butterflies. More active play includes tag, hide-and-seek, playing with balls, skating, and playing in mud or sand.

    The 7-Year-Old

    Seven-year-olds grow about 5 cm (2 in.) in height during the year. The average 7-year-old weighs about 22 to 25 kg (49 to 55 lb.) and is 119 to 124 cm (47 to 49 in.) tall. Hand coordination increases, so they are able to do cursive writing and not just print. They are quieter than 6-year-olds and spend more time alone. They are more serious, less stubborn, and more concerned about being well liked by others. Seven-year-olds are more aware of themselves, their bodies, and the reactions of others. They do not like being teased or criticized and are sensitive about how others treat them. They usually like going to school, learning, and reading but may worry about marks and what the teacher thinks of them. Reading skills increase, and they can tell time.

    Play includes swimming, biking, collecting and trading objects, playing ball and other games with rules, working on puzzles, and playing video games, usually in groups. For children who have no personal gender-conflict issues, boys tend to prefer to play with boys, and girls tend to prefer to play with girls.

    The 8-Year-Old

    The 8-year-old enters Grade 3. Increases in height and weight continue, and more permanent teeth appear. Movements are faster and more graceful.

    Peer-group activities and opinions are important at this stage in development. Being accepted and included in peer groups is vital for the fulfillment of the needs for love, belonging, and self-esteem. Children this age get along with adults, but they give more importance to peer-group fads, opinions, and activities. Boys and girls typically continue to play separately, and their interests relate to group games, collections, television, and movies.

    Eight-year-olds are sometimes described as defensive, opinionated, practical, and outgoing. They give advice freely to others but do not accept criticism well. They are able to help with many household tasks such as vacuuming, cooking, and yardwork but also expect more privileges than those given to younger brothers and sisters.

    Learning continues, and these children are curious about science, history, and places and countries other than their own. School provides social opportunities with peers. Eight-year-olds are more daring in the classroom and may pass notes to or talk with others when they think the teacher is not looking. However, they are usually well mannered, relate well to adults, take part in adult conversations, and are friendly and affectionate.

    Late Childhood (9 to 12 Years)

    Late childhood (preadolescence) is the stage between leaving childhood, with its dependency on others, and entering adolescence. Developmental tasks at this stage are similar to those of middle childhood. However, a preadolescent is expected to show more refinement and maturity in achieving the following tasks:

    1. • Becoming independent of adults and learning to depend on oneself
    2. • Developing and keeping friendships with peers
    3. • Understanding the physical, psychological, and social roles of one’s identified gender
    4. • Developing moral and ethical behaviour
    5. • Developing greater muscular strength, coordination, and balance
    6. • Learning how to study

    Boys grow about 2.5 cm (1 in.) per year. Girls grow about 5 cm (2 in.) per year. Boys gain weight of about 1.8 kg (4 lb.) each year. Girls gain weight of about 2.3 kg (5 lb.) each year. Girls are usually taller than boys during late preadolescence. Most permanent teeth have erupted by this age.

    Body movements are more graceful and coordinated (FIGURE 31.9). Muscular strength and physical skills increase. Skill in team sports is often important to the child.

    Body changes begin to occur as the onset of puberty nears. In girls, the pelvis becomes broader, fat appears on the hips and chest, and the budding of breasts occurs. Boys show fewer signs of maturing sexually during this time, but genital organs begin to grow.

    Children of this age must be given factual sex education. Information that is shared among friends about sex is often incomplete and inaccurate. Parents and children may be uncomfortable discussing sex with each other and may avoid the subject. When children do ask questions, with the parent’s permission, you can give honest and complete answers in terms that children can understand.

    Peer groups, which are central to preadolescent activities, begin to affect the child’s attitudes and behaviour. Preference for companions of the same self-identified gender continues. In some instances, boys need to show their strength and toughness and may give each other nicknames. Arguments between boys and girls are common, and they often tease each other.

    At this stage, preadolescents are more aware of the mistakes and weaknesses of adults. They do not accept adult standards and rules without question, and rebellion against adults is common. Disagreements between parents and children tend to increase, although parents continue to be important for the child’s development.

    Adolescence (12 to 18 Years)

    Adolescence is a time of rapid growth and psychological and social maturity. The stage begins with puberty. Puberty is the period during which the reproductive organs begin to function and secondary sex characteristics appear. Girls experience puberty between the ages of 10 and 14. Most boys reach puberty between the ages of 12 and 16. Knowing or questioning sexual orientation usually occurs at this stage of development (see BOX 31.1: Some Facts About Adolescent Sexual Orientation).

    Because the age of puberty varies, adolescence ranges from the ages of 12 to 18 years. The developmental tasks of adolescence include the following:

    1. • Accepting changes in the body and appearance
    2. • Developing relationships with others of the same age
    3. • Exploring their gender identity and accepting the role considered normative for one’s age and identified gender
    4. • Becoming independent from parents and adults
    5. • Developing morals, attitudes, and values needed for functioning in society
    6. • Increased use of social media and electronic communication devices for communicating with peers

    Menarche, the beginning of menstruation, marks the onset of puberty in girls and the appearance of secondary sex characteristics, which include the following:

    1. • Increase in breast size
    2. • Appearance of pubic and axillary (underarm) hair
    3. • Slight deepening of the voice
    4. • Widening and rounding of the hips

    During late childhood, male sex organs begin to increase in size. This growth continues during adolescence. Ejaculation (the release of semen) signals the onset of puberty in boys, and involuntary nocturnal emissions (“wet dreams”) occur: During sleep (nocturnal), the penis becomes erect, and semen is released (emission). The following secondary sex characteristics also appear:

    1. • Appearance of facial hair
    2. • Pubic and axillary (underarm) hair
    3. • Hair on the arms, chest, and legs
    4. • Deepening of the voice
    5. • Increases in neck and shoulder size
